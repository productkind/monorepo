## What works

Nine posts is a small sample, so read these as patterns to test, not rules. All nine used a single image or photos, so this period can't compare formats (no carousels, videos or link cards).

### 1. A personal story in the first line, then the point

The three best posts by impressions all open with something that happened to you, in one or two plain sentences:

- "Last year I was one of 215 women in the first SheBuilds on Lovable." (1,221)
- "When my co-founder Tamas and I work from coffee shops, I order a batch brew and he orders a latte." (1,191)
- "I did a developer bootcamp to get into product management." (736)

The posts that open with the announcement or a question to the reader did less well: "We designed our Portugal Tech Week workshop for product managers…" (671), "How long did your last product strategy hold up…" (667), the partnership post (210).

The clearest comparison is three posts promoting the **same workshop**. The coffee-and-bias story with the workshop after it got 1,191 impressions and 843 people reached. The bootcamp story got 736. The plain announcement ("so far the biggest group signed up is startup founders") got 671. Same ask, and the story roughly doubled the reach.

**Post more like:** open with a small, specific moment from your own week or career, let it lead to one point, and put the event or offer after that.

### 2. A relatable everyday scene with a bit of humour travels beyond your network

The coffee post had the highest out-of-network share (60%) and the most people reached (843), plus the most profile views (8). It mixes an everyday scene anyone recognises (who gets handed the black coffee), a light emoji, a surprising detail ("Tamas and a helper"), and the AI's own admission ("Two reasons, and neither is a good one."). It also reached the right people for the cohort-based course: 15% product managers, the highest PM share of the period alongside the ProductTank promo.

**Post more like:** a real, slightly absurd moment that shows an assumption about who's technical, told with humour, with the PM point at the end.

### 3. A free, time-limited offer with concrete steps converts

The SheBuilds post is the only one that clearly converted: 21 link clicks, 6 followers (two-thirds of the period's total), 10 comments, 54 engagements. It has:

- your own SheBuilds history and an honest reason for rejoining ("it's the participants' fault 😁")
- a free offer with a deadline (second mission free until 16 November)
- what each mission actually does, in plain steps
- one practical tip people can use without the course ("cut your app down to the one thing your user needs to do")
- thanks to named Lovable people, inside an active community (SheBuilds Slack)

Its audience was founders (10%) more than PMs (5%), so it builds the Lovable course audience more than the cohort course audience.

**Post more like:** when there's a free window, say what you'll be able to do after each part, give one tip that works without buying anything, and post where a community is already paying attention.

### 4. Ending with a question about the reader's own experience gets conversation

The Ladies Who Talk post had the highest engagement rate (6.1%) and 9 comments on only 478 impressions. It lists the real questions women asked, makes one clear argument (you need vocabulary like "line-height", not "technologically challenged"), names the women you spoke to, and ends with "If you've started building with AI recently, what was the hardest thing to put into words?"

The 16 November recap also ends with a question, but a broader one ("How do you decide whether to change direction…"), and got 2 comments.

**Post more like:** end with a question people can answer from their own recent experience, and close to the story you've just told.

### 5. Reposts by co-organisers push event posts out of network

The ProductTank promo got 4 reposts (the most of the period, likely your co-organisers) and 61% out-of-network, plus 11 link clicks to Meetup. Its audience was heavily Lisbon (25%) and PMs (15%).

**Post more like:** for community events, ask co-organisers and the host to repost on the day you publish.

### What didn't work

- **Partnership quote reposts** (Tech Women Moldova: 210 impressions, 82% in-network). The commentary was about values in general, with no story or detail from you.
- **Promo graphics as the image (tentative).** The two best posts used a candid photo (café table: 1,191; desk with Little Parrot on screen: 1,221), while the poster, flyer and graphics stayed at 484–736. Event photos didn't stand out either (478, 537), and the text differed too, so this needs testing.
- **Event recaps** (16 November: 537) did fine but not more, and mostly reached Lisbon (25%).

**Post:** [View on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7528184900399603712/) · [Analytics](https://www.linkedin.com/analytics/post-summary/urn:li:activity:7528184900399603712/) **Published:** Monday 16 November, 19:59 **Format / attachment:** Original post with three photos from the ProductTank Lisbon event at SIXT

### Full text

```text
We've changed direction at Little Parrot a couple of times this year, and sometimes I had a nagging question: is this right?

Little Parrot offers hands-on courses that enable people to build technical confidence without an engineering background, and we came to that after learning from our users through experiments.

Then last Thursday at ProductTank Lisbon, I asked our speaker, Andrew Skotzko, who's a product strategy advisor, if he'd ever seen an early-stage startup change strategy too quickly.

His answer was no.

In his book, The Strategy Cycle, he even writes that crafting a strategy feels messier than it looks, with false starts and pivots along the way: "That's not you doing it wrong."

He sees a strategy as a series of bets. You can't be certain any of them will work, and there's no right answer you can know in advance. So you keep evaluating your bets and fine-tuning your strategy as you learn more.

He also contrasted strategy with plans. A plan tells you who's doing what by when, but it doesn't give you any insight into how you'll succeed.

How do you decide whether to change direction or keep going with something you're building?

Thank you to Andrew for the talk, and to my fellow co-organisers Michelle, Luis, Yuliya and Paulo. And a big thank you to the SIXT team, especially Caio and Dr. Ingmar, for hosting us!
```

### Full text

```text
Several women told me last Wednesday that they were "technologically challenged". A little later, they were building software.

I was volunteering at a Ladies Who Talk event in Lisbon, helping women build with Lovable, many of them for the first time.

I expected most of the questions to be about how to build things. Most of them were about how to explain what they wanted:

→ How much should I write in my first prompt?

→ How specific should I be?

→ Should I work this out in ChatGPT first, or ask Lovable directly?

→ How do I explain that I don't like the font, the spacing or the colours?

→ How do I phrase this so it gives me what I have in my head?

So AI has made building software much more accessible, but it hasn't removed the need to learn how to build. What you need to learn has changed, and those questions show what it is.

You don't necessarily need to know how to write the CSS that changes the line-height. But it helps enormously to know that line-height is the thing you want to change.

The same goes for everything else you build: you need to know what you want, say it clearly, check what your AI agent gives you back and ask it to fix what's wrong.

That's also why getting started seemed to be one of the hardest parts. The women I talked to were clearly capable. When you haven't built before, you just don't have the vocabulary or the mental models for directing the tool yet. That's something you can learn, and it's a very different thing from being "technologically challenged".

The conversations went both ways, too. Lis, Daria, Jane, Aniko and Valentina shared with me the amazing things they're building and told me how they think about running a business. We're recalibrating LittleParrot.app at the moment, and those conversations have helped us with it.

Thank you, Irina, for bringing women together to build and swap ideas. I'd love to help again next time.

If you've started building with AI recently, what was the hardest thing to put into words?
```
