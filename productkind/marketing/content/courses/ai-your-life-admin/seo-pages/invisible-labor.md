# Invisible Labor: The Work Nobody Counts

Invisible labour is work that gets done, keeps a household or a workplace running, and doesn't register as work to anyone but the person doing it.

Nobody schedules it. It rarely gets thanked. It only becomes visible when it stops.

## Where the term comes from

The sociologist Arlene Kaplan Daniels named it in [*Invisible Work*](https://academic.oup.com/socpro/article-abstract/34/5/403/1665982), published in *Social Problems* in 1987.

Her argument was that we define "work" as the things somebody pays you for. So unpaid work done at home stops counting as work at all, and because that work has mostly fallen to women, other people treat that effort as a personality trait rather than work. Being organised. Being thoughtful. Being the sort of person who remembers.

Since then the term has widened. It now covers any work that's physically hidden, culturally overlooked, unpaid, or simply never written down.

## What counts as invisible labour

At home:

- Noticing that something needs doing before anyone else does
- Keeping everyone's sizes, allergies, doses and appointment dates in your head
- Being the person who knows where things are kept
- Tidying before guests arrive so the house looks like it always looks
- Sitting up with a child who can't sleep
- Remembering which relatives haven't been called

The same pattern shows up at work, with different jobs:

- Organising the leaving card and the collection
- Taking notes in a meeting nobody assigned you to
- Showing the new person how things really work, because nobody else offered
- Smoothing over a disagreement before it reaches anyone senior
- Being the one who books the room

None of this appears in a job description or a rota, and all of it takes time you'd otherwise spend on your own work.

## Invisible labour, mental load, emotional labour, cognitive labour

Four terms, often used interchangeably, and they aren't the same thing.

**Invisible labour** is the widest of the four. It covers the doing as well as the thinking, at home and at work, paid and unpaid.

**The mental load** is the thinking part specifically: noticing what needs doing, working out the options, deciding, and checking it happened. It's the thinking you keep doing while you're getting on with something else. We cover it in full in [what is the mental load](/guides/what-is-the-mental-load).

**Cognitive labour** is what the research calls the mental load. If you want the academic literature rather than the popular writing, that's the phrase to search. Allison Daminger's [*The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007) is widely cited.

**Emotional labour** comes from the sociologist Arlie Hochschild's [*The Managed Heart*](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-managed-heart/paper), published in 1983. It originally described managing your own feelings as part of a paid job, like a flight attendant staying friendly through a twelve-hour shift. It's now widely used for the work of managing everyone's moods at home, remembering who needs cheering up and heading off arguments before they start, which is a broader use than the original.

Visible work can be divided by asking. **Invisible work has to be seen before it can be shared**, which is why writing it down gets you further than agreeing a rota: the other person can see the whole list before you split it.

## Why it stays invisible

Three things keep it that way, and none of them require anyone to be behaving badly.

**It has no output anyone can point at.** A clean kitchen is visible. Noticing on Tuesday that you'll run out of nappies by Friday is not.

**It's most visible when it fails.** A passport renewed on time goes unmentioned. A passport that expired the week before the holiday gets talked about for days.

**Competence makes it worse.** Whoever knows where things are kept gets asked where things are kept, which keeps them the only one who knows.

## Making it visible

Start by writing it down for a week. It's boring, but it works. Write down what you noticed needed doing, rather than only the jobs you finished. By the end of the week the list is usually longer than you expect, and that list is what you can show someone.

We keep a [mental load list](/guides/mental-load-list) you can copy rather than starting from a blank page, and a piece on [how to explain it to your partner](/guides/explain-mental-load-to-your-partner) once you have something to show them.

Writing it down is itself invisible labour. A notebook only works if you have a free hand and a pen when you notice something, and noticing tends to happen while you're driving, feeding someone, or halfway up the stairs.

So you can say it out loud instead. You talk, however it comes out, and your phone sorts what you said into lists the people you live with can see and tick off. You don't have to type anything, open an app, or keep up a weekly session.

It takes under 10 minutes, and it's the first challenge, so you can build it for free.

Making the work visible won't settle who should be doing it. You'll still have to have that conversation. But you can't share what nobody else can see, so writing it down comes first.
