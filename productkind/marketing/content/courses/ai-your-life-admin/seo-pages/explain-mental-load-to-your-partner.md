# How to Explain the Mental Load to Your Partner

Your partner probably does help, and would help more if you asked. The difficulty is that the work you want to share is work they can't see.

You're trying to describe something that only exists in your head, when you're tired, usually in the middle of a disagreement about something else. So it comes out as a complaint about the bins, and your partner answers you about the bins.

Here's a way to raise it that doesn't depend on winning an argument.

## Start by showing, not describing

Showing your partner the list usually works better than explaining it.

Spend a week writing down every time you notice something that needs doing. Write down the noticing, rather than what you actually did about it. "We're low on nappies." "Nursery forms due Friday." "Mum's birthday soon." "Boiler service is overdue."

Don't filter it and don't tidy it. A week of that is usually between forty and a hundred items, and the number is what makes the size of it visible. One item sounds like nagging. Eighty items on one page is a workload, and your partner can see that at a glance.

If you know you won't keep writing each one down as it happens, and most people don't, you can [say them out loud instead](#make-it-visible-permanently).

Then show it as a description of what you're keeping track of rather than as evidence in an argument: *this is what I'm holding in my head all the time, and I'd like to share some of it with you.*

We keep a full [mental load list](/guides/mental-load-list) you can start from if writing your own from scratch feels like one more job.

## Name the four parts

"Help more" sounds like it means doing more tasks, which leaves out the part that takes up the most room in your head.

The sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed 35 couples for her 2019 paper [*The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007), and found every household job has four parts:

1. **Anticipate.** Noticing it needs doing.
2. **Identify.** Working out the options.
3. **Decide.** Choosing one.
4. **Monitor.** Checking it happened.

Daminger found couples often split the deciding fairly evenly, while anticipating and monitoring stayed mostly with women. Those two run all day, and nobody else can see them happening.

This is worth saying out loud, because it explains why "just tell me what to do" doesn't fix it. If you tell them what to do, you're still doing steps one, two and four. You keep the job and hand over only the doing.

## Ask for whole jobs

Ask for the whole job instead of asking for more help:

> "Can you own this completely? Not do it when I ask. Own it, including remembering it."

Then pick two or three whole jobs and hand them over properly. Dentists. Bins and recycling. Car: tax, MOT, service, tyres. Whatever fits.

Owning a job means you don't remind them, you don't check, and you accept it'll be done their way rather than yours. Accepting their way is the hardest part, and you have to accept it if you want to stop carrying the job yourself. If you're still checking, the job is still yours.

Eve Rodsky's [*Fair Play*](https://www.fairplaylife.com/the-book) is built entirely around this idea, with a card per job, and is worth reading if you want a system to run week to week rather than a single conversation. Emma's comic [*You Should've Asked*](https://english.emmaclit.com/2017/05/20/you-shouldve-asked/) is the five-minute version, and sending it is easier than starting the conversation cold.

## Say what you want, not only what is wrong

"I feel like I do everything" is true, and there's nothing your partner can say back to it except defend themselves.

A specific request gives them something they can actually do:

- "I want to stop being the only person who knows when things are running out."
- "I want to be able to go away for a weekend without writing three pages of instructions."
- "I want two jobs off my list completely, and I don't mind which two."

## Expect it to take more than one conversation

Habits that took years to form don't change in one evening. Usually you have one good conversation, then a fortnight of change, and then things slowly go back to how they were.

## Make it visible permanently

Once the list is somewhere you can both see, you don't have to bring each item up. Nobody is telling anyone to do anything; the item is just on the list.

Two ways to do that:

**A shared note.** Any phone. One note per category, shared with your partner, both of you adding and ticking. Free, works everywhere, and needs both of you to remember to type into it.

**Or have your phone fill it in.** You say what you noticed, out loud, whenever it's on your mind, and AI sorts it onto the right shared list.

That version changes who has to raise things, not only how the list gets written. You notice the barrier cream is nearly out and you say so, to nobody, while getting the pram out of the car. It appears on the household list. Your partner's phone has it. Later they pick some up, and neither of you had to bring it up, so the old argument about who remembers things never started.

You stop being the messenger. You no longer have to both notice a thing and ask for it, and having to ask for it is what usually causes the resentment.
