# What Is the Mental Load?

The mental load is the work of running a household in your head. Noticing that something needs doing, working out when and how, deciding who does it, and then checking it happened.

It's the part nobody sees behind the visible jobs. Someone cooks dinner, and that's the visible part. Knowing what was in the fridge, that one child has gone off pasta, that there's nothing in for tomorrow, and that the shop shuts at six, is the mental load.

People will share the cooking and the washing up much more readily than the remembering.

## A short definition

> The mental load is the ongoing work of anticipating, tracking and organising what a household needs, held in one person's head rather than written down or shared.

It's sometimes called cognitive labour, invisible labour, or the third shift, meaning the household thinking that carries on after your paid job and the housework are done.

If you already recognise it and want to put some of it down, skip to [what actually helps](#what-actually-helps).

## Where the term comes from

The phrase reached most people through a comic. In 2017 the French cartoonist Emma published [*You Should've Asked*](https://english.emmaclit.com/2017/05/20/you-shouldve-asked/) (*Fallait demander*), in which a mother runs a household and a dinner party at once, and her husband answers her frustration with "you should've asked", which is where the title comes from. Her argument was that being asked to help puts the job of managing the household back onto the person already managing it. So asking someone to take a job over is itself part of the work.

The sociologist Allison Daminger then researched what that work is actually made of. She interviewed 35 couples for [*The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007), published in the *American Sociological Review* in 2019, and broke cognitive labour into four parts:

1. **Anticipate.** Noticing a need before it becomes urgent.
2. **Identify.** Working out the options for meeting it.
3. **Decide.** Choosing between them.
4. **Monitor.** Checking it actually got done.

She found that couples often split the deciding fairly evenly, while anticipating and monitoring fell mostly to women. Those two run all day, and nobody else can see them happening.

Eve Rodsky's [*Fair Play*](https://www.fairplaylife.com/the-book) (2019) turned the same idea into a system of cards, one per household job, where whoever holds a card owns all of it: the noticing, the planning and the doing, rather than only doing the job once someone else has asked.

## Why it is heavy

Each individual job is small. Booking a dentist takes four minutes. The load is heavy because:

- **There's no end to it.** You can finish the washing up. You never finish noticing what needs doing.
- **It runs while you're doing something else.** During a meeting, in the shower, at three in the morning.
- **It's invisible, so it goes unacknowledged.** Nobody thanks you for work they can't see, and nobody offers to take it off you.
- **Delegating it costs more than doing it.** Explaining what needs doing, to what standard and by when, often takes longer than doing the job yourself. So you keep it.
- **Forgetting has consequences.** Missing a deadline for nursery forms isn't the same as forgetting to buy biscuits, and nobody else is checking, so if you forget it, it doesn't happen.

## Why it falls unevenly

Some of it is a habit that formed early and never got revisited. One person took on more during maternity leave, or was simply home more at the start, and the split stayed the same after the reason for it went.

Some of it is that the person carrying it notices first, and once you've noticed, waiting for someone else to notice means risking that nobody does, and you're the one who deals with it either way.

Some of it is that knowing things gets you asked more. The person who knows where the passports live is the one everyone asks, so she stays the only one who knows.

None of this means anyone is acting in bad faith. But it won't change on its own, so someone has to change it deliberately.

## What actually helps

**Write it down.** The load is invisible while it's in your head. Once it's on paper or on a screen, both of you can see the same list and talk about the same jobs. We keep a full [mental load list](/guides/mental-load-list) you can copy.

**Hand over whole jobs, not tasks.** "Can you book the dentist" leaves you holding the noticing and the checking. "Dentists are yours" hands over all four parts.

**Make it take no effort to get a thought out of your head.** Daminger's first stage, anticipating, happens wherever you are: feeding a baby, driving, halfway up the stairs. If getting a thought out requires finding a pen and a free hand, it stays in your head, and at three in the morning you carry it until you're up. Saying it out loud and letting your phone file it is one way round that, and you can [build a shortcut that does it](https://littleparrot.app/05900334-0998-4a98-be6d-946653c9b82d/course-overview?ref=seo-what-is-mental-load) in less than 10 minutes.

**Don't expect a system to settle a disagreement.** Making the work visible is useful, and it isn't the same as agreeing how to split it. You still have to sit down together and work out who takes which job.
