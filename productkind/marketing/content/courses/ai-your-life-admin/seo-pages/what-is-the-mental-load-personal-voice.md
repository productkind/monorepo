# What Is the Mental Load? What I Found When I Went Looking

A couple of weeks ago I spent a morning looking at what mums actually type into a search box. We'd just finished a course for them and I wanted to know whether we'd named it in words anyone would recognise.

We hadn't, as it turned out. Almost nobody searches for "AI for busy mums". Same with "ChatGPT for mums". But "mental load" gets searched a lot, and the number of searches has more than quadrupled in five years.

So I went and read about it properly, and I want to write about what I found, because a lot of the explanations out there are either an academic paper or a meme, and not much in between.

I should say upfront: I'm not a mum, so I've never carried the version of this that comes with children. Take what follows as a summary of the research, written by someone who went and read it rather than lived it.

## What it actually is

The mental load is the work of running a household in your head. Noticing that something needs doing, working out when and how, deciding who does it, and then checking it happened.

It happens before and around the visible jobs, and that's what makes it hard to talk about. Someone cooks dinner and everyone can see that. Knowing what was in the fridge, that one child has gone off pasta, that there's nothing in for tomorrow and the shop shuts at six – that's the mental load, and it doesn't look like anything from the outside.

## Where the term comes from

The phrase spread widely in 2017, when the French cartoonist Emma published [*You Should've Asked*](https://english.emmaclit.com/2017/05/20/you-shouldve-asked/), in which a mother runs a household and a dinner party at the same time, and her husband answers her frustration with "You should've asked", which is where the title comes from. Her argument is that a partner who offers to help and waits to be told what to do leaves all the managing with the person already doing it. Which is why "just tell me what to do" doesn't fix it.

The sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed 35 American heterosexual couples for [*The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007), published in 2019, and split the thinking work into four parts: anticipating a need, working out the options, deciding, and then checking it got done.

Daminger found that deciding was often shared fairly evenly, while anticipating and monitoring were not, and that surprised me. Those two steps, noticing what's needed and checking it got done, fell mostly to women. Her couples also shared the cooking far more readily than the remembering.

Which explains an argument I've heard versions of for years without ever being able to name it. A partner who does help, and does take part in the decisions, can be telling the truth when they say the work is shared. They're thinking of the deciding. The other two steps, the noticing and the checking, are the ones that never stop.

Eve Rodsky's [*Fair Play*](https://www.fairplaylife.com/the-book) turned the same idea into a deck of cards, one per household job, where whoever holds a card is responsible for that whole job – the noticing and the planning, not only the doing.

## Why it's heavy, when each job is small

Booking a dentist appointment takes four minutes. What makes it heavy is that the noticing never stops. It runs during a meeting, in the shower, at three in the morning.

And because nobody can see it, nobody thanks you for it and nobody offers to take it, so the effort ends up looking like a personality trait. Being organised. Being on top of things. Being the sort of person who remembers.

One more thing works against you, and it was the most interesting part of everything I read: explaining what needs doing, to what standard, by when, usually takes longer than just doing it. So on any given day, doing it yourself is the rational choice, even though over a year it costs you far more time than handing the whole job over once would. But you make that decision on the day, every time.

## What I'd actually do about it

Three approaches, all worth trying.

**Write it down for a week.** Write down the things you *noticed* needed doing, rather than the jobs you actually finished. It's boring, and at the end you have a list of everything you noticed, which you can show the person you live with. We keep a [mental load list](/guides/mental-load-list) you can copy so you're not starting from a blank page.

**Hand over whole jobs rather than tasks.** "Can you book the dentist" leaves you doing the noticing, the working out and the checking. "Dentists are yours" hands over all four parts. The hard bit is accepting it'll be done differently from how you'd do it, and to be honest I think most advice skips past that part.

**Make it as quick as possible to get a thought out of your head.** This is the one I'd start with, actually. You notice things while you're holding a baby or driving or standing in a queue, and a notebook needs a free hand and a pen right then. You rarely have both, so the thought goes back in your head, and that's where it's still going round at three in the morning.

We ended up building something for that last one. You say out loud whatever you've just noticed, and your phone sorts it onto the right list, where the person you live with can see it and tick it off. No typing, no app to open, nothing to keep updated.

It's the first challenge of our course [Hand Off the Mental Load](https://littleparrot.app/55dfd25b-b9a5-4f04-860d-ef5c1d1e20b9/course-overview?ref=seo-what-is-mental-load), it takes under 10 minutes, and it's free. You build it by tapping a few blocks together in the Shortcuts app that's already on your phone. You'll need an iPhone 15 Pro or newer, because it runs on Apple Intelligence.

And one honest caveat, because I don't want to oversell our own course: a shared list won't settle who ought to be doing what. It makes the size of the mental load visible to both of you, which stops the argument being about whether it exists at all. Who actually takes on which job is still a conversation the two of you have to have.

The research didn't answer one question for me, so I'll ask you. For those of you who've tried to hand some of this over, what actually worked for you, rather than what's supposed to work?
