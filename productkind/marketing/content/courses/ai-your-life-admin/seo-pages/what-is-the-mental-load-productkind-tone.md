# What Is the Mental Load? A Guide to the Work Nobody Sees

If you're reading this, somebody has probably said the words "mental load" to you recently, and you want to know what it actually means.

By the end you'll have a definition you can say out loud to your partner, an understanding of why it feels so heavy when each individual job is small, and three things you can try this week.

## What the mental load is

The mental load is the work of running a household in your head.

It's four kinds of work happening at once:

- Noticing that something needs doing, often before anyone else has spotted it
- Working out how and when it could happen
- Deciding what to do
- Checking afterwards that it actually got done

In practice it works like this. Someone cooks dinner, and everyone can see that part. Knowing what was in the fridge, that one child has gone off pasta, that there's nothing in for tomorrow, and that the shop shuts at six: that's the mental load, and it doesn't look like anything from the outside.

You may also see it called **cognitive labour** (the term researchers use), **invisible labour**, or the **third shift**, meaning the household thinking that carries on after your paid job and the housework are finished. They all describe the same work.

## Why it feels heavy when each job is small

Explaining this to someone who isn't carrying it is the hardest bit.

Booking a dentist takes four minutes. The hard part is that the noticing never finishes. A visible job has an end: the washing up is done, and you can stop thinking about it. Noticing what needs doing has no end, so it carries on running while you're in a meeting, in the shower, or lying awake at three in the morning.

Three other reasons it feels heavy:

**Nobody can see it, so nobody offers to take it.** Nobody thanks you for work that leaves no trace, and nobody offers to share it, because sharing starts with somebody else knowing it exists.

**Explaining it takes longer than doing it.** Telling someone what needs doing, to what standard, by when, is often more work than the job. So on any given day, doing it yourself is the sensible choice. Across a year it isn't, because nothing ever gets handed over permanently, and you make that decision on the day, every time.

**Forgetting has consequences.** Missing the nursery form deadline isn't the same as forgetting the biscuits, and if nobody else is tracking it, nobody will catch it when you forget.

If you've been feeling tired in a way that's hard to point at, this is often why. That's a reasonable response to carrying all of it, not a personal failing.

## Where the idea comes from

You don't need the history to fix anything, but it helps to know that researchers have studied this, and it gives you a name and a study you can cite when you talk to your partner.

**Emma's comic, 2017.** The French cartoonist Emma published [*You Should've Asked*](https://english.emmaclit.com/2017/05/20/you-shouldve-asked/), where a mother runs a household and a dinner party at the same time and her husband answers her frustration with "You should've asked", which gave the comic its title. Her point is that asking someone to help hands the managing straight back to the person already managing it.

**Allison Daminger's research, 2019.** A sociologist interviewed 35 American heterosexual couples for [*The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007) and split the thinking work into the four parts listed above. She found that couples often shared the deciding fairly evenly, while the noticing and the checking fell mostly to women.

That last point explains a conversation many couples have without getting anywhere. A partner who does help, and does take part in decisions, can honestly believe the work is shared. They're thinking of the deciding. The noticing and the checking are the two parts that never switch off.

**Eve Rodsky's *Fair Play*, 2019.** [A system of cards](https://www.fairplaylife.com/the-book), one per household job, where whoever holds a card is responsible for that whole job: the noticing and the planning, right through to the doing.

## Three things to try this week

Take these in order. The first one gives you the list the other two depend on, and the third one is the easiest to skip.

### 1. Write it down for a week

Every time you notice something that needs doing, write it down. The noticing, rather than the jobs you finished. "We're low on nappies." "Nursery forms due Friday." "Boiler service overdue."

Don't tidy the list and don't filter it. At the end of the week you'll have a written list of everything you noticed, which you can show to the person you live with. That's the whole point: **you can't share what nobody else can see.**

We keep a [mental load list](/guides/mental-load-list) you can copy so you're not starting from a blank page.

### 2. Hand over whole jobs, not tasks

"Can you book the dentist" leaves you doing the noticing, the working out and the checking. "Dentists are yours" hands over all four parts.

This one is hard, so don't be surprised if it takes a few goes. Owning a job means you don't remind, you don't check, and you accept it'll be done differently from how you'd do it. Accepting a different standard is uncomfortable at first, and it's the price of not carrying the job any more. That's normal.

### 3. Make it quick to get a thought out of your head

If you can't get a thought out of your head the second you have it, the list stops getting updated and the jobs drift back to you within a fortnight.

You notice things while you're holding a baby, driving, or standing in a queue. Writing it in a notebook needs a free hand and a pen at exactly that moment, and you rarely have both. So the thought goes back into your head, and that's where it stays until it wakes you at three in the morning.

Instead, say what you've just noticed out loud and let your phone put it on the list. You talk, however it comes out, and the shortcut sorts what you said onto the right list, where the person you live with can see it and tick it off. No typing, no app to open, and no weekly session to keep up with.

It takes under 10 minutes. You build it by tapping a few blocks together in Shortcuts, Apple's app for building small automations, which is already on your phone, and each step tells you exactly what to tap.

## One thing a shared list won't do

A shared list won't settle who ought to be doing what. It shows both of you how much there is to keep track of, so you stop arguing about whether the work exists. Agreeing how to split it is still a conversation the two of you have to have.

A shared list is a tool. It doesn't decide who does what, and expecting it to sets you up for disappointment.

## Where to go next

You've now got a name for this work, the four parts it breaks into, and three things to try this week.

Start with the list. One week, everything you notice, no tidying. Then have a look at how long it is, and decide which two jobs you'd most like to hand over completely.

If you could take two things off your list permanently, which two would actually change your week?
