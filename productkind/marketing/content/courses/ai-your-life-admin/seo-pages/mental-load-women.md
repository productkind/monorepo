# The Mental Load on Women

Ask two people in the same house who keeps track of what needs doing, and you might get two answers that don't match. Both of them honest.

So why does the noticing mostly fall to women? Here's what the research found, and what changes it.

## What the research found

The sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed 35 American heterosexual couples for [*The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007), published in the *American Sociological Review* in 2019. She split the thinking work into four parts:

1. **Anticipate.** Noticing a need before it becomes urgent.
2. **Identify.** Working out the options.
3. **Decide.** Choosing between them.
4. **Monitor.** Checking it actually got done.

Daminger found that deciding was often shared fairly evenly, but anticipating and monitoring were not. Those two fell mostly to women.

That might be why the same argument keeps coming back without either person being wrong. A partner who does help around the house, and who does take part in the decisions, can be telling the truth when they say the work is shared. They're thinking of step three, deciding. Anticipating and monitoring are the two that never switch off, and those are the ones you're still doing.

### Does the same thing happen in same-gender couples?

Not in the same way, and the difference is worth knowing, because it tells you the pattern isn't inevitable.

Daminger's couples were all heterosexual. A 2023 study in *PLoS One* by Caitlan McLean and colleagues, [*The management of cognitive labour in same-gender couples*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10343096/), looked at same-gender couples instead. It's a much smaller study, so read it as a pointer rather than a settled finding.

They didn't split cognitive labour evenly either. What changed was the basis for the split: these couples divided it by task preference, by what each person was better at, and by how the relationship worked, rather than along gender lines.

So the work doesn't disappear when nobody is defaulting to a gender role. It gets allocated on other grounds, and it gets talked about.

## Why it falls this way

Four reasons, none of which need anyone to be acting in bad faith.

**It started early and never changed back.** Maternity leave puts one person at home with the nursery, the health visitor and the delivery slot for months. The split that made sense during maternity leave usually stays in place long after both of you are back at work.

**Noticing first means owning it.** Once you've seen that the nappies are running out, you can wait and see whether anyone else notices. If nobody does, you're the one buying nappies at nine in the evening anyway. So you hold on to it, and nobody else ever knows you were holding it.

**The more you know, the more you get asked.** Whoever knows where the passports are kept is the one everyone asks, which keeps you the only one who knows.

**Asking costs more than doing.** Explaining what needs doing, to what standard and by when, often takes longer than the job. So on the day, doing it yourself is faster. Over a year, explaining it once is what saves you the time.

Sociologists have a broader name for this. Unpaid work at home stops counting as work at all, because nobody pays for it. Arlene Kaplan Daniels made that argument in [*Invisible Work*](https://academic.oup.com/socpro/article-abstract/34/5/403/1665982) back in 1987, and we cover it in [invisible labour](/guides/invisible-labor).

## What it costs

The individual jobs are small. Booking a dentist takes four minutes.

The noticing is what costs you, because it never stops. It runs during a meeting, in the shower, at three in the morning. It uses the attention you were going to spend on your work, your sleep, or a conversation with someone you like.

And because it's invisible, there's no credit attached to it, so it looks like a personality trait rather than work. Being organised. Being on top of things. Being the sort of person who remembers.

## What actually shifts it

**Hand over whole jobs, not tasks.** "Can you book the dentist" leaves you doing the noticing, the working out and the checking. "Dentists are yours" hands over all four. That means not reminding, not checking, and accepting it'll be done differently from how you'd do it. Letting it be done differently is the hard part, and it's what you have to accept if you want to stop carrying it.

**Write it down before you divide it.** You can't share what nobody else can see. Write down everything you notice for a week, then look at how long the list is. We keep a [mental load list](/guides/mental-load-list) you can copy, and a piece on [how to raise it without it becoming a row](/guides/explain-mental-load-to-your-partner).

**Make it easy to record a job the second you notice it.** You notice things while you're holding a baby, driving, or standing in a queue, and writing it in a notebook needs a free hand and a pen right then. You rarely have both, so it goes back in your head, and that's where it stays until it wakes you at three in the morning.

## One honest limit

None of this settles who ought to be doing what. A list shows both of you how much there is and stops the argument being about whether it exists. The two of you still have to agree how to split it, and that conversation is still ahead of you.

The difference is that you're no longer the only person who can see the list.
