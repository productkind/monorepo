# Ten content ideas that feed the two waitlists

Five per audience. Each one teaches something a person can use the same day, collects an email, and sends the reader to the learning path that continues the work.

## How this sits next to what already exists

Two campaigns already point at these pages:

- [2026-09-vibe-coder-validation](../2026-09-vibe-coder-validation/) has eight outcome-led videos for `https://littleparrot.app/guides/building-apps-with-ai`.
- [2026-09-pm-technical-fluency-validation](../2026-09-pm-technical-fluency-validation/) has eight for `https://littleparrot.app/guides/technical-product-manager`.

Those videos name a situation and show the outcome. They don't teach a method, because Kinga's direction was that teaching sequences are where a viewer leaves. So there's nothing yet that gives a person something usable before the ask.

That's what these ten ideas are. Same two destinations, same research, one layer below: the useful thing that earns the email.

Two resources are already drafted and can carry ideas 3 and 9 immediately, in [2026-09-practical-resources](../2026-09-practical-resources/): _Investigate a Bug in Your App_ and _Check the Impact of a Feature_.

## Rules this plan follows

Taken from our own data and research, not from general practice.

- **LinkedIn carries the conversion.** It converts impressions to site visits at roughly 0.9%, more than ten times TikTok's rate, and delivers more visitors than every other social channel combined. Documents and carousels are its best format (30 to 67% engagement). ([channel strategy](../../../channel-strategy-2026-08.md))
- **TikTok is a testing lab.** 16.7K views produced 8 followers and about 11 site visits. Use it to find out which opening line works and what people are searching for, then move the winner to LinkedIn. Don't judge it on traffic.
- **YouTube changes role.** The strategy drops Shorts because the channel served women-focused content to a 100% male audience. That mismatch is about the subject, not the platform, so YouTube stays here for the Product Manager path only, as longer search-intent video. The search demand is real: `what is an api` is 673,000 US a month, `ci/cd` 14,800, `what is technical debt` 2,900, `what is vibe coding` 49,500. ([search-demand analysis](../../../../ai-research/pm-technical-fluency/pm-technical-fluency-search-demand-analysis-2026-08.md))
- **Instagram is Reels only.** Carousels reached 18 to 35 accounts against 130 to 165 for Reels.
- **Community and partner emails are the highest return per hour of work.** One Geek Girls email drove 106 unique visitors, three times Instagram's entire year.
- **No deficit framing.** "Women are behind" alienates the most capable part of this audience, and there's an articulate refusal of it on the record. What works is deflating the competition rather than the reader: "we are all early", "most people are asking ChatGPT to write captions and calling it a day". ([women research §11.5, §15.5](../../../../ai-research/women-ai-building-research-2026-08.md))
- **Don't build content on the credits problem.** It's felt intensely and it doesn't travel: videos about running out of credits scored 17, 23, 57, 66 and 119 likes. Answer it on the page and in the emails instead. (§17.2)
- **Gender framing is the strongest engagement lever we've measured.** The one post where a large number of women described their own projects was framed that way explicitly. (§11.6)
- **Say app, tool, website, build.** Not software, not digital product, not MVP or SaaS. Both audiences here have already built something, so "built" and "launched" are safe; they aren't for the earlier-stage woman. (§15.3, §17.1)

# Audience 1: non-technical women who've already built with AI

Destination: `https://littleparrot.app/guides/building-apps-with-ai`

She has a working app. The research is unusually clear that her problem is no longer making things, it's judging them. Across two independent parts of the study this comes out as the single need nothing in the market serves: _"Everyone teaches generation. Nobody teaches checking."_ (§15.4)

## 1. Ten things you can check in the app you built, without reading any code

**The pain.** "The problem is I can't judge what it's doing." "I don't know if my app is secure, if there is junk code, if there are any bugs that I will need to fix." "I won't use vibe-coded apps for client work because I don't know what I don't know." Three separate women, three separate communities. (§3.2)

**The content.** Ten checks she can run on her own app in an afternoon, each one a thing to do and a result to read, none of them requiring code: sign up twice with the same email address, submit the form with every field empty, open it on a phone, request a password reset and see whether the email arrives, sign out and try to reach a page that should need an account, look for a key in the page source, delete a test account and see whether the data goes with it.

Be clear about the scope: these are checks she can run herself, and they don't add up to a security audit. That line protects us and it's the honest one.

**Email capture.** _Check your app before your first customer_, a PDF worksheet with the ten checks, space to record what happened and a short section on what to do with each failure. New resource, closest sibling to the bug pack already drafted.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document (lead). Instagram Reel and TikTok cut showing three of the checks running against a real app, with the failure visible on screen. One email to registered users who haven't paid. Discord SheBuilds and the women-in-tech Slacks, where this audience already sits.

**Route.** Last page of the worksheet: the learning path covers the rest of it, steps 4 and 5, testing the journeys customers depend on. Link to the waitlist.

## 2. What you actually own when you build on Lovable, Replit or Base44

**The pain.** "I was extremely alarmed when I was apparently submitting changes to the main branch." "Free to sign up isn't free." "What if someone has a site they need to update on a regular basis? What will the client do if the website is a pure code?" And the maintenance question the research calls the most sophisticated beginner question in the whole study: what happens when you need to change it later and the tokens have run out. (§3.1, §17.2, §17.6)

**The content.** What leaves with you and what stays behind. How to get the project into GitHub so there's an online home for it outside the building platform, how to save a version you know works, and how to make one change you can undo. Git shows you what changed on your computer; GitHub is where the project lives online. Both are worth naming correctly, because she'll hit both words and nobody separates them for her.

This is the ownership question rather than the credits question, which is why it can carry a post when credits content can't: it's a decision she can act on, not a complaint.

**Email capture.** _Put your app in GitHub_, a walkthrough with screenshots: connect the project, save a working version, make a change, undo it. Roughly the shape of step 2 on the learning path, stopping before branches.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document. Email. Instagram Reel of the undo working, which is the moment that sells it. Communities.

**Route.** Waitlist link at the end of the walkthrough, framed as continuing: the path picks up at what to do when the change you want to undo is three changes back.

## 3. Stop prompting. Go back to the version that worked.

**The pain.** The grind that doesn't end. "There I was in April, 3 AM, trying to convince an AI to fix the same bug for the dozenth time." "I'd spent over a month and about 27 attempts to rebuild it from scratch." "I'm too scared to tell it to remove them in case it wrecks something." (§3.4, §3.2)

**The content.** A rule for when to stop asking the AI agent to try again, and what to collect before you ask it anything else. Three things: the exact steps that trigger the problem, what you expected against what happened, and the error wording copied out rather than described. Then the decision: if two attempts with that evidence haven't worked, restore the working version and rebuild the feature instead of repairing the repair.

Kinga has her own stuck moment for the opening. Told as a flop we learned from, not as advice from above.

**Email capture.** _Investigate a Bug in Your App_, already drafted in [2026-09-practical-resources](../2026-09-practical-resources/toolkit-investigate-a-bug-in-your-app.md). Nothing new to make.

**Channels.** LinkedIn plain-text story post, then the resource in the first comment. TikTok and Instagram Reel of the loop and the moment of going back. Email.

**Route.** Step 3 of the learning path, fix bugs without rebuilding everything.

## 4. The receipts: women with apps that work, and what broke on the way

**The pain.** The corpus shows women who can't tell whether their experience is normal, and an accusation vocabulary waiting for them if they say so out loud. "Would anyone mind just snooping around and making sure I'm not an idiot." Isolation is named as a primary pain by five independent sources. (§6.1, §8)

**The content.** One woman per post, with her consent. What she built, who it's for, what broke, what she did about it, and what she'd do differently. The un-glamorous middle is the whole point, and refusing to oversell works as a difference rather than a weakness: _"Not in a weekend. Learning by doing takes time."_

Gender framing is explicit, because it's the highest-engagement lever in the research. The model to study is the post that opened: "The boys keep getting the headlines for vibe coding. Nobody's talking about the girlies making real money off it. So I went and found the receipts."

**Email capture.** This series drives to the waitlist form directly rather than to a separate resource. The waitlist is an email capture, and someone who reads a builder's story is ready to say "email me when this opens" in a way she isn't after a checklist.

**Channels.** LinkedIn (lead, and the format Kinga's audience already rewards). Instagram Reels. Communities, where we ask for the next subject and credit her back.

**Route.** Straight to the waitlist page, named as the thing that covers the problem in her story.

## 5. You launched and three people came. Is it the app or the audience?

**The pain.** "I did it guys. I built my dream and published my dream. Now is the hard part getting people to see my dream." "I didn't have the energy to figure out Product Hunt." "One person tried the free tool and got an answer about their business that wasn't right." (§5.4, §6.1, §3.2)

**The content.** Kinga's product-management expertise, shared as the thing she does at work. Pick the one action that means a person got the result the app promises, count it, and read the number against visits. If people arrive and don't reach that action, the app is the problem. If nobody arrives, the app isn't the problem yet. That distinction can save weeks of rebuilding the wrong thing.

**Email capture.** _The one question your analytics should answer_, a one-page worksheet: name the promise, name the action that proves it, name what you'll do at each of the three possible readings.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document. Email. Instagram Reel. Worth a TikTok test because it fits the post-build cliff the research says is dense there.

**Route.** Steps 7 and 8 of the learning path, analytics and focused experiments.

# Audience 2: software product managers

Destination: `https://littleparrot.app/guides/technical-product-manager`

The five ideas map to the five opportunity territories in the [synthesis](../../../../ai-research/pm-technical-fluency/pm-technical-fluency-opportunity-synthesis-2026-08.md). The thing this audience says it can't find is the thing we can make cheaply: _"most of what I find from other people are usually developers, so a lot is not really applicable."_

## 6. Draw your own product on one page, using five questions

**The pain.** "They spoke the language of engineers in a way I couldn't." "Nodding along in standups, pretending to get it." "Are there things I should study up on that could help me bridge the gap from the person who knows the product to the person who builds the product?" (Territory 1, validation evidence)

**The content.** The research makes a point worth building the whole piece on: the search demand is for `what is an api` and `frontend backend database`, but a generic explainer can't teach a Product Manager her own company's architecture. So the content is five questions to ask in the next engineering conversation, which produce her own diagram rather than somebody else's stack: where does this screen get its data, what happens between the button and the database, which of these do we run and which do we pay someone else for, what else reads this data, and what breaks if that external service is down.

**Email capture.** _Map your product in five questions_, a worksheet with the five questions, room to draw, and a worked fictional example.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document (lead). YouTube, a 6 to 10 minute video against the real search terms. Email. Women In Product and Mind the Product communities.

**Route.** Step 1 of the learning path.

## 7. Engineering says it's done. Users still don't have it. Where is it?

**The pain.** Seven Product Managers in the strict validation sample describe losing the thread around stand-ups, refinement, branches, builds, pipelines and release work. It's the territory with the strongest cross-community evidence. Search backs the vocabulary: `staging vs production`, `feature flags explained`, `how does ci cd pipeline work`. (Territory 2)

**The content.** The states a change passes through between merged and visible, named in order, and four questions that locate any change: is it merged, is it in staging or production, is it behind a flag, and who can turn the flag on. Plus where Product should have a say, which is the part that makes this her job rather than trivia.

**Email capture.** _Where is this change?_, a one-page card with the states, the four questions and what each answer means for what she can tell a customer.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document. YouTube against the search terms. TikTok test, because a 40-second version of "it's done but you can't have it" is a recognisable situation. Email.

**Route.** Step on the learning path covering review, tests, environments and rollback.

## 8. Send engineering a bug they can act on in five minutes

**The pain.** "Forwarding a vague customer problem." Product Managers want to reproduce behaviour, gather evidence and write a useful escalation without touching production code. Search visibility for this is low, which means low competition on a real need. (Territory 3)

**The content.** Five minutes, five things: reproduce it yourself and write the steps, record the account and what it could see, capture the exact error wording rather than a description of it, note the time and the browser, and say what you already ruled out. Then the escalation itself, which says what's broken, for whom, how often and what you checked.

The honest framing: what this buys her is engineering trusting that she looked first.

**Email capture.** A Product Manager version of [_Investigate a Bug in Your App_](../2026-09-practical-resources/toolkit-investigate-a-bug-in-your-app.md). The existing one is written for someone who owns the app; the edit for a Product Manager escalating inside a company is small.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document. Email. Women In Product and the Product Manager Slacks, where this is a daily problem. Low priority for video.

**Route.** Step on the learning path covering investigation and escalation.

## 9. "Can we just add a field?"

**The pain.** Very strong evidence, and no search term that represents it, which the research names as the reason nobody serves it. Demand shows up next door instead: `what is technical debt` at 2,900 US and 720 UK a month. The job is spotting hidden complexity, asking what breaks, and turning a technical answer into a trade-off she can explain to a stakeholder. (Territory 4)

**The content.** Five questions that expose the size of a "small" request before she commits to it: what else reads or writes this data, what happens to the records that already exist, can we undo it once it's live, who else has to change something, and what does saying yes to this delay. Then how to write the recommendation so it reads as a product decision with a reason, not a relayed engineering objection.

**Email capture.** _Check the Impact of a Feature_, already drafted in [2026-09-practical-resources](../2026-09-practical-resources/toolkit-check-the-impact-of-a-feature.md). Nothing new to make.

**Channels.** LinkedIn document (strongest of the five for saves). YouTube, aimed at the technical-debt search demand. Email. Communities.

**Route.** Step on the learning path covering technical debt, feasibility and build against buy.

## 10. You prototyped it with AI. Now someone wants to give it to customers.

**The pain.** This is where the technical-fluency need and the AI pressure meet. "Vibe coding as a Product Manager being a job requirement, everyone, including Product Managers and Ops, is expected to vibe code." "No plans have been made as far as who will maintain this code." "The double standard in code quality is a slap in the face." And underneath it, the fear of being accountable for output she can't fully vouch for. (Territory 5, pain points 8 and 10)

**The content.** The handover note. What the prototype proves, what it doesn't prove, what data went into it, and the four things production would need that this doesn't have. Being able to write that note is what lets a Product Manager build freely without the prototype turning into a commitment she didn't make.

This is also the piece that answers the wider pressure without being a post about AI overwhelm, which is the biggest pain in the research but routes to the newsletter rather than to this page.

**Email capture.** _Prototype handover note_, a one-page template: what it proves, what it doesn't, what's fake in it, and what production would require.

**Channels.** LinkedIn (lead; the AI-mandate framing is what this audience is living through). YouTube, against `what is vibe coding` search demand, 49,500 US a month. TikTok test. Email.

**Route.** Step 5 of the learning path, build to learn and know where production starts.

## Channel plan across all ten

| Channel | What it does here | Cadence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LinkedIn (Kinga) | Carries the conversion. Every idea gets a document or a story post. | 3 a week, rotating: how-to document, story, reassurance |
| Email (registered, unpaid) | One practical idea per send, one link. Learner-centred, not company news. | Fortnightly, between the monthly updates |
| Communities | Resource shared value-first, no build-up. Discord SheBuilds, women-in-tech Slacks, PWIT, Women In Product, Mind the Product. | One per idea, where it fits |
| Partner email | The Geek Girls play, repeated. Highest return per hour we have measured. | One a month |
| Instagram | Reels only, women's path only. | Repurpose, no original production |
| TikTok | Hook testing and reading search demand. | Repurpose, judge on what it teaches |
| YouTube | Search-intent video, Product Manager path only. | One per Product Manager idea, when there's capacity |

## Two things to decide

**Email segmentation.** The plan sends women's-path content to the whole registered-unpaid list and Product Manager content only to a segment, but I don't know from the repo whether the list can be split that way. If it can't, the fallback is to send Product Manager ideas through the communities and LinkedIn only, and keep the list on the building-apps content.

**Order.** If only two of these get made this month, make 1 and 9: idea 1 hits the need the research says nothing in the market serves, and idea 9 needs no new resource because the worksheet is already drafted.
