### Post 4. “Always not too sure and asking people to clarify”

**Primary search phrase:** `how to ask for clarification in technical meetings`

**Treatment:** Presenter shows a clarification ladder: define it, show it here, connect it to the decision.

**Script:** Are you “always not too sure and asking people to clarify” in technical meetings? Keep asking. Change the question so the answer connects to your product. Use this three-step ladder: What does that term mean? Where does it appear in our product? What does it change about this decision? If someone says the new feature creates a dependency, ask which team or system you now rely on, what must happen first, and what that does to scope or timing. A definition helps you follow the sentence. Product context helps you use the answer. ### Post 6. “Speak their language”

**Primary search phrase:** `how to speak the language of engineers`

**Treatment:** Presenter turns one technical explanation into three lines: term, example in our product, effect on the user or plan.

**Script:** Product Managers in our research said they wanted to “speak their language”. Translate one technical explanation into three lines: the term, an example from your product and the effect on the user or plan. If the term is caching, define it as storing a temporary copy so the product can respond faster. Then name where your product uses it and what users might see if that copy is out of date. Play the explanation back to an engineer and ask them to correct it. Credibility grows when your summary is accurate and useful for a decision. ### Post 7. “Ask sharper questions”

**Primary search phrase:** `how to ask engineers better questions`

**Treatment:** Presenter replaces “Is this possible?” with four questions written on cards.

**Script:** Non-technical Product Managers told us they want to “ask sharper questions”. Start by replacing “Is this possible?” with four questions. What has to change? What else depends on it? What could fail? How would we know it worked? If the decision is hard to reverse, add: how would we undo it? These questions help engineering explain scope, dependencies, failure and evidence. They also give you information you can use to compare product options. Asking better questions makes the decision conditions visible while engineering works out the implementation.
