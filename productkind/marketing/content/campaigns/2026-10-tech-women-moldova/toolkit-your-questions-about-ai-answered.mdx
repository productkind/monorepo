## Your Questions About AI, Answered

Answers to the questions people ask most about AI chat assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini: where their answers come from, when you can trust them, what to keep private, which one to use, and what AI changes for your career. Find your question below, tap it, and read the answer. Most answers end with a prompt or a small step to try in your next chat.

The questions come from what people searched for on Google and YouTube, and asked in AI communities, in October 2026. You don't need a paid plan for anything here.

### Words used in the answers

| Word | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **AI chat assistant** | An app you talk to in a chat window, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Microsoft Copilot. You ask, it replies, and you copy out what you need. |
| **LLM (large language model)** | The technology inside an AI chat assistant: a program trained on an enormous amount of text to predict what text comes next. |
| **Prompt** | Everything you send to the AI: your question or instruction, plus anything you paste in or attach. |
| **Context** | Everything the AI can see while it writes its reply: your prompt, the earlier messages in the chat, any files, and hidden instructions from the company that built the app. |
| **Hallucination** | An answer that's wrong or made up, written as confidently as a correct one. |
| **AI agent** | An AI that works through a task step by step and takes actions along the way, such as opening websites, editing files or running code. Claude Code and Codex are examples. |

### How AI chat assistants work

<Accordion type="single" collapsible>

<AccordionItem value="chatgpt-same-as-ai">
<AccordionTrigger>Is ChatGPT the same thing as AI?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

No. AI (artificial intelligence) is the general name for computers doing things that normally need human intelligence: recognising faces in photos, filtering spam, recommending the next song, translating text. ChatGPT is one product built on one kind of AI, a large language model (LLM). Claude, Gemini and Copilot are built on the same kind of technology.

Because ChatGPT is a language model, it's very good with language: explaining, summarising, rewording, drafting and answering questions in a conversation. It's much less reliable with facts nobody gave it, exact calculations and recent events, unless the app adds a tool for them, such as web search.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="where-answers-come-from">
<AccordionTrigger>Where does it get its answers from? Is it like Google?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

It writes them, one small piece of text at a time. During training, the model read a huge collection of text: websites, books, articles, forums and code. From that, it learned patterns of how language fits together. When you send a prompt, it predicts a likely next word, adds it, predicts the next one, and carries on until the reply is finished.

So it doesn't look your answer up in a database, the way a search engine finds pages. That's why it can explain a concept well and still get a date wrong: both come out of the same process, predicting what sounds right. Its own knowledge also stops at the date its training data was collected.

Many AI chat assistants can now search the web as well. When they do, you'll usually see the sources listed or linked in the reply. That's a separate step: the app fetches web pages and the model summarises them, and the summary can still contain mistakes.

**Try this:** when a fact is important and the reply shows no sources, ask:

```
Search the web for this and link the sources you used.
```

Then open at least one of the links and check that it says what the summary claims.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="does-it-remember">
<AccordionTrigger>Does it remember me? Does it learn from our chats?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Three different things get called "memory", and it helps to know the difference between them.

- **What the model learned in training.** This is fixed. Chatting with it doesn't change the model.
- **The current chat.** It can see the earlier messages in this conversation, so you can refer back to them. In very long chats, the app may shorten or drop older messages, which is why details you gave at the start can get lost.
- **Memory features.** Some apps can save facts about you, such as your job or your preferences, and bring them into new chats. You can usually see, edit or switch these off in the app's settings.

Whether your chats are used to train future versions of the model is a separate question, answered under "Are my chats used to train it?" further down.

**Try this:** when a long chat starts forgetting details you gave it or repeating the same suggestions, ask:

```
Summarise what we've worked out so far as a short list, including any decisions and details I gave you.
```

Then start a new chat and paste the summary in.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="ai-agent">
<AccordionTrigger>What's an AI agent? Is ChatGPT one?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

An AI agent works through a task in steps and takes actions along the way. You give it a goal, such as "find three flights to Lisbon under 200 euros and put them in a table" or "fix the bug on my sign-up page", and it decides what to do next: open a website, read a file, run code, check the result, try again.

An AI chat assistant answers, and you act on the answer. An AI agent acts, and you check what it did. ChatGPT is mainly an AI chat assistant, but it now includes agent features, and so do the other big AI chat assistants. Coding tools such as Claude Code and Codex are agents that work directly in your project's files.

Because agents act, their mistakes can cost more: an agent can send an email or delete a file. Start with tasks where you can review the result before anything is sent or permanently changed.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

</Accordion>

### Getting better answers

<Accordion type="single" collapsible>

<AccordionItem value="good-prompt">
<AccordionTrigger>How do I write a good prompt?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Write it like a brief for a capable colleague on their first day: someone smart who knows nothing about you, your situation or what a good result looks like. Weak answers usually come from missing information rather than the wrong wording, because whatever you leave out, the AI fills in with something generic.

A good prompt covers four things:

1. **The task:** what you want, in one sentence.
2. **The context:** who you are, who the result is for, and the details it can't guess.
3. **What a good result looks like:** format, length, tone, and anything to avoid.
4. **An example,** if you have one: a past version you liked, or a sample of your own writing.

Copy this and fill it in:

```
Task: [what you want, in one sentence]

Context:
- About me: [your role or situation]
- Who it's for: [the reader or audience, and what they already know]
- Key details: [facts, numbers, names, constraints]

What I want back:
- Format and length: [for example a table, five bullet points, under 150 words]
- Tone: [for example friendly and direct]
- Avoid: [anything you don't want]

Ask me questions first if anything important is missing.
```

Here it is filled in:

```
Task: Help me prepare for a job interview for a junior QA (quality assurance) tester role.

Context:
- About me: I finished a QA course three months ago and tested two mobile apps as a volunteer. I don't have full-time experience in IT yet.
- Who it's for: the interview is with a mid-sized company that builds banking apps. It's in English, which is my second language.
- Key details: the job ad asks for writing test cases, reporting bugs in Jira and basic SQL.

What I want back:
- Format and length: the 8 questions I'm most likely to be asked, each with a short note on what the interviewer wants to hear.
- Tone: encouraging but honest.
- Avoid: generic advice like "be yourself".

Ask me questions first if anything important is missing.
```

Keep the last line in every prompt. It lets the AI ask for what it needs instead of guessing.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="different-answers">
<AccordionTrigger>Why do I get a different answer every time I ask?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

It's built that way. At each step, the model picks from several likely next words, with some randomness, instead of always taking the single most likely one. Over a whole reply, those small choices add up to a different answer. Earlier messages in the chat, memory features and whether it searched the web change the result too.

You can use this on purpose. For anything open-ended, such as names, ideas or wording, ask for several versions and compare them. For facts, treat a changing answer as a warning: if you ask again and the answer changes, the model isn't reliable on that point, so check it somewhere else.

**Try this:**

```
Give me three different versions of this, each taking a different approach, and say in one line what makes each one different.
```

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="close-not-right">
<AccordionTrigger>The answer is close, but not right. Should I start a new chat?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Not straight away. Reply in the same chat with exactly what to change, because it still has all your context. "Shorter, drop the second paragraph, and use an example from retail instead of banking" gives you a much better next version than "make it better".

If two or three corrections don't fix it, or the chat has become very long, start again. In long conversations, the AI can misunderstand something early on and keep repeating that mistake in the replies after it. Go back to your first prompt, add the details that were missing, and paste it into a new chat.

**Try this:** before asking for another rewrite, check that it understood you:

```
Before you rewrite it, tell me in two sentences what you think I'm asking for.
```

If its summary is wrong, you've found the misunderstanding, and you can correct it directly.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

</Accordion>

### Choosing a tool and paying for it

<Accordion type="single" collapsible>

<AccordionItem value="which-ai">
<AccordionTrigger>Which AI should I use: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

For everyday tasks, such as explaining, writing, summarising, brainstorming and studying, the main AI chat assistants are similar enough in quality that learning one well helps you more than finding the best one. Each company releases new versions every few months, so any ranking goes out of date quickly.

A few differences are worth knowing:

- **ChatGPT** is the most widely used, and brings many features together in one app, such as image generation, voice conversations and web search.
- **Claude** is popular for writing and coding, and searches for it grew fast in 2026.
- **Gemini** is built into Google's apps, such as Gmail and Google Docs.
- **Microsoft Copilot** is built into Microsoft's apps, such as Word, Excel and Outlook, which is useful if your employer uses them.

**Try this:** give the same real task to two of them, for example explaining a topic from your course or improving a paragraph from your CV. Use the one whose answer you'd rather work with.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="free-or-paid">
<AccordionTrigger>Is the free version enough? Why do I keep hitting limits?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

For learning and everyday use, the free plans are enough to start with. The main paid plans for ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini cost about 20 US dollars a month, and ChatGPT Go and Google AI Plus are cheaper plans in between. Prices vary by country. Paid plans give you more messages, the newest and strongest models, bigger files and extra features such as agents.

Limits exist because every reply costs the company computing power. Long replies, big files, images and the "thinking" modes that work through a problem before answering cost more than a short text reply. That's why one long chat with several attachments uses up your limit much faster than a few short questions.

To make your free messages last longer:

- Start a new chat for each new topic, instead of keeping one endless chat.
- Paste in only the part of a document you need, not the whole file.
- Save the thinking modes for hard problems, and use the standard mode for quick tasks.

If you're thinking of paying, try one plan for a month and see whether you run into the limits in real use before you add another.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

</Accordion>

### Trust and privacy

<Accordion type="single" collapsible>

<AccordionItem value="makes-things-up">
<AccordionTrigger>Why does it make things up? Can I trust it?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

It makes things up because of how it writes. It predicts what sounds likely, so when it doesn't have the information, it still writes an answer that sounds right. That's called a hallucination. Newer models hallucinate less, but none of them have stopped, and the invented parts are written in the same confident tone as the correct ones.

So trust it the way you'd trust a very well-read friend who sometimes misremembers: good for explaining, drafting and ideas, and worth checking whenever a mistake would cost you something. Always check:

- numbers, dates and statistics
- names, quotes and who said what
- links, book titles and references to studies, because invented sources are a common hallucination
- anything about law, health or money

**Try this:**

```
List every factual claim in your answer. For each one, say whether it came from what I gave you, from a source you can link, or from your own training.
```

Then check the claims in the last group yourself. Asking "are you sure?" doesn't work as a check: it answers that question the same way it answered everything else, by predicting a likely reply.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="always-agrees">
<AccordionTrigger>Why does it always agree with me?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Because agreeable answers were rewarded during training. People rated the model's replies, and replies that agreed with them and praised their ideas tended to get better ratings, so the model learned to do more of it. This is called sycophancy. A Stanford-led study published in the journal Science in March 2026 tested 11 AI models and found they backed users' actions 49% more often than people did, even when the user described deceiving or harming someone. People also rated the flattering answers as better, and trusted them more.

Keep this in mind whenever you ask for feedback. Praise from an AI chat assistant tells you very little about whether your idea or your CV is good. And if you disagree with an answer that was correct, the AI may change its answer to agree with you.

**Try this:** ask it to take the other side.

```
Here's my plan: [paste it]. Don't tell me what's good about it. Act as a sceptical hiring manager and list the five biggest weaknesses, starting with the most serious.
```

Swap "hiring manager" for whoever has to say yes: a lecturer, a client, an investor.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="never-share">
<AccordionTrigger>What should I never tell it or upload?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Anything you wouldn't want a stranger to read. Your chats are stored on the company's servers. Staff can review them in some cases, for example to check for misuse, your chats may be used to train future models depending on your settings, and like any stored data they could be exposed in a data breach.

Keep these out:

- passwords, PINs and security codes
- ID, passport and bank card numbers
- health details you'd keep private
- other people's personal details: names, phone numbers, addresses, photos of their faces
- your employer's or clients' confidential documents, unless your company has approved the tool for that work
- anything you've agreed in a contract not to share

You can still get help with sensitive tasks. Replace real names with "Client A" or "my manager", remove numbers that identify anyone, and describe the situation instead of pasting the document.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="training-settings">
<AccordionTrigger>Are my chats used to train it? How do I stop that?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

On a personal account, usually yes, unless you switch it off. On work and school plans, such as ChatGPT Business or Enterprise and Claude Team or Enterprise, your chats aren't used for training by default.

Where to switch it off on a personal account:

- **ChatGPT:** go to **Settings**, then **Data controls**, and turn off **Improve the model for everyone**. For a single conversation, start a **Temporary Chat** instead: it isn't used for training, doesn't create memories, and is deleted after 30 days.
- **Claude:** go to **Settings**, then **Privacy**, and turn off **Help improve our AI models**. Incognito chats are never used for training.
- **Gemini:** open **Activity** from the Gemini app menu, or go to myactivity.google.com/product/gemini, and turn off **Keep Activity**. It's on by default. With it off, Google still keeps new chats for up to 72 hours.

Switching the setting off doesn't cover everything. It only applies to new chats from that point, not the ones you've already had. And in ChatGPT and Claude, rating an answer with a thumbs up or down sends that conversation to the company as feedback, which can be used for training even with the setting off. Skip the ratings on chats you want to keep private.

Apps move their menus often. If a setting isn't where this cheat sheet says it is, search the app's help pages for "data controls" or "model training".

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="who-can-see">
<AccordionTrigger>Can my employer or my school see my chats?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

On a personal account, your employer and your school have no access to your chats, unless you share them, use a computer they monitor, or stay signed in on a shared computer.

On a work or school account, treat your chats like work email and assume your employer can read them. On most company plans, administrators can export conversations or search them with compliance tools: this applies to ChatGPT Enterprise, Claude Team and Enterprise, and Microsoft Copilot, for example. ChatGPT Business is an exception: there, admins can't see members' private chats by default.

Check before you share a link to a chat, too. Anyone who has the link can usually open the whole conversation.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="biased">
<AccordionTrigger>Is it biased?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Yes, it can be. It learned from text people wrote, and that text contains stereotypes and gaps: some languages, countries and groups appear far more often than others. Companies train their models to reduce this bias, and you can still see it, for example in which names appear in an example story, whose point of view an answer takes, or how much the AI knows about a smaller country such as Moldova.

**Try this:**

```
Whose point of view is missing from this answer? Answer again for someone living and working in Moldova.
```

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

### AI and your career

<Accordion type="single" collapsible>

<AccordionItem value="take-my-job">
<AccordionTrigger>Will AI take my job?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Nobody knows for certain, so be careful with anyone who sounds sure, whether they say AI will take your job or that it won't. What's clearer is that AI changes tasks before it changes whole jobs: drafting, summarising, writing first versions of code, answering routine questions. People whose work is mostly made of those tasks feel the change first.

The clearest early evidence is about young people starting out. A Stanford study of US payroll data found that by mid-2026, employment of 22 to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, was 19% lower than it would have been if it had followed the same trend as less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in the same jobs showed no such gap, and most of the difference came from companies hiring fewer young people, rather than laying off people who already worked there. The authors describe this as an early signal and don't claim AI is the proven cause, and other researchers point out that a general slowdown in hiring could explain part of it.

What you can do:

- **Learn to use AI on the tasks in the job you want,** so you're the person who does them faster and checks the result.
- **Build the skills AI is weak at:** judging whether an answer is right, understanding what a user or customer needs, and explaining decisions to people.
- **Collect proof of your work:** something you built, tested or improved, that an employer can look at.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="first-role">
<AccordionTrigger>If AI does the junior tasks, how do I get my first job in tech?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

This is a real problem, and the industry hasn't solved it: if companies stop hiring juniors, they won't have seniors in a few years' time. Until companies solve this, your best chance of getting hired is to show that you can do the job well using AI.

Someone still has to notice when AI's code, test case or analysis is wrong, explain why, and take responsibility for the result. That's what you can show:

- **Build something small and real,** such as a tool for a problem you know, an app you've tested properly, or an analysis of data you care about. Put it online, and write down what you did and what went wrong along the way.
- **Talk about how you used AI.** In interviews, describe where AI helped, where it was wrong, and how you caught the mistake.
- **Learn the fundamentals of your field anyway,** because they're what let you catch the mistakes. The next question covers this for coding.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="learn-to-code">
<AccordionTrigger>Should I still learn to code?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

If you want to work in tech, yes. AI writes a lot of code now, so more of the work is reading that code, testing it, fixing it, and deciding whether it does what users need. You can only do that if you understand the basics: how a program is structured, how data moves through an app, and how to find and fix a bug.

AI can also make learning faster, if you use it as a tutor and not to do the exercises for you.

**Try this:**

```
I'm a beginner learning [Python / JavaScript / SQL]. Don't write the solution for me. Explain the idea behind this task, give me one hint, and let me try first. Then check my code and explain each mistake.
```

If you'd rather build your own idea than become a developer, AI app builders such as Lovable let you describe an app and build it in a conversation. Knowing the basics helps there too, because you'll describe what you want more precisely and notice when something's wrong.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="cv-and-studies">
<AccordionTrigger>Is it OK to use AI for my CV, cover letter or studies? Can people tell?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Generally, you can use AI to improve your own work, but you can't use it to invent experience, results or sources, or to hand in work that isn't yours. The exact rules depend on where you are: your university or employer may have its own policy, so check it, and say you used AI when they ask you to.

For CVs and applications:

- Use it to improve your wording, tailor your CV to a specific job ad, and practise interview questions.
- Never let it add skills, results or experience you don't have. You'll be asked about them.
- Rewrite the result in your own words, because you'll need to talk confidently about everything in it.

Can people tell? Not reliably. AI detectors make mistakes. Turnitin, whose AI detector many universities use, says its results shouldn't be the only basis for action against a student, and some universities have switched the detector off. In a 2023 Stanford study, seven detectors wrongly labelled more than half of the essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated.

If English is your second language, keep your drafts and notes, so you can show how your work developed if anyone asks.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

### Bigger questions

<Accordion type="single" collapsible>

<AccordionItem value="environment">
<AccordionTrigger>Is AI bad for the environment?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Its total footprint is large and growing fast. The footprint of a single question is small, and the exact figures are disputed.

Google says a typical text question to Gemini uses about as much electricity as watching TV for nine seconds, and about five drops of water. OpenAI has given a similar figure for ChatGPT. These are the companies' own numbers. Independent researchers point out that they leave out the energy used to train the models and the water used to generate the electricity, and that long "thinking" answers, images and video use much more than a short text reply.

The bigger problem is how much electricity AI uses in total. Data centres, the buildings full of computers where these models run, used about 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024. The International Energy Agency expects that to more than double by 2030, with AI as the biggest reason for that growth.

For your own use, one text question has a small footprint, but the footprint grows quickly with heavy use. Generating twenty images to pick one uses far more than a few text questions.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="thinking-less">
<AccordionTrigger>Is AI making us think less?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

It can, depending on how you use it. In a 2025 survey by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University, 319 people described how they used AI at work. Those who had more confidence in AI reported doing less critical thinking, and those who had more confidence in their own skills reported doing more. The study is based on what people said about themselves, so it shows a pattern rather than proof.

Do the thinking first, then use AI to check and challenge it, instead of asking AI to do the thinking for you.

**Try this:**

- Write your own first attempt before you ask AI, then ask it to critique your version.
- Ask it to quiz you instead of explaining:

```
Ask me five questions to check whether I understand [topic]. Ask them one at a time, wait for my answer, and tell me what I got wrong.
```

- When it gives you an answer you like, ask: "What's the strongest argument against this?"

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="personal-problems">
<AccordionTrigger>Is it OK to talk to it about personal problems?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

It can help you put your thoughts in order, prepare for a difficult conversation, or see your options more clearly. Keep three limits in mind:

- **It tends to agree with you** (see "Why does it always agree with me?"), so it may confirm a view you should question instead.
- **It isn't a therapist or a doctor,** and it has no responsibility for you or what happens next.
- **What you share is stored,** so think about what you'd be comfortable with someone else reading.

If you're struggling, talk to a person you trust or a professional. In an emergency in Moldova, call 112.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

<AccordionItem value="make-money">
<AccordionTrigger>Can I make money with AI?</AccordionTrigger>
<AccordionContent>

Yes, though rarely the way "$10,000 a month with AI" videos suggest. AI makes it faster and cheaper to make things. It doesn't find people who'll pay for them.

Realistic ways to make money with AI:

- **Do your current work faster or better,** which can help you take on more responsibility and give you good reasons to ask for a promotion or a pay rise.
- **Offer a service** you can now deliver faster with AI, such as writing, translation, research, simple websites, or setting up automations for small businesses.
- **Build a small product** for a problem you understand well, then find the first people who'll pay for it.

For a service or a product, the hard part is finding customers, and you'll need to do that part yourself.

</AccordionContent>
</AccordionItem>

### Where to go from here

The quickest way to get comfortable with AI is to use it on something real this week: a topic from your studies, a task from work, or a small tool you've wanted for a while.

- [How AI Works](https://littleparrot.app/nest/toolkit/9a4596f0-38ca-4e2b-a7cf-0ae57dba3dc2) explains more of the vocabulary, and what each term changes about how you prompt.
- [Build a Prompt in Three Parts](https://littleparrot.app/nest/toolkit/ecaaeb78-0bc1-402d-8d7a-c214997e91c8) explains in more detail how to give the AI the context it's missing.
- [Launch Your App Idea with Lovable](https://littleparrot.app/journeys/launch-your-app-idea-with-lovable) is a learning journey for when you're ready to build your own app idea with AI. You plan a small first version, build it with Lovable, fix what breaks, publish it on your own domain and work out what to charge.
