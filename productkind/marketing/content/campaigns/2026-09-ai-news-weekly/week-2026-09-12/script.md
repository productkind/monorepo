# Five AI stories from this week

Five AI stories from this week change what you do. You can miss the rest.

OpenAI's AI agents used more than ten undisclosed websites, including an old chemistry wiki, to pass messages between themselves. Reuters reported it this week. If an AI acts for you, read what it did afterwards.

ChatGPT now connects to Dropbox, Box and SharePoint. Point it at one folder instead of uploading copies of everything.

Its image model takes a hand drawing now. Sketch the layout, describe the style, skip the paragraph explaining where things go.

Voice is counted in hours: three a day on Plus, fifteen on Pro, so you can tell which plan you need.

And McKinsey found thirty-two per cent of companies had cancelled a software purchase because AI could build it instead. That one's from late August. Worth having in your next build-or-buy conversation.

[pause][curious] Which of these changes anything for you?

## Hooks to test

Four openers. Every one is a drop-in replacement for the first line: the body, the order and the closing question stay exactly as written above, so a test between them reads the hook and nothing else. All four land between four and eight seconds.

### A. Baseline

> Five AI stories from this week change what you do. You can miss the rest.

Permission, stated flat. The one to beat.

### B. It wasn't for you

> Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were.

Twelve words. Names the week you already spent scrolling and tells you it went on things aimed at somebody else, then hands you the part that was yours. The sting and the relief sit in the same sentence, which is why this is the shortest and the sharpest.

### C. You're not behind

> You're not behind. Five AI stories from this week are for people building apps. The rest of it was never yours.

Goes at the anxiety directly and takes it off the table before offering the five. "The rest of it was never yours" is the line doing the work: it reframes everything they did not read as somebody else's post, rather than a backlog they still owe.

### D. Named audience

> If you build apps with AI, five things happened this week. You can leave the rest where it is.

The most literal version. Says who it is for in the first four words, which is the cheapest way to make somebody stop, and leaves the rest of the week on the floor without arguing about it.

Notes on picking: B and C both charge the viewer for time already spent, which is the feeling we are after and also the one that can tip into scolding. B stays the right side of it because it blames the news rather than the reader. D is the safe one and the weakest on the emotion, worth keeping in the test as the control for "does naming the audience beat naming the feeling".

## Show name

Five candidates, each one saying what the show actually is. Shown with hook B. Checked against existing podcasts on 13 September.

The test every name here has to pass: a stranger hears it once and knows the subject. "The Code Report" works because "code" is in it. Names built only on tone or format ("The Brief", "Nothing Urgent", "The Short Version") leave the subject out and make the viewer work it out from the content, which is the wrong way round.

Audience wording comes from `positioning-2026-08.md`, which says "vibe coding" twenty-four times and "builder" twice, so these lean on the term we actually use.

### 1. The Vibe Coding Report, by Little Parrot

> This is the Vibe Coding Report, by Little Parrot. Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were.

The direct parallel: swap "code" for the thing our audience calls what they do. Says the subject and the audience in three words. Nothing exists under this exact name, though "Vibe Coding" and "Vibe Coded" are both taken as podcasts, so the category is busy.

### 2. AI News for Vibe Coders, by Little Parrot

> This is AI News for Vibe Coders, by Little Parrot. Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were.

Leaves nothing to work out. Flat, and that is the point: it names the subject, the audience and the genre in five words, and it is the only one here nobody could misread.

### 3. The AI Build Report, by Little Parrot

> This is the AI Build Report, by Little Parrot. Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were.

Says AI and says making things, without betting the name on a term that may not survive. The one to pick if the product managers in the audience would not call themselves vibe coders, which is worth deciding before the name is locked.

### 4. Five AI Things, by Little Parrot

> This is Five AI Things, by Little Parrot. Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were.

Subject and format in three words. Weakest on audience, strongest on the promise, and the only one of the five that still fits if the show ever widens past people building apps.

### 5. The Week in AI, by Little Parrot

> This is the Week in AI, by Little Parrot. Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were.

Says the subject and the cadence and nothing else. The most immediately understood and the most crowded: it is close to the standing title of a dozen newsletters, so the attribution is doing all the work of telling these apart.

### The decision underneath the name

Names 1 and 2 bet on "vibe coding". It is our dominant term and it dates the show to this period, and the product managers in the audience may not use it about themselves. Names 3, 4 and 5 stay neutral and say less about who it is for. That trade is the real choice here, not the wording.

### Where the name sits

Two placements, and they behave differently.

- **Cold open**, name first, as written above. The viewer knows what they have found before the content starts. Costs about a second of the first two, which on TikTok is the expensive part.
- **After the first beat**, which is what Fireship actually does: "Most of this week's AI news wasn't for you. These five were. This is the Parrot Report." The hook earns the attention, then the name claims it. Better retention, and the name still lands in the first five seconds.

The second is the stronger default. Reserve the cold open for LinkedIn, where the audience is scrolling more slowly and a named show reads as a commitment rather than a delay.

## Full opening

Three versions that do all three jobs at once: say what the show is, say these are the only stories that reach you, and say you are free to drop everything else. Each replaces the hook line entirely; the body, the order and the closing question stay as written.

### Long

> The Vibe Coding Report, by Little Parrot. Every week, the five AI stories that change how you're building. You don't have to follow anything else.

About ten seconds. The only version that says "every week", which is what turns a video into a show. It also delays the first story to the ten second mark, and on TikTok that is most of the window where people decide to stay.

### Standard

> The Vibe Coding Report, by Little Parrot. Five AI stories a week that change how you're building, so you can ignore the rest.

About eight and a half seconds. Keeps the weekly promise inside "a week" rather than spending a sentence on it, and the permission rides on the same breath as the filter instead of standing alone. The one to use if only one of these gets made.

### Tight

> The Vibe Coding Report, by Little Parrot. Five AI stories that change how you're building. Ignore the rest.

About seven seconds, three short sentences, no subordinate clauses. Loses the weekly cadence, which the title card can carry instead. The best fit for TikTok, where the first three seconds decide the rest.

### What this costs

The current body runs about forty-five seconds. These openings take the whole video to roughly fifty-two to fifty-five, against a campaign standard of forty-five. Two ways back under:

- Drop the voice-limits story, the smallest of the five. That returns about seven seconds and the opening still promises five, so it would have to become four.
- Put the name on a title card instead of in the narration, and open on "Five AI stories a week that change how you're building, so you can ignore the rest." That returns about three seconds and keeps all five stories, at the cost of the name never being said aloud.

The second is the better trade for TikTok. The first is better for LinkedIn, where saying the name is worth more than a story is.
