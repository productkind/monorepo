# Cross-source synthesis: what the experts recommend

Built from the seven per-source extractions in this folder (all from full transcripts, read 2026-09-24). This is the experts' advice in their own terms, not yet adapted to our voice or rules. Each principle lists who teaches it; the more sources agree, the stronger the signal.

Sources: **Parr** (Sam Parr with Alex Garcia and Brian Blum, copywriting), **Kallaway** (neuroscience of addictive storytelling), **Mino** (Mino Lee, viral hooks), **Ralston** (Caleb Ralston with Tiffany K. Guillen, personal brand), **Galloway** (Paddy Galloway, YouTube strategy), **Garcia** (Alex Garcia on The Grant Owen Podcast, future of social), **Howard** (Anna Corinne Howard, curiosity).

Coverage note: none of the seven talks about LinkedIn specifically. Short video is covered in depth (Mino, Garcia, Kallaway, Galloway, Parr). Written posts are covered through classic copywriting (Parr) and through storytelling principles that the sources say apply to "emails, tweets, posts" too (Kallaway, Mino).

## 1. Before you write: the idea sets the ceiling

| Principle | Who | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The idea caps how far a piece can travel | Galloway, Garcia | No hook or edit saves a weak idea. "The concept is so much more important" than budget. |
| Glance test | Galloway | If the idea can't be said in one second as a one-line log line, it isn't an idea yet. |
| It must pose a question you have to watch to answer | Galloway, Parr, Kallaway | Every 10m+ view video leaves a question only the content resolves. |
| Proven idea + your twist | Mino, Galloway, Garcia, Parr | Don't invent from scratch. Take a structure that's already winning (in your niche, an adjacent niche or another category) and twist it about 3%. Never copy word for word. |
| Borrow formats from other categories | Garcia, Galloway | "$100 accountant vs $1,000 accountant" came from cooking's "$1 vs $10" format. Fashion is where new formats appear first. |
| Painful problem + your solution = idea | Ralston | List 3 to 10 costly problems your ideal customer has; each problem has many angles; each angle is a piece. |
| Contrarian take | Ralston, Mino | What does everyone in your niche say that you disagree with? Lead with it. Ralston says contrarian openings "typically do really well as a hook". |
| Specificity and obsession beat universality | Howard, Mino, Ralston | Universal messages become "empty platitudes that AI could write". Hyper-specific detail and visible obsession draw people in even when they don't share the interest. |
| Start from lived experience and pain points | Garcia, Mino, Ralston | Your own situations are relatable and give novelty "baked in". A student can post as a student ("interest bank"), never as a fake expert. |
| Find the story in the day | Garcia | A day of eight meetings becomes a story once framed with a goal, a day count and stakes. |
| Universal language for core, casual and new viewers | Galloway | Every piece should work for your die-hard followers and a total stranger. Cut jargon (the curse of knowledge). |
| Generate many, keep one | Galloway | 100 ideas, filter to 10 with elimination criteria ("can we package it?" removes about two thirds), pick the one you'd bet your career on. |

## 2. The hook: first 1 to 3 seconds of a video, first line of a post

**Why it matters.** Ogilvy: five times more people read the headline than the body (Parr). Skip rate (swipes before 3 seconds) should stay under 50% (Garcia; Parr's hosts say holding over 50% at 3 seconds signals a hit). Sam Parr's 4m-view video held about 80% at 3 seconds.

| Principle | Who | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three hook layers | Garcia | On-screen text hook, visual hook and spoken hook. Plan all three. |
| Plan seconds 1, 2 and 3 individually | Garcia | What you show, say and do in each second. Then plan 3-second blocks to 12 seconds. |
| Open a curiosity gap ("mind the gap") | Parr, Galloway, Kallaway, Mino | Say something incomplete or contradictory that only the rest resolves. |
| The gap must be specific, not an empty teaser | Kallaway, Mino | "Something crazy happened" fails because the viewer can't form a guess. Give just enough to predict. |
| Visual pattern-disrupt | Garcia, Mino | Change the angle, add an action, choose the aesthetic. Show what is not usually shown in that format. |
| "Click to unpause" first frame | Galloway | The frame is a paused moment just before the payoff (mid-action, mid-sentence), not someone smiling at the camera. |
| Least context possible | Galloway | "This is an FBI agent. He's got 24 hours to catch me, go." Drip-feed names, backstory and credentials later. |
| Extreme or emphasised word | Mino | Provoke shock, curiosity, fear or anger. Painful emotions are the strongest; negative hooks work. |
| Specific number, name or visual proof | Mino, Parr | Can two or three times views. Specifics give people something to compare themselves with (and comment about). |
| Proof or credibility in sentence one | Mino, Ralston | You're a stranger in the feed. Show results, not names; show them rather than list them. |
| Read the viewer's pain back to them | Mino, Parr | Describe their exact state so they feel heard. State their worst fear. |
| Call out the specific audience | Parr, Ralston | "Niches make riches." Insider lingo filters for the right viewer. |
| Short stated time frame | Mino | "Six objections in 60 seconds" signals high value per second. |
| The first word or sentence has to punch | Parr | Self-deprecating ("My friends think I'm smart. I'm not."), shocking visual comparison ("Imagine storing your food in the toilet bowl"), breaking the fourth wall. |
| Contradiction | Galloway | "I found the cheapest 911 GT3... but nobody will buy it." Good, better, best rewrite: plain, then add story, then superlative, then contradiction. |
| Fewest words, plain words | Galloway, Parr | Readable at a glance. No all caps. Text should complement the visual, not repeat it. |

**Hook formulas the sources give (verbatim structures, fill in the blanks):**

- Mino's five parts: proven idea + twist; extreme word; confirm in sentence two; specific proof; re-hook.
- "I can't [X], so today I'm [surprising Y]." (Mino)
- "Is it possible to [outcome] in [short time or constraint]?" Needs a question the viewer has actually asked themselves, real stakes and a real chance of failure. Don't reveal the answer in the first 3 seconds. (Mino)
- "Here's how to [specific, differentiated outcome]." + pain points read back + "The solution that's always worked for me is..." + "Now, I know you're probably thinking..." (Mino)
- "POV: [relatable or celebratory real moment]", real clip first, then narration. (Mino)
- "$X version vs $10X version" of your thing. (Garcia)
- "This is day [N] of trying to [big goal], but today [obstacle], because [constraint], and [personal stake]." (Garcia)
- "Imagine [something obviously absurd]. But that's exactly what you're doing when you [everyday behaviour]." (Parr)
- "You're reading this not because you want to, but because I want you to." (Parr, fourth wall)

## 3. Seconds 3 to 10: confirm the promise or lose the trust

This is the most repeated warning across sources and the one "most people get wrong" (Mino).

| Principle | Who | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirm the hook in sentence two | Mino | Within 5 seconds, make it abundantly clear the video is about what the hook said. Otherwise it's clickbait, and viewers trust you less next time on the For You page. |
| Deliver the promise within 10 seconds | Galloway | 60 to 70% of viewers can leave in the first 10 seconds, nearly always because they didn't get what they came for. Visual confirmation beats verbal. |
| Match hook type to real purpose | Mino | Teaching content gets an educational hook; suspense content gets a challenge or story hook. |
| Seamless flow | Galloway | "The best intro doesn't feel like an intro." No "anyway, let's get into it". |

## 4. The body: keep the loop running

**Kallaway's four-step addiction loop** is the most complete structure any source gives, and the others describe parts of it:

1. **Stakes.** A character (you, a client, someone you talked to), something at risk, urgency. Before the story starts, the viewer must know who, what's at risk and why now. Stakes don't need drama, only personal relevance ("48 hours before the paid budget ran out"). Most common mistake: skipping this.
2. **The big question.** A specific question the viewer wants to solve, loaded as early as possible. Just enough information to predict, not enough to overwhelm. Before any important reveal: stop, back up, add context so they guess first.
3. **The head fake.** A reveal that breaks their prediction. Surprising but logical: plant clues beforehand. A random twist confuses rather than rewards.
4. **The re-hook.** Close one loop and open the next in the same breath. Never a moment where the viewer thinks "OK, I'm done". Phrases: "which would have been great except", "but here's the problem with that", "and that's when I realised", "which is exactly why".

Supporting principles:

| Principle | Who | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stakes menu | Galloway | Doubter or enemy, time limit, reward, punishment, fear of missing out. For teaching content: the real cost of not paying attention ("$3 million retirement difference"). |
| Emotional why, not logistics | Mino | Nobody cares about the plan; they care about why it matters to you. |
| Stack open loops through the whole piece | Parr, Kallaway, Mino | One host adds a new curiosity gap "almost every 8 seconds". |
| Danger zones | Kallaway, Garcia, Parr | Every section end is where attention drops. Put a re-hook there. Use the retention graph to find the drop-off second and add a gap just before it. |
| But / therefore, not "and then" | Parr | South Park's rule for linking beats. |
| Don't lead with the lesson | Mino | Opening with the takeaway closes the loop. It makes the piece educational, which is fine, but it isn't a story. |
| Drag out the key moment | Mino | Stretch the one-sentence moment over several beats. |
| Reveal the uncomfortable details | Mino, Ralston | If you don't feel slightly exposed, you're probably hiding the detail people would care about. Share insecurities; don't overshare. |
| Handle objections | Mino, Parr | "Now, I know you're probably thinking..." Mino credits this line with a video that brought about 30,000 followers. |
| Failed solution / not your fault | Parr | "You tried X and it didn't work. That's not your fault. You just didn't have Y." |
| Get early yeses | Parr | Lines the viewer nods at, as early as possible. |
| Make numbers visual | Parr | A bare number "means nothing". Show 150g of fat as a brick. |
| Tease what's next | Parr | "But wait until..." keeps people through the current section. |
| Long isn't bad, boring is | Parr | "There's no such thing as too long, just too boring." One host's videos did better at 1:15 to 1:20 than at 45 seconds once loaded with gaps. |
| Avoid conclusion language mid-piece | Galloway | "At the end of the day" causes drop-off across all audiences. |
| Never make a newcomer feel they missed something | Galloway | No "as you saw in my last video". Sow context inside natural lines. |
| Customer is the hero, brand is the guide | Parr | Hero's journey with the customer at the centre. Founder story variant: the specific moment you wished you'd had this. |

## 5. Sentence craft (written posts and captions especially)

All from Parr unless noted.

- **Slippery slope:** the only job of sentence one is to get sentence two read, and so on (Joe Sugarman).
- **Rhythm:** vary length (short, medium, long, short). Start some sentences with And or But.
- **One point per sentence.** Turn commas into full stops.
- **Seventh-grade reading level**, checked in Hemingway App. Buffett averages about 17 words a sentence.
- **Cut a third, three times.** Write the rough draft first, then edit.
- **Talk like you already know them.** Don't be stiff.
- **Humour** if it fits the brand.
- **Twist a well-known phrase** so it becomes yours.
- **Write for the right reader,** not everyone. The goal is for the right person to read all of it.

## 6. The ending

| Principle | Who | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The payoff must resolve the loop | Parr, Mino, Kallaway | A curiosity gap that isn't resolved by real content is "junk" clickbait. |
| Hold the product back (Trojan horse) | Parr | Build the problem, cause and desire first. Weak ads throw the product in immediately. |
| Don't value-edge | Ralston | Give the full takeaway away rather than withholding it to sell; capture email instead. |
| Social proof near the close | Parr | "Trusted by 100,000 dads just like you." |
| Product inside the story | Galloway | Red Bull never stops to say "buy Red Bull". |

## 7. Measure and iterate

| Metric or method | Who | Benchmark or rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skip rate (3 seconds) | Garcia, Parr | Under 50% swiping away. Over 50% means the hook is the problem. |
| Average watch time (45 to 60s videos) | Garcia | 12 seconds is "where good videos die". Watch the trend in the first hours: rising can pop, flat is fine, falling is "cooked". |
| Retention graph | Garcia, Parr, Galloway | Find recurring drop-off seconds across videos; fix what happens there. |
| Likes to shares | Garcia | About 2 likes per share is strong. Shares are the strongest signal on Instagram. |
| Views first, not CTR or AVD | Galloway | Views are the all-in-one metric; plus retention curves. |
| Business outcome per piece | Ralston | Decide before posting: exposure (views), community (comments, shares) or conversion (leads, sign-ups). A niche video with few views that signs a client worked. |
| Top 10% vs 90-day median | Ralston | Monthly: study your top 10% against the median, hypothesise what they share (topic, format, hook structure, first frame), add 1 to 3 validated items to a checklist. |
| At tiny view counts, relative outliers are the signal | Ralston | "If you do 30 videos, one of them is going to get 415." Double down on it. |
| Predict before publishing | Galloway | Predict first hour, day and week; judge against the expectation. |
| Cheap topic test | Garcia | B-roll plus one line of on-screen text. If it pops, the topic is proven; scale it into a series, carousel or talking video. |
| Trial reels | Garcia | Test hook and on-screen text variants before posting to the main feed. |
| Accordion method | Ralston | Expand volume to learn fast, contract to fewer better pieces, repeat. |
| Test first, strategy later | Garcia | Spend one day a week planning the week's tests. |
| Repeat yourself | Ralston, Galloway | Audiences forget within seconds. Re-use winning ideas and formats; turn winners into series. |
| Repurpose winners | Garcia, Parr | A winning on-screen line becomes an email subject, ad headline or landing page. |

## 8. Practice habits

- **Copy work** (Parr): copy classic ads, top short stories, famous scripts word for word, by hand, about an hour a day for 3 to 6 months. For short video: transcribe five multi-million-view scripts and mark the transition words and tension points.
- **Study playlists** (Galloway): save great hooks, intros, titles and outliers as you scroll; review 3 to 5 hours a week.
- **Ideal viewer document** (Mino): one person, in granular detail; their exact pains and fears go straight into hooks.
- **Write 10+ hooks or titles per piece** (Galloway).
- **Picture one friend who needs this** before recording (Mino).
- **Delivery for talking to camera** (Mino): emphasise a word in every sentence, gestures, no monotone; good front lighting.
- **Feed your curiosity** (Howard): "see something, save something", chase references you don't understand, act on your own "I want to learn about X" remarks. Creators who struggle to make impactful content often aren't curious enough.
- **Read outside your field** (Parr, Garcia): magazines, fiction, fashion.
- **Use AI for research and ideation, write the final yourself** (Galloway: AI gets writing "80% of the way there"; Parr uses Claude to find stats).

## 9. Where the sources disagree

- **Curiosity-gap packaging.** Parr, Kallaway, Mino and Galloway build on it. Ralston says that on search-driven platforms plain, evergreen packaging beats curiosity titles over time, and uses curiosity packaging only for the launch spike.
- **Optimise for virality or for trust.** Galloway is a self-described "view maximalist". Ralston says optimise for trust and business outcomes and even "sandbag" reach to filter for the right viewer. Galloway concedes small high-value audiences can be the right goal.
- **Hooks at the beginning.** Ralston tells beginners to ignore hook tactics and build the posting habit first, then optimise from data. Everyone else treats the hook as the priority.
- **Storytelling vs education.** Mino calls storytelling an overhyped buzzword for business and prefers educational content for trust and selling. Kallaway and Parr say everything should be a story.
- **Emotion.** Mino recommends negative, painful emotions (fear, anger). Parr says humour is one of the easiest ways to sell if it fits the brand.
- **Length.** Parr: longer converts better if it's interesting. Garcia: B-roll plus text reels of about 8 seconds are valid tests.
- **Niching.** Ralston and Galloway: start narrow and expand. Howard: niching works for selling but flattens content; be "more confusing", with odd combinations of interests.
- **Originality.** Mino, Galloway, Garcia and Parr: remix proven formats. Ralston: don't start ideation from competitors' outliers; start from customer problems and your own credibility bank.
- **Production quality.** Garcia argues aesthetic and composition create demand. Galloway says make it watchable first, then ideas and packaging, and only later polish.
