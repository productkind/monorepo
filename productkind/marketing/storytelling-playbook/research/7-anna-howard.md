# How to fall down a curiosity rabbit hole & reconnect to your creativity: Anna Corinne Howard (Wild Geese podcast)

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Em66j9Xlsqg

## Core thesis (2-4 sentences)

A rabbit hole is not a distraction but your brain telling you to pay attention to something you are curious about, and following it (as "research as a leisure activity") is how you rebuild wonder and creativity. The standard "niche down" and personal branding advice pushes people to package themselves into a neat, universal box, which produces empty platitudes that AI could write and makes you easy to replace. What makes people and content compelling is hyper-specificity, strange combinations of passions and visible obsession; people will meet you there even if they do not share the obsession. The process will not always be fun (synthesis is often stressful), and the goal is presence, wonder and engagement rather than happiness or self-improvement.

## Principles and techniques

### Treat a rabbit hole as a signal, not a distraction

- **What they say:** Quoting the Are.na about page: a rabbit hole is your brain trying to tell you to pay attention to something you are curious about. Allow yourself to go down them.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Curiosity is information about what resonates with you; ignoring it cuts you off from the material that makes your thinking and work distinctive.
- **How to apply:** When something tugs at your attention, give yourself permission to follow it rather than dismissing it as unproductive.
- **Examples they give:** Her reading about ancient Greek communal lamentation, which "enthralled" her and became a casual research project.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:01:32]

### Ignore algorithmic rabbit holes; save what resonates

- **What they say:** Also from the Are.na blurb: ignore algorithmic rabbit holes. "If you see something, save something." Pay attention to what feels resonant to you and save breadcrumbs to build a path you can walk down later.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The rabbit holes worth following are the personal ones, not the ones a feed pushes at you. Saved breadcrumbs let you return and connect things later.
- **How to apply:** Keep a place to save anything that resonates (links, images, notes) the moment you see it; classify it in your own personal way.
- **Examples they give:** Her Are.na channels per topic.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:01:32]

### Creativity is connecting things over time

- **What they say:** "Creativity is just connecting things over time." Build contexts for yourself and your ideas, then analyse what happens when you connect those contexts to somebody else's. The ability to classify information personally is a muscle worth exercising.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Ideas come from accumulated, connected material rather than from a blank page in one sitting.
- **How to apply:** Group saved material into contexts (channels, boards); deliberately link items across contexts and compare your contexts with other people's.
- **Examples they give:** Are.na lets you connect any item in one channel to any other channel.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:02:03], [0:14:24]

### The goal is engaging with the world, not self-improvement

- **What they say:** Quoting the Are.na page: the goal is not self-improvement, it is engaging more deeply with the world. She also says you should not fall down rabbit holes only to make better content; it can be a tool for that, but it is really about maintaining a sense of wonder.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Curiosity pursued only instrumentally loses its point; wonder is valuable for your personhood.
- **How to apply:** Pursue some rabbit holes with no output in mind.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond her own podcast research.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:02:03], [0:12:20]

### Question the "niche down" and one-line "I help" statement advice

- **What they say:** Niching down is one of the first things any content creator is told: pick a niche, have an elevator pitch, an "I help" statement with what you do and who you help in one sentence. She finds this is starting to sound like an AI summary of a Google search, and she does not want her personhood to feel like that. She finds niching easier for selling products and services than for creating content.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The internet moves fast, so people want to say "this is who I am" quickly and catch attention, but reducing a person to a neat box strips out what makes them interesting.
- **How to apply:** Keep a clear offer for selling, but do not flatten your content persona into a single-sentence box.
- **Examples they give:** Aesthetic labels like "clean girl aesthetic"; a friend starting content creation who immediately assumed she had to "pick a niche".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:03:36]-[0:05:08]

### Specificity beats universality (what niching gets right)

- **What they say:** What niching down gets right, when explained properly, is that a message trying to be universal is much less impactful. Universal messages turn into empty platitudes "that, let's face it, AI could write for you." What is missing is specificity that adds real texture.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Texture and specific detail are what make a message land; generic statements are indistinguishable and replaceable.
- **How to apply:** When drafting, replace general claims with the specific, textured detail only you would include. Fight the impulse to be universal.
- **Examples they give:** "Likes reading and hiking" is a "midling Hinge profile"; researching historical poison gardens for a murder mystery while learning to make kimchi makes her want to invite you to dinner.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:06:10]-[0:07:12]

### Be more confusing: have edges

- **What they say:** Quoting Michelle Pellezon Lepsitz's essay "Be More Confusing": charismatic, magnetic people are confusing; they have "craggy edges" that snag attention "like a ring catching on a sweater". They have passions that make no sense together, which is exactly what makes them captivating. Part of the magnetism is that you cannot quite figure them out or guess what they will do next.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Edges give people something to hold on to; being a little elusive keeps people interested.
- **How to apply:** Show the odd, unrelated interests rather than hiding them to appear coherent.
- **Examples they give:** A podcast about medieval brewing while learning to code to replace a website cursor with a sparkly firework; collecting vintage toasters for three months then teaching yourself classical Greek.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:05:08]-[0:05:40], [0:07:43]-[0:08:14]

### Connect dots that do not obviously go together

- **What they say:** Quoting the same essay: the most interesting insights, compelling art and innovative solutions come from connecting dots that do not obviously go together, "endeavors that no AI would ever think to combine."
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Unexpected combinations are what generic (and AI) thinking does not produce.
- **How to apply:** Look for links between your unrelated projects and interests; let them inform each other.
- **Examples they give:** Poison gardens plus kimchi; medieval brewing plus coding.
- **Applies to:** ideation / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:06:41]

### Live a project-based life and lead with what you love

- **What they say:** Interesting people live project-based lives full of "strange, unjustifiable, seemingly arbitrary" endeavours. They lead with what they love and would rather talk about their obscure passion than spend mental energy chasing trends or trying to be palatable.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Enthusiasm for something you love is "intoxicating" to others; if you do not know the show, they are excited to tell you about it.
- **How to apply:** Lead conversations and content with your current projects and obsessions, not with trends.
- **Examples they give:** Someone writing novel-length fanfiction about an obscure show.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:06:41], [0:08:14]

### Start with the smallest possible project

- **What they say:** Projects do not have to be big (a screenplay, an EP, a podcast). When starting out, start as small as possible to train your mind that you can do side quests outside work. Start with one project (or two if they are small) and let projects change as you follow your curiosity.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Small projects build the belief and habit before bigger ones.
- **How to apply:** Pick one tiny project; treat it as practice in doing whatever you want even if it is not immediately productive.
- **Examples they give:** Learning line dancing, learning Spanish, researching ancient Greek lamentation; from the essay, learning every word of Nicki Minaj's verse in "Monster".
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:08:44]-[0:10:16], [0:11:19]

### Do not package yourself into a perfectly sellable product

- **What they say:** Content creator advice sells the idea that if you become perfectly understandable and boxed you will make money, but making perfect sense makes it "so so easy for AI to replace you." Packaging yourself for mass consumers makes you more robotic, not less. You should also have projects you never intend to make money from.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** AI is replacing jobs in every market; the human, non-generic parts of you are what resist that. We need to get more in touch with our humanity.
- **How to apply:** Keep some projects deliberately uncommodified; resist smoothing yourself for mass appeal.
- **Examples they give:** None specific.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:10:16]-[0:11:19]

### Creators who struggle often are not curious enough

- **What they say:** Working with podcast hosts, she suspected those having a hard time creating impactful content were not falling down rabbit holes enough and were not getting curious enough. That suspicion is why she started her podcast.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Impactful content draws on genuine, deep curiosity; without it there is little to say.
- **How to apply:** If content feels flat, look at your inputs: are you actually researching and following curiosity?
- **Examples they give:** Her clients as a freelance podcast manager.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:11:50]

### Curiosity is a muscle you build

- **What they say:** People think curiosity is either there or not, but it has taken her a while to build up "the rabbit hole muscle".
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Like any habit, it strengthens with repetition.
- **How to apply:** Practise regularly through small projects, saving breadcrumbs and her research workflow.
- **Examples they give:** Her podcast, where each episode is a rabbit hole.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:11:50]-[0:12:51]

### Start from a general curiosity

- **What they say:** Her rabbit holes begin with a very general curiosity: sometimes a question she wants to answer, sometimes a general idea, sometimes a piece of someone else's content that has stuck in her head that she wants to dig into and understand more.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A loose starting question or idea is enough to pull you in; you do not need a defined topic.
- **How to apply:** Notice the questions and pieces of content that keep coming back to you, and use them as starting points.
- **Examples they give:** Each Wild Geese episode starts this way.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:12:51]-[0:13:22]

### Separate a source dump from note-taking

- **What they say:** She uses Are.na as a preliminary dump for all sources on a topic (images, links, PDFs, YouTube videos, notes) in one channel, then later sits down in Obsidian (her digital garden) to dig into the sources and take notes.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Having all sources for a topic in one place makes them immediately available; mixed media is easy to include; connections between channels become visible.
- **How to apply:** See the framework below.
- **Examples they give:** Are.na as "Pinterest but better" because it has no ads.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:13:22]-[0:15:25]

### Research as a leisure activity is about play

- **What they say:** Quoting Selene Gian's Substack "Research as Leisure Activity": it is intellectual inquiry from idiosyncratic passion, not formal credentials; "fundamentally about play". It is directed by passions and instincts and is "exuberantly undisciplined". The best content on the internet is created by people who turned research into their leisure activity.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Autodidacts following passion become genuine experts in odd corners, which produces the best, most alternative culture. There is always something new to learn, which creates wonder.
- **How to apply:** Treat reading, learning and writing about things you are into as leisure, not homework.
- **Examples they give:** A Tumblr user who wanted to buy a bra and became an expert on Edwardian corsetry and garment industry labour history; someone scanning obscure Japanese fashion magazines who became an amateur archivist of 1990s editorials.
- **Applies to:** ideation / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:15:25]-[0:16:29], [0:20:37]-[0:21:08]

### Treat dense material and unknown references as an invitation

- **What they say:** You can see dense material as frustrating, or as an invitation down a rabbit hole. When you hit a reference you do not understand, it is a conscious choice: instead of skating past it and deciding you will never understand it, go find that thing, save it and read it too.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Dense writing is not pretentious; it builds on many layers of the writer's knowledge, so each unknown reference is a doorway to that context.
- **How to apply:** When you notice "I have no idea what that reference is", look it up and add it to your source dump.
- **Examples they give:** A college professor explaining why feminist theatre theory was so dense.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:17:00]-[0:18:02]

### Find a community of curious people

- **What they say:** Research is strengthened by a social and intellectual community. Outside an institution it is easy to hit a question and stop; a community where you can bounce questions around deepens the rabbit hole.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** You will hit things that are hard to understand alone; being in conversation helps.
- **How to apply:** Find people curious about similar things and talk through questions with them.
- **Examples they give:** Emily Spinach's essay on independent study in 2025 and her resources for independent scholars.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:18:33]-[0:20:05]

### Do not expect it to be fun; aim for presence and wonder

- **What they say:** Fun is not the main objective. There will be worry about not finding the answer, moments more confused than at the start, frustration and feeling lost. The objective is a sense of presence and wonder. Expecting it to be fun and happy all the time takes away resilience.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Projects diverge from hobbies here: hobbies are expected to be fun; projects involve struggle. Accepting that builds resilience.
- **How to apply:** Expect frustrating phases and keep going through them.
- **Examples they give:** Her own research confusion.
- **Applies to:** ideation / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:21:08]-[0:22:13], [0:24:48]-[0:25:19]

### A project of your own makes you engaged, not necessarily happy

- **What they say:** Citing Paul Graham's "A Project of One's Own": a project done voluntarily and by yourself (or your own piece of a group project). Keep working after work, on your own project. Graham says it makes you "excited or engaged" rather than happier; writing, he is mostly worried and puzzled, and the first few attempts often fail.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The enriching part of life is doing work on something that is yours.
- **How to apply:** Own a piece of something voluntarily; accept that synthesis will be hard.
- **Examples they give:** Doing the flyer design for a group event counts.
- **Applies to:** ideation / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:22:45]-[0:23:45]

### Expect research to be enjoyable and synthesis to be stressful

- **What they say:** For her, the research phase is enjoyable, but sitting down to form a finished product is stressful and anxiety-inducing: turning ideas that have floated around for weeks or months into a 30-minute video, with the fear it will fail. That is okay.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Naming it as normal stops the stress from derailing the project.
- **How to apply:** Plan for the synthesis stage to be hard; do not read the anxiety as a sign you are doing it wrong.
- **Examples they give:** Recording Wild Geese episodes.
- **Applies to:** short video / written posts / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:23:45]-[0:24:48]

### Use accountability so research becomes output

- **What they say:** With so much research per episode, it is easy to say "I'm not ready to record yet, I'm still compiling". Without accountability to herself, it would just be her researching in her room. She is also trying to record further ahead so editing is not crammed and rushed.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Research can expand forever; a commitment to ship forces synthesis.
- **How to apply:** Set yourself a publishing commitment; record ahead of deadlines to leave time for editing.
- **Examples they give:** The joke about "an essay where it's just research" and you never write it.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:00:00]-[0:01:01]

### Make hyper-specific things around your obsessions; people will meet you there

- **What they say:** Passion projects with hyper-specificity inspire even people who do not share the obsession. Get away from the thought that what we produce needs to be a shared obsession. Talk about and create around what you care about, nurture those obsessions, and "people will meet you there". They will be enthralled because they may not have had that sense of obsession themselves for a long time.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It is inspiring to see someone care deeply about something; the depth of care, not the topic, draws people in.
- **How to apply:** Pick topics by your own obsession, not by assumed shared interest; show the time and energy you have poured in.
- **Examples they give:** An archivist site of 1990s Japanese fashion magazines (Milk Bar) she had never thought about but found fascinating; The Pudding's visual essays; a couple's fan site and podcast for "Stop! Or My Mom Will Shoot".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:29:57]-[0:30:59]

### Listen to your own offhand "I want to learn about..." remarks

- **What they say:** If you have not found a rabbit hole yet, listen to yourself when you say things flippantly like "I want to learn about X" or "I wish I knew more about XYZ". Train yourself to take yourself seriously enough to go and do it.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** People throw these remarks out constantly; they are latent curiosities waiting to be acted on.
- **How to apply:** Catch those phrases in your own speech and act on them.
- **Examples they give:** Her friends saying this all the time.
- **Applies to:** ideation
- **Timestamp:** [0:30:59]-[0:31:30]

## Frameworks and templates

**Anna's rabbit hole research workflow**

1. Start with a general curiosity: a question, a general idea, or a piece of someone else's content that has stuck in your head.
2. Create an Are.na channel for it. If the curiosity came from a piece of content, put that in first.
3. Go back through content you have already come across that you suspect ties back to the question and dump it all in (images, links, PDFs, videos, notes).
4. Connect items across channels to surface links between topics.
5. When you meet references you do not understand, find them and add them to the channel.
6. Later, sit down in Obsidian (digital garden) to dig into the sources and take notes.
7. Synthesise into a finished piece (for her, a roughly 30-minute episode), accepting this stage is stressful.

**Qualities of research (from Selene Gian's "Research as Leisure Activity")**

1. Begins with a desire to ask and answer questions, contributing to human knowledge and culture; usually a hypothesis, question or avenue of inquiry.
2. Requires commitment to evidence, broadly defined.
3. Requires understanding the history, theory and practices of the disciplines you work within.
4. Is about advancing new arguments and ideas in some form (a presentation, paper, book, etc.).
5. Ideally has contemporary relevance but should not be exclusively devoted to contemporary concerns.
6. Is strengthened by a social and intellectual community. Distinction: research as leisure is directed by passions and instincts and is "exuberantly undisciplined or anti-disciplinary".

**Project of one's own (Paul Graham, as she summarises it)**

- Done voluntarily.
- Done by yourself, or you own one portion of a larger group project.

## Where this source disagrees with common advice or is nuanced

- **Niching down:** she rejects the common "pick a niche, have an elevator pitch" advice as a box that makes you sound like an AI summary, but concedes what it gets right: universal messages are weak and specificity matters. She also finds niching more useful for selling products and services than for content. [0:04:07], [0:06:10]
- **Specificity over relatability:** common advice says to create content your audience already cares about; she argues hyper-specific obsessions work even when viewers do not share them, because the depth of care is what draws people in. [0:29:57]-[0:30:59]
- **Anti-trend:** she frames chasing trends and being palatable as the opposite of what makes people captivating, and praises The Pudding for not chasing current events or clickbait. However, the research criteria she cites say research "ideally has contemporary relevance", just not exclusively. [0:08:14], [0:18:33], [0:27:55]
- **Hobbies versus projects:** she departs from "do what is fun": projects are about play and curiosity, but fun is not the objective, and frustration is part of it. [0:21:41]-[0:25:19]
- **Work-life balance:** via Paul Graham, she questions the idea that you should do no work after work; working on a project of your own is enriching. [0:23:15]
- **Content as a by-product:** although she links curiosity to impactful content, she insists rabbit holes should not be pursued only for content. [0:12:20]
- **Being confusing as an asset:** against the "be instantly understandable" logic of fast-moving feeds, she argues being a little elusive and hard to figure out is magnetic. She acknowledges the reason for the common advice: the internet moves fast and people want to catch attention quickly. [0:04:38], [0:08:14]
- **Scope note:** the transcript contains no advice on hooks, the first seconds of a video, post formats or captions specifically; its value for the playbook is in ideation, specificity and the substance behind content.
