# 10,000 Hours of Studying the Best Viral Hooks in 51 Minutes: Mino Lee

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t3OGy0oMFZc

## Core thesis (2-4 sentences)

Every viral short-form hook, whatever the style (storytelling, educational, POV, challenge, yapping), follows the same repeated five-part formula: a proven idea with a novelty multiplier, an extreme or emphasised word, a second sentence that confirms the hook, a specific example/story/proof, and a re-hook that keeps a loop open. The goal is to make people "irresistibly attracted" to your words in the first 3 seconds, then keep them by not betraying that hook and not closing the loop early. Underneath it all, the viewer is a sceptical stranger giving you their time, so the hook is an offer: raise its perceived value, earn trust fast, handle objections, and write to one precisely understood ideal viewer.

## Principles and techniques

### Pick the content "stencil" by goal

- **What they say:** Videos are not all unique; they bucket into styles depending on the outcome you want. Five stencils: storytelling, educational, POV, challenge, yapping. Decide the goal first (woo with emotion and story, teach directly, or let people live vicariously through your life), then pick the formula. Regardless of stencil, the same five-part hook formula applies.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The goal determines the formula; the underlying hook pattern is constant.
- **How to apply:** Ask "what is the goal of this video?" and choose the matching stencil, then run it through the five-part formula.
- **Examples they give:** The five stencils listed above.
- **Applies to:** short video (principle transfers to all)
- **Timestamp:** [0:00:32], [0:01:34]

### Hook = proven idea + novelty multiplier

- **What they say:** Don't wait for a brilliant original idea. Find what is already going viral and use it as a "stencil or template" (the proven idea), then add your own novel spin (the novelty multiplier). Don't copy word for word.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** What others are going viral with shows what demand exists in the market. Your own life and stories give you the novelty "baked in".
- **How to apply:** Take the structure of a proven hook, template it with blanks, fill in your own situation. Example template he extracted: "I can't [blank], so today I'm [blank]".
- **Examples they give:** Brad Chetwyn's 9.9M-view hook ("I want to propose... day five of DoorDash for a diamond ring") became Mino's 1.8M-view "I can't afford to pay rent for me and my girlfriend... I started to just resent her for it."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:01:02], [0:07:46], [0:08:49]

### Research proven concepts with two search methods

- **What they say:** (1) Search your niche keyword on YouTube/TikTok and see which concepts and packaging are winning. (2) Search the keyword on TikTok specifically to see which creators are "popping off" right now, and study them (including small performative touches, such as a creator always wearing the same wired earbuds to look sophisticated).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It reveals market demand and current winning creators to use as stencils.
- **How to apply:** Search the keyword, note recurring concepts/titles, find the currently hot creator, study their hooks and positioning.
- **Examples they give:** "magnetic personal brand", "how to build a personal brand full course", "Fame is not found, it's created".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:02:05], [0:02:35]

### Novel spin through disagreement

- **What they say:** The "deeper question" is: what is everyone else saying that you disagree with? Lead with that contrast.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It helps you go viral "so much quicker" than straight copying.
- **How to apply:** Identify what the niche's common voice gets wrong and position against it in the first line.
- **Examples they give:** Everyone on YouTube talks conceptually and studies famous people without having grown accounts, so he leads with "This is lived experience" (he has grown many accounts).
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:03:05], [0:03:36]

### Positioning to a relatable desire or pain point (no expensive setup needed)

- **What they say:** Normal people with an iPhone can still go viral; it's about positioning yourself to match the relatable desire or pain point your ideal viewer has.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Relatability, not production, is what hits.
- **How to apply:** Frame the hook around your viewer's desire or pain.
- **Examples they give:** He made all his hooks on his iPhone.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:03:05]

### Extreme or emphasised word (target strong, painful emotions)

- **What they say:** Use an extreme or emphasised word meant to provoke shock, curiosity, anger or fear. The most shared content is the content that provokes the most emotion, not the most intellectually stimulating. The strongest emotions to target are the painful ones (fear, anger). Negative hooks work.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Emotion drives sharing; same concept, far more shock/curiosity/anger/fear.
- **How to apply:** Rewrite a neutral promise as an extreme, often negative version of the same idea.
- **Examples they give:** "Here's how to make 10K a month" vs "how to make sure you never become rich... and your bloodline starves."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:04:07], [0:04:39]

### Confirm the hook in the second sentence

- **What they say:** "Most people get this wrong." People copy hooks but miss what the creator does in the second sentence: confirm that what the hook said is actually what the video is about. Make it "abundantly clear in the first 5 seconds", ideally in the second sentence, and the more directly related the better. Small callbacks to the first sentence throughout reinforce it.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Otherwise it's clickbait: you hook them in 3 seconds and on second four they realise you're full of it, you lose trust, and next time on the For You page they trust you less. Viewers "can sniff it out" when a video is misleading them, which hurts performance.
- **How to apply:** Sentence two restates or directly extends the hook's topic (e.g. hook about rent, sentence two about touring apartments and insisting on paying rent). For text hooks, make the spoken words match the on-screen text.
- **Examples they give:** "For the entire past month of us touring apartments..." confirming the rent hook; Shelby Sapp's text hook "Six sales objections in 60 seconds" confirmed by "I'm going to roll six objections in the next 60 seconds."
- **Applies to:** short video (principle applies to all)
- **Timestamp:** [0:04:39], [0:05:09], [0:05:41], [0:10:56], [0:11:27], [0:41:55]

### Specific example, story or proof (numbers, names, visual proof)

- **What they say:** The best way to get two or three times as many views is to inject a specific number, a specific famous person or name, or visual tangible proof on screen.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It leans into familiarity bias. Specifics give viewers concepts to latch onto so they can relate and compare their own situation (and they comment about them).
- **How to apply:** Add exact prices, places, names, times; show proof on screen.
- **Examples they give:** "$5,600 a month" plus "New York City" in the rent video; commenters compared their own rent and city.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:05:41], [0:06:13], [0:10:24], [0:10:56]

### Re-hook: keep the loop open

- **What they say:** After the hook, add a re-hook that keeps a loop open so the viewer stays in suspense and desire to know the answer. (Credits the concept to YouTuber Luke Bolsh.) In storytelling, the re-hook signals conflict that only resolves at the end.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Stops people leaving at 10 seconds of a 60-second video; they want to watch to the end.
- **How to apply:** After the setup, introduce the complication/conflict; hold the resolution/lesson until the end.
- **Examples they give:** "The only problem was that it was $5,700 a month... my heart immediately sank."
- **Applies to:** short video (also written storytelling)
- **Timestamp:** [0:06:13], [0:06:43], [0:11:58], [0:12:29]

### Don't lead with the lesson (that closes the loop)

- **What they say:** If you open with the takeaway ("Here's what I learned"), you close the loop and you're educating, not storytelling. That's fine, but it's not storytelling.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Open loop (can't afford) plus immediate lesson (it's motivation) resolves the tension instantly.
- **How to apply:** Keep the lesson for the end; the opening states the problem, not the resolution.
- **Examples they give:** Bad: "I can't afford to pay rent... but I realise it's motivation to work hard."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:12:29], [0:13:00]

### Storytelling is dragging out the moment

- **What they say:** Take one moment you could summarise in one sentence and drag it out over several (he used seven), walking moment by moment. If 80% of the video is teaching, it's an educational video.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A one-line summary is barely a story, just context for a lesson.
- **How to apply:** Expand the key moment beat by beat (heard the number, heart sank...).
- **Examples they give:** The leasing agent telling him the price.
- **Applies to:** short video, written posts
- **Timestamp:** [0:13:00], [0:13:32]

### Reveal the uncomfortable details

- **What they say:** If you don't feel uncomfortable, or that people will judge you for what you reveal, you're probably hiding things. Leaving out details that protect your ego leaves nothing for people to care about. Honest personal content invites strong opinions (and comments).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The emotion-provoking details are the ones you're tempted to omit.
- **How to apply:** Check each draft: what detail am I leaving out to protect myself? Put it in.
- **Examples they give:** The rent video drew comments like "This is a huge ick", which he expected.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:09:21], [0:09:53]

### Educational content: specific and differentiated hook

- **What they say:** Generic lines ("here's how to level up in life", "three lessons that changed my life") are broad words said before. If your words sound like something said "two scrolls ago", you're scrolled past instantly. Say what makes your thing different.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Crowded niches (fitness, self-improvement) repeat the same information; differentiation earns attention.
- **How to apply:** Scroll your niche for common keywords, then find a differentiated but top-of-mind recognisable way to say the same thing.
- **Examples they give:** "10K per month" sounds like a scam; he used "I just barely touched f*** you money as a 21-year-old."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:15:35], [0:16:07], [0:16:37], [0:17:09]

### Own a keyword

- **What they say:** One layer below "niche" is keywords. Building a personal brand is figuring out which words you own. Pick words that make your story different and use them repeatedly.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** If ten other people haven't said it that day, you can be the one who owns the word.
- **How to apply:** Identify distinctive recurring elements of your story and make them your keywords.
- **Examples they give:** "dropped out", "Korean mom".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:17:09], [0:17:40]

### The hook is an offer: Hormozi's value equation

- **What they say:** Every hook makes an offer to the viewer in exchange for their time. Raise perceived value with four levers: dream outcome, perceived likelihood of achievement, time delay, effort and sacrifice.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Viewers trade time for value; make the offer clear and high-value.
- **How to apply:** Check each hook against the four levers (below).
- **Examples they give:** See following entries.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:17:40], [0:18:12]

### Lever 1: extreme, unique dream outcome

- **What they say:** Make the dream outcome extreme and feel unique.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** When something feels unique we perceive it as higher value.
- **How to apply:** Push the outcome further and make it distinctive.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond earlier extreme-wording examples.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:18:12]

### Lever 2: perceived likelihood of achievement (trust, proof, relatability)

- **What they say:** Perceived likelihood equals trust. On the For You page you are a stranger, so you must appeal to strangers. Use believable language, visual proof, social proof, and make credentials clear in the first sentence. Screenshots, receipts, or your setting/background can inspire trust. Relatability also raises perceived likelihood because it feels attainable.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Hormozi drops portfolio revenue in the first 10 to 20 seconds and those are his best performers.
- **How to apply:** State proof in sentence one; show receipts or a trust-building setting; make yourself relatable.
- **Examples they give:** "I've grown a bunch of accounts"; a fitness creator showing their physique while talking.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:18:43], [0:19:13], [0:19:44], [0:41:44]

### Lever 3: time delay

- **What they say:** State how long it takes and keep it short. Stating the length sets expectations so people aren't caught off guard by a long video; a high value-per-second ratio attracts.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** "Six objections in 60 seconds" is 10 seconds per objection; 600 seconds would feel low value.
- **How to apply:** Put a short time frame or duration in the hook ("in the next 60 seconds", "in X minutes").
- **Examples they give:** "Everything I learned in marketing in 59 minutes"; "6 sales objections in 60 seconds".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:20:15], [0:20:46]

### Lever 4: reduce effort and sacrifice (read their pain points aloud)

- **What they say:** Lower perceived effort through your copywriting. Read the viewer's exact pain points back to them in the first sentence so they feel heard; if you understand their pain better than they can articulate it, they trust you and watch. Framing like "the lazy method to..." signals low effort.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Feeling heard creates unique trust ("I'm bought in") and makes the change feel less hard.
- **How to apply:** Open with a description of their state ("Maybe you're a little demotivated... you keep procrastinating"), then the solution.
- **Examples they give:** "Here's how to get out of a slump... The solution that's always worked for me is monk mode"; "This is the lazy method to get rich/lock in/discipline".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:20:46], [0:21:16], [0:21:47], [0:22:17]

### Objection handling: "I know you're probably thinking"

- **What they say:** Called one of the most powerful sentences, able to "10x your views overnight". When you tell someone to do something, they resist ("Don't tell me what to do", "he doesn't understand me"). In a video they won't voice objections, they just scroll, so pre-empt: "Now, I know you're probably thinking..." and insert the exact thought they're about to have.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Resistance to being told what to do and to change is universal. Voicing their objection builds rapport and makes people feel heard ("this person has lived my life"). He credits this line with an 800k-like video that brought ~30,000 followers.
- **How to apply:** After giving the instruction, intuit your ideal viewer's instinctive pushback and say it for them, optionally reinforced visually (he used an "Asian mom" meme/apron).
- **Examples they give:** "Stop using your phone for the first hour of the day. Now, I know you're probably thinking, 'What are you, my mom?'"
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:22:48], [0:23:18], [0:23:49], [0:24:19], [0:24:49]

### Address why they haven't applied known information

- **What they say:** Almost all information already exists for free; the problem is people haven't applied it. Ask what excuses and narratives stop them applying it, and speak to those.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Viewers doubt strangers and resent advice; understanding beats new information.
- **How to apply:** List the excuses your viewer uses and address them in the script.
- **Examples they give:** Everyone knows the gym is useful, yet 80% don't work out regularly.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:14:03], [0:25:21], [0:25:53]

### POV hook: open with a real clip of an interesting moment

- **What they say:** Use a POV hook when you've filmed a specific moment and the audio itself is useful: an interaction with another person, a celebration, or a relatable/funny moment. It works as a modifier: attach the clip at the start, then start narrating or talking head.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A story on its own may lack pull; a shocking or relatable clip first grabs attention ("combine a viral Twitch clip with a story time video"). He says the format has generated over 10M views.
- **How to apply:** Film real interesting moments; lead with the clip; play up the emotion (he deliberately over-celebrates); use relatable keywords; add editing touches like a beat drop.
- **Examples they give:** Filming his mum reading his Boston College expulsion letter; "POV your app made its first dollar without knowing how to code"; "2:00 a.m. lock in".
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:26:24], [0:26:56], [0:27:28], [0:27:59], [0:28:31], [0:29:02]

### Challenge hook: "Is it possible to [outcome] in [condensed time frame/constraint]?"

- **What they say:** His best-performing format (12M views on one video; ~100,000 followers cumulatively). It creates a binary outcome the viewer waits to see resolved. It needs: (1) a relatable desire or pain point (a question the viewer has actually asked themselves), (2) perceived struggle and real stakes (something to lose if you fail), and a genuinely binary outcome where failure is possible. Don't reveal the payoff in the first 3 seconds.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Hormozi's copy ("Is it possible to sell $100 million of books and break the Guinness record") got 3,000 likes because nobody has asked that question and he revealed the payoff immediately, so there was no suspense (like Avengers: Endgame opening with "We stopped Thanos").
- **How to apply:** Choose an outcome your viewer has wondered about; add a constraint; add relatable identity ("broke college student"); establish why it matters and the stakes early; show struggle; withhold the result.
- **Examples they give:** "Is it possible to ace a midterm after studying for just 1 hour?"; "Is it possible for a broke college student to make $100,000 a month?"
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:29:32], [0:30:04], [0:31:38], [0:32:40], [0:33:11], [0:33:42]

### Ground big goals in the why and emotion, not logistics

- **What they say:** Before going into events, cover why it matters. Nobody cares about your business plan; a plan only matters in the context of an emotion-provoking why. Relate a big numerical goal to a relationship. Avoid explaining logistics; anything explained must be understandable to a 5-year-old.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Viewers relate to sheer desire and emotion (wanting acceptance, approval from a loved one), not the goal itself. Stakes also offset the low believability of a big number, which creates "scroll risk".
- **How to apply:** Early line stating the personal stakes; cut logistics details that don't serve the struggle.
- **Examples they give:** "1 year ago, I promised my mom I'd hit this crazy goal if she let me drop out"; "against all Asian parent will"; "My mom gave me one last chance."
- **Applies to:** short video, written posts
- **Timestamp:** [0:34:12], [0:34:43], [0:36:17], [0:36:47], [0:37:18], [0:37:49], [0:39:51]

### Match the hook to the video's real purpose

- **What they say:** If the video is really teaching, use an educational hook rather than a challenge hook. Hormozi could have led with "a complete breakdown on how I broke a Guinness World Record".
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A challenge hook promises suspense; if the content teaches, the promise mismatches.
- **How to apply:** Suspense content gets challenge/story hooks; teaching content gets educational hooks.
- **Examples they give:** As above.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:37:18]

### Show, don't tell, to create proof

- **What they say:** Instead of "here's my three-step framework", showing real stories and real-life moments can be the most powerful proof lever. This requires putting something on the line, e.g. publicly documenting an embarrassing goal.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Buying from or following you requires an immense level of proof and relationship.
- **How to apply:** Document real attempts with real stakes.
- **Examples they give:** Publicly documenting his attempt to make 100K a month.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:38:50], [0:39:21], [0:39:51]

### Visual hooks through creative editing

- **What they say:** You can also catch attention through visual hooks and creative editing; B-roll can support confirmation of the hook.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Not elaborated beyond catching attention.
- **How to apply:** Add standout visual moments/edits (he spent ~20 hours editing one recap video).
- **Examples they give:** A visual effect on "to try and make a hundred thousand dollars a month"; B-roll of apartment touring.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:10:56], [0:38:19]

### Yapping hook: tonality over words

- **What they say:** Yapping (unscripted talking to camera, minimal editing) is high leverage: videos in about 5 minutes that can get millions of views. The words need not be emotional; emotion comes from tonality. Emphasise a word in every sentence, use hand gestures, "eyebrow maxing"/"zest maxing", subtle camera movement. Unlearn nonchalant monotone.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Yapping is "content on hard mode" without B-roll to mask a flat personality; getting good at it makes you a better storyteller and educator.
- **How to apply:** Practise emphasis, gestures and expressive delivery even though it feels weird; try different things to find your voice.
- **Examples they give:** Shelby Sapp, "I'm going to roll six objections in the next 60 seconds" (3.8M views).
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:40:21], [0:41:24], [0:42:26], [0:42:57], [0:43:28]

### Talk to one friend who needs to hear it

- **What they say:** Imagine you're FaceTiming a friend who desperately needs this information, or a younger version of yourself. It removes the "camera voice" and makes you someone's comfort creator.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Conviction and energy come from knowing the person needs it; it also overcomes feeling unqualified.
- **How to apply:** Close your eyes for 10 seconds before recording and picture that person.
- **Examples they give:** His best content was talking to himself of a year earlier.
- **Applies to:** short video (framing applies to all)
- **Timestamp:** [0:44:30], [0:45:00], [0:45:31]

### Setting, lighting and outfit signal credibility

- **What they say:** Always have good front-facing lighting. Background, setting (e.g. a car) and outfits subconsciously signal wealth or that someone is worth listening to, especially in business/finance.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Viewers make subconscious judgements about whether you're qualified.
- **How to apply:** Front-light yourself; choose settings/outfits that fit the credibility you need.
- **Examples they give:** Shelby Sapp filming in cars; his ring light.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:19:13], [0:45:31], [0:46:03]

### Ideal viewer persona: one person, in granular detail

- **What they say:** Write down in the most granular detail the single person you speak to in every video: exact pain points, deep-down desires, how they think subconsciously. His is his lost freshman-year self. Content needs to help one person; if it genuinely does, in words only your lived experience can give, it reaches many.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It tells you the exact language that makes the viewer feel heard; deep resonance spreads to 10,000 or 100,000 people and builds die-hard fans.
- **How to apply:** Keep a document of the persona; put their exact fears into hooks.
- **Examples they give:** "If you want to start creating content, but you're afraid people are going to judge you... don't worry. They will." (300,000 likes, unscripted).
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:46:34], [0:47:04], [0:47:35], [0:48:06], [0:49:08], [0:49:40]

### State the viewer's worst fear in the hook

- **What they say:** If your viewer fears something specific (e.g. friends sending their video to group chats calling it cringe), say it in the hook.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Articulating an exact felt pain point makes people comment "I feel this"; his video got 300,000 likes from that alone.
- **How to apply:** Pull the fear verbatim from your persona doc and open with it.
- **Examples they give:** As above.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:49:08], [0:49:40], [0:50:11]

## Frameworks and templates

**The five-part "Krabby Patty" hook formula** (applies to every stencil) [0:01:02]-[0:06:43]:

1. Proven idea + novelty multiplier (copy a winning structure, add your own spin; ideally a point you disagree with the niche on).
2. Extreme or emphasised word (provoke shock, curiosity, anger or fear; negative framing is fine).
3. Confirm the hook in the second sentence (within the first 5 seconds).
4. Specific example, story or proof (number, famous name, or visual proof).
5. Re-hook (open a loop or conflict that only resolves at the end).

**Storytelling hook template** [0:08:18]: "I can't [blank], so today I'm [blank]" / "I can't [afford X], so instead of [reasonable action], I [surprising reaction]."

**Educational script template** [0:15:04]-[0:15:35], [0:22:48]:

1. "Here's how to [specific, differentiated outcome]."
2. Read the viewer's pain points back ("Maybe you're... and you keep...").
3. "The solution that's always worked for me is [X]."
4. "Now, I know you're probably thinking [their objection]."

**Value equation check for hooks (Hormozi)** [0:18:12]-[0:22:17]: dream outcome (extreme, unique); perceived likelihood (believable language, visual/social proof, relatability, credentials in sentence one); time delay (short, stated duration); effort and sacrifice (pain points read back, "the lazy method to...").

**Challenge hook** [0:29:32]: "Is it possible to [outcome] in [condensed time frame or constraint]?" Requirements: relatable desire/pain point the viewer has wondered about; perceived struggle and real stakes; binary outcome with real chance of failure; payoff withheld.

**POV hook** [0:26:24]-[0:29:02]: "POV [relatable or celebratory moment]" clip first, then narration/talking head.

**Yapping hook** [0:41:24]: plain spoken promise confirmed immediately, with a time delay ("six objections in the next 60 seconds"), delivered with emphasis on a word per sentence and gestures.

## Things they say NOT to do

- Don't copy a hook word for word, especially someone else's story. [0:08:49]
- Don't use a hook unrelated to the video, or follow a flashy hook with a bland second sentence (clickbait costs trust). [0:05:09]
- Don't use generic, broad phrasing that sounds like something said two scrolls ago. [0:16:07]
- Don't use overused, scammy-sounding keywords like "10K per month". [0:16:37]
- Don't open with the lesson or takeaway in a story; it closes the loop. [0:13:00]
- Don't summarise the key moment in one sentence. [0:13:32]
- Don't reveal the payoff in the first 3 seconds of a challenge video. [0:33:11]
- Don't build a challenge around a question nobody has asked themselves. [0:31:38]
- Don't explain logistics or business plans without an emotional why. [0:34:12], [0:36:47]
- Don't talk in a nonchalant monotone on camera. [0:42:57]
- Don't just tell people what to do without handling their resistance. [0:23:18]
- Don't try to appeal to everybody. [0:47:35]

## Where this source disagrees with common advice or is nuanced

- **Copying is encouraged, with a twist:** rather than originality, he advocates templating proven viral hooks and adding your own spin; the novelty comes from your life. [0:01:02], [0:08:49]
- **Negative emotion outperforms:** fear and anger are called the strongest emotions to target, and negative hooks are recommended. [0:04:07]
