# The Future of Social Media (Do This Before 2027): Alex Garcia, interviewed by Grant Owen (The Grant Owen Podcast)

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jAEtl9mJZtM

## Core thesis (2-4 sentences)

Making content that works is a skill built through reps, so you start with a weekly testing plan rather than a strategy, then become a "ferocious tester and a ferocious analyst" who reads skip rate, like-to-share ratio, retention and average watch time to find micro wins and scale them. The concept and its packaging (often borrowed from a different category or platform) matter more than budget, and craft details (angle, action, aesthetic, composition) can turn a boring subject into something people stop for. Organic social content is the nucleus of the best brands being built today: winning organic pieces should feed ads, emails and landing pages, and brands are heading towards operating like entertainment studios or TV networks with dedicated accounts and repeatable concepts.

Note: the interview is structured by budget tier ($0, $5k, $10k, $50k, $100k per month). Much of the hiring advice at the higher tiers is outside a two-person company's reach, but it is captured below because it contains principles (setting the bar, taste matching, narrative building, dedicated segment accounts) that still apply.

## Principles and techniques

### Don't start with a strategy; start with reps

- **What they say:** The first thing to avoid is sitting down and building a strategy; that is "the last thing that you should do". Content is a skill like shooting free throws: you don't plan foot angles, you start shooting and get as many reps as possible.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Creating content that works on the internet is a skill set, learned by doing rather than planning.
- **How to apply:** Replace up-front strategy with a testing strategy (see next principle). Get volume out.
- **Examples they give:** The free-throw analogy.
- **Applies to:** strategy / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:00:00] to [0:01:02]

### Build a one-day weekly testing plan

- **What they say:** Be "a ferocious tester and a ferocious analyst". Map out everything going out this week (it can be done in a day). Look at what is working on other platforms and in other categories, bring it over to your topic, and test.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Testing different ideas, concepts, formats and packaging is how you find "micro wins" to build on.
- **How to apply:** Each week: list the week's posts; for each, vary one of content idea, concept, format or packaging; keep testing until micro wins appear.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond the category-borrowing examples below.
- **Applies to:** strategy / short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:01:02] to [0:01:33]

### Borrow a format that already works in a different category

- **What they say:** You don't need to be extremely creative. Take an angle that works elsewhere and position your idea in it. "Format and packaging" is how you take an idea and position it in a way that is already working on a completely different channel.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The contrast is what makes it interesting; the format is already proven with audiences.
- **How to apply:** Find a proven format in another niche (for example cooking's "$1 vs $10" comparisons) and map your subject onto it.
- **Examples they give:** For a CPA: a $100 online accountant versus a $1,000-an-hour accountant, the cheap one "just going to get my [stuff] done" versus the expensive one who might make you money back. Cooking does $1 vs $10, $100 vs $1,000 comparisons all the time.
- **Applies to:** short video / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:01:33] to [0:03:05]

### Skip rate is your hook score: keep it under 50%

- **What they say:** Skip rate is whether your hook is interesting enough to stop the swipe; it is measured at 3 seconds. Aim to always be under 50% (fewer than half swiping away by 3 seconds); smaller is better.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** "Your skip rate is literally just how good is your hook."
- **How to apply:** Check skip rate on every video; treat anything above 50% as a hook problem.
- **Examples they give:** A Sam Parr video (about his dad's onion company doing $10m revenue) that reached about 4 million views still had around 83% of viewers at the 3-second mark.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:03:05] to [0:06:09]

### A hook has three layers: title, visual, verbal

- **What they say:** The hook "has to be your title hook, your visual hook, and your verbal hook". Brands play a different game: about half don't want someone talking (no verbal hook), most don't want a title hook, and many avoid native text because it can read as an ad. When those layers are unavailable you must be the best at visual hooks.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Each layer is a chance to stop the scroll; losing layers puts all the weight on the visual.
- **How to apply:** Plan all three layers for each video; if you can't use text or speech, invest in the visual hook.
- **Examples they give:** None specific.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:03:36] to [0:04:37]

### Visual hooks: angle, action, aesthetic (pattern disruption)

- **What they say:** For a visual hook look at three things: the angle it's shot at, the action, and the aesthetic. Changing the angle is a great way to pattern-disrupt on feed.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Showing something not typically shown in that format disrupts the pattern viewers expect.
- **How to apply:** Ask what the usual shot is for your format, then change the angle (for example overhead instead of straight on), add an action, and consider the setting/aesthetic.
- **Examples they give:** An overhead shot with text on screen on a podcast, which "is not what's typically shown". Later, the lookbook-in-a-classic-car example (see Frameworks).
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:04:37] to [0:05:08]; revisited [0:43:55] to [0:44:25]

### Like-to-share ratio: aim for one share per two likes

- **What they say:** Citing how MrBeast talks analytically: two likes for every one share is "a good ballpark". It's a very strong metric that you have something valuable and sharable.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Shares are the strongest signal to Instagram to show the content to others: "if it's worth sharing privately, it's worth sharing publicly" (Explore feed). Grant adds it indicates relatability.
- **How to apply:** Track shares against likes on each post; treat a 1:2 ratio as a strong result.
- **Examples they give:** MrBeast's analytical framing.
- **Applies to:** short video / strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:06:40] to [0:07:11]

### Be obsessive about analytics; the best creatives are data-obsessed

- **What they say:** Be "legitimately like obsessive and addicted to your analytics", which is not the norm for creators. The best creatives are the ones obsessed with the data.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Data tells you where you actually win; MrBeast talks from an analytical rather than creative perspective.
- **How to apply:** Review skip rate, like-to-share ratio, retention graph and average watch time for every post.
- **Examples they give:** MrBeast.
- **Applies to:** strategy / short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:06:09] to [0:06:40]

### Read the retention graph for storytelling drop-offs

- **What they say:** The retention graph measures your hook but also your ability to tell stories and keep someone engaged; look for patterns like "why does everyone always drop off at the 8-second mark?"
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Consistent drop-off points reveal where the story loses people.
- **How to apply:** Compare retention graphs across videos; find recurring drop-off seconds and fix what happens at that moment.
- **Examples they give:** The 8-second drop-off question.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:07:11] to [0:07:42]

### The 12-second trap in average watch time

- **What they say:** "12 second average watch time is usually like where good videos die." Watch the average watch time over the first hours: if it climbs (12 to 13 to 14, up to 22-24 seconds) the video can pop; if it stays at 12 it's a good video but not a 100k-plus one; if it regresses (12 to 11) "you're basically cooked". Moving from 12 to 15 seconds average watch time can add tens of thousands of followers a month for a brand.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Longer watch time gets you shown to more people. Caveat he gives: this applies to the majority of videos, which sit in the 45-second to one-minute range.
- **How to apply:** Check average watch time over the first few hours after publishing and read the trend, not just the number.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond the numbers.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:07:42] to [0:09:44]

### Plan the first 3 seconds second by second, then in 3-second blocks to 12

- **What they say:** When planning a video, map the first 3 seconds second by second, "because the hook is the most important part. You don't get to 12 seconds if the first three seconds don't matter." Then plan in 3-second increments (3-6, 6-9, 9-12) to avoid the 12-second trap.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Hook first, then sustained retention past the point where good videos die.
- **How to apply:** See the second-by-second planning framework below.
- **Examples they give:** Their internal planning process.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:08:43] to [0:09:44]

### One-shot videos as the no-budget format

- **What they say:** With no budget, test one-shot videos: phone on a tripod, step back, do some kind of action, text on screen. "A single scene where I do some kind of action and I put text on the video." There are levels to it, but you're not in the territory of beautifully locked-off vignettes.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Cheap, fast, and testable.
- **How to apply:** One scene, one action, one line of on-screen text.
- **Examples they give:** Contrast with About Blank's polished cafe vignettes, which is not where a no-budget creator plays.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:10:45] to [0:11:48]

### Use short B-roll + text reels as a topic-testing mechanism

- **What they say:** He is not against the ~8-second reel (text on screen, rest in caption, B-roll), which many in his space hate. It is "a testing mechanism for anything else". If it pops, the text did the heavy lifting, which is a data point that people are interested in the topic.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It isolates the idea: if a plain B-roll plus text gets 250,000 views, the topic itself is proven.
- **How to apply:** Before investing in a bigger video, post the idea as B-roll plus one line of text. If it performs, scale the idea into a series, carousel, green screen video or "yap" (talking) video.
- **Examples they give:** Text on screen: "the biggest marketing prediction of 2026 is launching a social show and brands should do it now". Graza olive oil: B-roll of a glass bottle shattering with text about a 2-year-old grabbing the olive oil; if it pops, it reveals an ad angle targeting mothers.
- **Applies to:** short video / captions / strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:11:48] to [0:13:52]

### Concept matters more than money

- **What they say:** "The concept is so much more important." You don't need money to do the B-roll-and-text test, but you do need to be creative.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A strong concept travels regardless of production value.
- **How to apply:** Spend effort on the concept and the line of text rather than on production.
- **Examples they give:** Graza example.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:13:52]

### Start from relatable scenarios and your own pain points

- **What they say:** Start with scenarios and stories that are relatable and things you have dealt with (if you have kids, use that). Most people start brands because of a specific pain point, which makes it easy to talk from your POV.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Lived experience is easy to speak to authentically and relatable to others with the same pain.
- **How to apply:** List the pain points and everyday situations behind why you started; turn each into a content idea.
- **Examples they give:** His CPG brand, started because of a pain point he experiences.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:14:22] to [0:15:25]

### Find the story in each day (reframe the mundane as stakes)

- **What they say:** "Some of the best creators are the ones that are able to find the story within each day." A day of eight meetings looks like nothing, but reframed around the struggle it becomes a story people follow to see if you make it.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Framing adds stakes and a question ("is he gonna make it?") that turns the most boring day interesting.
- **How to apply:** Look at what you did today, find the obstacle or tension in it, and frame it with a goal, a day count and the stakes.
- **Examples they give:** "Day three of trying to build my CPG brand to a hundred million", can't land a manufacturer, all want $250,000, "I can barely afford to get my kid into daycare". Ryan Trahan staying at a one-star hotel to check bad reviews, a zero-cost concept; also Casey Neistat.
- **Applies to:** short video / written posts
- **Timestamp:** [0:15:25] to [0:16:59]

### Overcome camera fear: everyone's first video sucked

- **What they say:** Your favourite creators all had to make a first video, and those probably sucked. Failing at a video hurts far less than being cut from a team: "all you have to do is archive".
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Even people with every resource shoot themselves imperfectly; the cost of a bad video is tiny.
- **How to apply:** Post anyway; archive what doesn't work.
- **Examples they give:** Dwyane Wade, worth hundreds of millions, filming his own vlog on a $400-500 Osmo Pocket at an event where crews had FX6s and REDs. Kobe Bryant and Michael Jordan getting cut from teams.
- **Applies to:** short video / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:16:59] to [0:19:35]

### Hand over the playbook when you bring someone in

- **What they say:** If you've been watching your analytics, you have a mental playbook of what works. When you hire, your job is to hand that over and theirs is to make it better.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The founder holds the data; the hire brings skill the founder lacks.
- **How to apply:** Document what has worked, what hasn't and what you think could work; ask the new person to make it 10x better.
- **Examples they give:** The Vintage Modern (formerly Vintage Broncos) founder, who made everything the page ever posted, then needed to 10x output and passed on his knowledge.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:24:16] to [0:26:51]

### Composition as a hook; captions as hooks

- **What they say:** Most brands won't be doing talking-head videos, so you have to understand composition and how to use it as a hook (for example the rule of thirds). With better kit you can add variation (gimbal, more angles), and "start leveraging captions even as hooks".
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** One small positioning change can make "the whole visual 10 times better".
- **How to apply:** Check framing and where the subject stands before shooting; vary angles beyond straight-on and overhead.
- **Examples they give:** "Can you stand right there when you do your action".
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:26:51] to [0:27:53]

### Every 10 seconds can be a story if it's shot interestingly

- **What they say:** "Every 10 seconds can be a story if it's shot interesting." He doesn't stage things purely for a shot; ordinary moments (shopping for magazines, browsing a shop) become compelling through framing.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Shot well, "it looks like we're doing the coolest thing in the world" even when you're just talking.
- **How to apply:** Film real moments of your normal life/work, and frame them deliberately (foreground, repetition in frame, high angles).
- **Examples they give:** A shot from outside a magazine shop with the right side of frame full of magazines; an overhead stair shot in a clothing shop with colourful shelves behind.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:29:58] to [0:32:34]

### Keep a quality standard; don't post what doesn't meet it

- **What they say:** He didn't post any of 20-plus vignettes from the Masters because they didn't meet his standard, despite the social proof value. Grant takes away that his own standards were too low.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The brand should be represented as pristinely as possible; the standard defines the brand.
- **How to apply:** Set a visual bar and hold it, even when the moment is valuable.
- **Examples they give:** The Masters vignettes versus the New York ones, which "all hit the standard".
- **Applies to:** strategy / short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:32:34] to [0:34:05]

### Content to get followers vs content for your followers

- **What they say:** There's content to get followers (top of funnel, made to go viral) and content for your followers (made to get 20,000 views, for the fan base). Many people stay only in the first world. Fan-base content shows who you are beyond your topic.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It deepens investment in you; people said they felt "invited" and like they were there.
- **How to apply:** Judge fan content by comments, not views: is the same person who comments on top-of-funnel videos commenting here, and are they getting more invested? When people start joking with you, "we're going deeper" with that audience.
- **Examples they give:** His New York content showing his wife, daughter, shopping and travel, to people who only know him from green screen content talk; commenters calling him "posture king". The Malbon Home Masters activation.
- **Applies to:** strategy / all
- **Timestamp:** [0:34:05] to [0:36:41]

### Once you have wins, scale output without dropping quality

- **What they say:** At the $10k tier the job is scaling: if you have a video that worked, "we need to do this 10 times this month" at the same quality. If a series crushes weekly, publish twice a week. Volume becomes the moat and a competitive advantage (for example 15 to 25 posts a month).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Increasing output of proven formats beats marginally improving aesthetics at that stage.
- **How to apply:** Identify proven formats/series and increase their frequency; "Don't let the bar drop".
- **Examples they give:** Everyday Better Club (athleticwear) grew by about 110,000 followers in 2-3 months from one-shot videos iterated relentlessly.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:37:43] to [0:38:44]; [0:48:01] to [0:50:06]

### The 80/20 and 3% twist rule for iteration

- **What they say:** Spend 80% scaling and improving what works, and keep making small tests and iterations on it. Borrowing Virgil Abloh: you can make anything novel by twisting it 3% (not 30%).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A small, well-chosen change can make a proven concept feel completely different and new.
- **How to apply:** For a proven video, ask "what's a 3% change that makes this concept better?"
- **Examples they give:** Saratoga water: a blue cap is just a changed cap, but a cork top makes it a novel product. For talking heads, start 25 ft away on a 70-200mm lens and crash-zoom in as the hook lands.
- **Applies to:** short video / strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:38:44] to [0:41:19]

### Aesthetic creates demand (TAM), it's not "majoring in the minors"

- **What they say:** Grant challenges whether topic and conviction matter more than creative details. Alex: brands must create TAM through desire, interest and aspiration; the aesthetic and how it's shot matter, and a crash zoom takes an extra five minutes.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** The market for candles is huge but small for a new brand's candles, unless visuals make you feel "my house isn't complete" without it.
- **How to apply:** Treat visual quality as the lever that makes people want what you offer.
- **Examples they give:** The candle example.
- **Applies to:** strategy / short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:40:48] to [0:41:50]

### Look to fashion (and other categories), not your own, for what's next

- **What they say:** To know what will work before it works, pay attention to fashion; it's so competitive it has to be ahead on formats, creativity and concepts. People only look at their own category and try to be 1% better instead of 100% better. Ask "How do I do fashion for my industry?"
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Doing what no one else does in your category makes you stand out (Grant calls it the purple cow theory; Alex agrees).
- **How to apply:** Study fashion, skincare and other categories' formats and transplant them into yours.
- **Examples they give:** Shinzo (clothing brand) lookbook in a classic car. A CPA version: the $100-an-hour person in a raggedy outfit versus the same person in a smarter outfit, arguing both sides, which Grant calls "authority framing".
- **Applies to:** short video / strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:41:50] to [0:45:57]

### Think big inside the box (constraints drive creativity)

- **What they say:** Quoting ex-Nike CMO Greg Hoffman: it's not how big you can think outside the box, but how big you can think inside it. That's how you stretch a small budget into viral content.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Constraints force cheap, clever concepts.
- **How to apply:** Look for simple, emotional, relatable prompts that cost little to stage.
- **Examples they give:** For Malbon Home: a $100 old phone from Etsy, a hidden camera in the bushes, and a prompt to call whoever introduced you to golf and say thank you; people cried and left voicemails. Bushes in the foreground framed it as hidden and beautiful.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:45:57] to [0:47:30]

### Choose repeatable concepts: one concept, many stories

- **What they say:** The phone concept was massively scalable because each shot is the same but each is "a different story each time". They shot 25 in 3 hours.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** One set-up yields many individual pieces of content.
- **How to apply:** Design a fixed format where the variable is a new person/story each time.
- **Examples they give:** 25 phone-call videos in 3 hours; Everyday Better Club running in the snow (ASMR crunching, one locked-off shot, 7-10 seconds, text "you're only as good as your circle"), then iterated to more people and more shots.
- **Applies to:** short video / strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:47:30] to [0:49:36]

### Use Instagram trial reels to test hooks and text

- **What they say:** Everyday Better Club used trial reels to test different hooks and different text on screen, then brought the best ones over and posted those.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Testing before main posting lets you "test and scale ruthlessly".
- **How to apply:** Post variants as trial reels, compare, publish the winners to the main feed.
- **Examples they give:** Everyday Better Club: 80-90 million views in one to two months; "inventory gone".
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:49:04] to [0:50:06]

### Narrative building and world building

- **What they say:** At the higher tier you move from attracting the right people to world building: cohesion across Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and narrative building, "the most important thing" for a brand or personal brand: what narrative do I want to implant in someone's head, and how do I use content to cement it.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It is all about perception: what people say about a brand comes from the content they consume.
- **How to apply:** Decide the narrative you want people to repeat about you; make content consistent with it across platforms.
- **Examples they give:** BPN: what you'd say about BPN is their narrative building.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:50:36] to [0:52:39]

### Set the bar with the first edit

- **What they say:** Internally, their DP always edits the first variation of a video to "set the tone... make it high", before passing to other editors. The second you just hand it off, the bar drops.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Competitive editors then try to beat that bar.
- **How to apply:** The person with the strongest vision makes the first version of a new format; others work from it.
- **Examples they give:** "Yo, Randy, I need you to set the tone."
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:55:14] to [0:56:47]

### Hire for matching taste: ask "what do you consume?"

- **What they say:** A lead creative's taste and vision must match the creative director's. Sam Parr's hiring question: "what do you consume?" If they consume what you do (or put you on to something), you're probably in the same world.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Mismatched worlds (tacky and playful versus luxury) collide.
- **How to apply:** Ask what someone watches and reads to sharpen their skills; compare with your own inputs.
- **Examples they give:** MrBeast's manifesto: make you as much of me as possible.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:53:09] to [0:55:14]

### Dedicated accounts per top customer segment (in-house creator)

- **What they say:** Awareness is becoming an increasingly expensive game. Bring in a creator in-house to run a completely separate account dedicated to your top customer segment. The brand owns the IP; deals have to be lucrative.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A main account serves two to three customer profiles with several pain points each; a dedicated account reaches one segment, lets the growth team build funnels and targeted ads for that segment with content known to work.
- **How to apply:** Pick your top segment; create an account giving nothing but value for them (for example whiteboard sessions or street-style interviews).
- **Examples they give:** Maurten: an account for marathon runners with nutrition and training advice, marathon whiteboard sessions, or a week training with a marathoner. Morning Brew's creator who left and was brought back.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [0:58:51] to [1:02:57]

### Organic content is the nucleus of the brand

- **What they say:** Everything he said stems from one thesis: "the best brands that are getting built today are built off of organic content". Content is the core and bleeds elsewhere. Organic solves siloed growth teams. Social is where culture and people are.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Publishing 60 pieces a month (stories plus feed) is 60 touch points; no other channel gets that (you don't send 60 emails, nobody sees your landing page 60 times).
- **How to apply:** Make organic content the source of brand perception and value, then build out to other channels.
- **Examples they give:** The 60-touch-points comparison.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [1:03:28] to [1:04:32]

### Repurpose organic winners into emails, landing pages and ad headlines

- **What they say:** Don't let a winning piece "die there". Turn it into a listicle landing page, an email, an ad headline. Organic content becomes your top-of-funnel data point for testing everything, and can drive bottom of funnel too.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It's proven data on what your audience responds to.
- **How to apply:** See the repurposing framework below.
- **Examples they give:** "Five reasons why your intra-marathon nutrition is terrible" (he admits it's terrible copy, as a reference): comments show surprise, so build a listicle landing page, an email with three reasons then "go to the listicle" for the rest. A winning one-shot text line is "probably a good headline for your ad" or email.
- **Applies to:** captions / written posts / strategy
- **Timestamp:** [1:04:32] to [1:06:36]

### Where social is heading: brands as entertainment studios / TV networks

- **What they say:** At the highest level brands are building their own entertainment studios or hiring them, operating like TV networks with dedicated accounts for specific concepts that they scale like a TV show. Roles include a screenwriter (probably from LA/TV, taught to write for short form, as it's easier to condense TV writing than to professionalise social talent). Earlier he predicts launching a "social show" as the biggest marketing move of 2026.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Proven concepts can be run repeatedly, like MTV Cribs.
- **How to apply:** Think in shows and series with repeatable concepts, not one-off posts.
- **Examples they give:** MTV Cribs; his own entertainment studio versus a content agency.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [1:06:36] to [1:08:10]; [0:12:20]

### An analyst who bridges content data across teams

- **What they say:** Have an analyst who understands growth marketing, looks at every piece of content on every platform, and tells every other team what's working and how to test it there (listicle video to email, text headlines to the ad team). Teams hit growth ceilings because they operate in silos and don't share data.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Every growth decision is based on data; not sharing it wastes money.
- **How to apply:** Regularly move winning headlines and formats from social into other channels.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond the listicle/headline cases.
- **Applies to:** strategy
- **Timestamp:** [1:09:12] to [1:10:44]

### Aim for dramatically better, not 3% better, on overall aesthetic

- **What they say:** Grant's closing takeaway, agreed in context: rather than getting "a little bit better", get "dramatically better" (10x), which changes how people perceive the whole format. (This coexists with the 3% twist rule for iterating on proven concepts.)
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A big step up changes the perception of the person/brand entirely.
- **How to apply:** When overhauling a format's look, go aggressive rather than incremental.
- **Examples they give:** Grant restructuring a podcast's aesthetic.
- **Applies to:** strategy / short video
- **Timestamp:** [1:10:13] to [1:11:14]

## Frameworks and templates

**1. Weekly testing loop ($0 tier)**

1. In one day, map out everything going out this week.
2. Look at what works on other platforms and in other categories.
3. Bring it over to your topic; vary content ideas, concepts, formats and packaging.
4. Publish, then analyse: skip rate, like-to-share ratio, retention graph, average watch time.
5. Find micro wins and build on them.

**2. Metric benchmarks**

- Skip rate (swipes before 3 seconds): under 50%; lower is better (a 4m-view video kept about 83% at 3 seconds).
- Like-to-share ratio: about 2 likes per 1 share is strong.
- Retention graph: look for recurring drop-off points (for example 8 seconds).
- Average watch time (45-60 second videos): 12 seconds is where good videos die. Climbing over the first hours = can pop; flat = good but not 100k-plus; falling = cooked. 12 to 15 seconds can mean tens of thousands of extra followers a month.

**3. Second-by-second video planning**

1. Plan seconds 1, 2 and 3 individually: what you do, show and say (shot list, words, angles, aesthetics, actions).
2. Plan 3-6, 6-9 and 9-12 seconds as blocks.
3. Goal: get past the 12-second trap.

**4. Three hook layers:** title hook (on-screen text), visual hook, verbal hook.

**5. Visual hook / "level five" upgrade: action, angle, aesthetic**

- Take a boring level-one format (lookbook: stand in front of a wall, camera on tripod, change outfits).
- Action: micro actions (adjusting glasses, putting a hat on) used as match-cut points.
- Angle: unusual POV (camera on a $40 mount on the side of a car).
- Aesthetic: setting (a rented classic convertible, the neighbourhood).

**6. Test-then-scale ladder**

1. Post the idea as a short B-roll clip with one line of on-screen text.
2. If it pops, the topic is proven.
3. Scale it into a series, carousel, green screen video or talking ("yap") video.
4. Use trial reels to test hook and text variants; post the winners.

**7. Borrowed-contrast format:** "$X version vs $10X version" of your service/product (from cooking/YouTube), showing what each gets you.

**8. Find-the-story-in-the-day template:** "This is day [N] of trying to [big goal], but today [obstacle], because [concrete constraint], and [personal stake]."

**9. 80/20 + 3% rule:** 80% scaling and improving what works; keep testing small iterations; a 3% twist (not 30%) makes something novel.

**10. Two content buckets:** content to get followers (top of funnel, built to go viral, measured by views) and content for followers (fan base, around 20,000 views, measured by whether existing commenters get more invested).

**11. Organic-to-growth repurposing**

1. Spot an organic winner (for example a "five reasons why your X is terrible" video with surprised comments).
2. Build a listicle landing page: reasons 1-5 in more depth, three ways to fix it, the product that helps.
3. Email: give some reasons, send people to the listicle for the rest.
4. Use a winning on-screen text line as an ad or email headline.

**12. Team by budget tier (summary)**

- $0: you do everything; test and analyse.
- $5k: one hybrid videographer/cinematographer (film school students or hungry PAs/extras), mentored, with milestone incentives; hand them your playbook.
- $10k: add an editor; scale output of what works.
- $50k: creative director/strategist (established, with proof), lead cinematographer whose taste matches, hybrid producer/set designer, in-house creator on a dedicated segment account.
- $100k: specialised roles (separate creative director and strategist, DP, lead videographer, screenwriter, set designer, producer) plus a growth analyst.

## Exercises / practice habits they recommend

- Get as many reps as possible, like practising free throws.
- Spend one day a week mapping out the week's tests.
- Scroll other platforms and categories (especially fashion) for formats to borrow.
- Check your analytics obsessively on every post, including watching average watch time over the first hours.
- Look at your calendar each day and find the story in it.
- Plan the first 3 seconds of each video second by second.
- For every proven video, ask what a 3% change would make it better.
- Read comments on fan-base content to see whether the same people are getting more invested; engage with commenters who joke with you.
- (For hiring) Ask candidates what they consume.

## Things they say NOT to do

- Don't sit down and build a strategy first.
- Don't let the skip rate go above 50% (it means a weak hook).
- Don't stall at a 12-second average watch time.
- For brands, title hooks and native text often read as ads (a common reason brands avoid them).
- Don't copy only what works in your own category and try to be 1% better.
- Don't post content that doesn't meet your standard, even if it carries social proof.
- Don't stay only in the "content to get followers" world.
- Don't let the bar drop while scaling output.
- Don't just hand off editing; the bar falls when there's no first version setting it.
- Don't let a winning organic post "die there"; reuse it in other channels.
- Don't let growth teams operate in silos or refuse to share data.
- Don't hire an unproven creative director at the $50k tier: "They can't be built."

## Where this source disagrees with common advice or is nuanced

- **Strategy last:** contradicts the usual "start with a content strategy" advice; start with testing instead.
- **Pro short text reels:** many in his space hate the ~8-second text-on-B-roll reel; he values it as a cheap topic test.
- **Aesthetic over "just yap":** Grant notes the usual advice for solo creators is talking-head, repeatable, time-efficient formats; Alex argues for investing in cinematography and composition, and that most brands won't make talking-head videos. Grant questions if this is "majoring in the minors"; Alex argues aesthetics create demand (TAM).
- **Views are not always the goal:** fan-base content is judged by comment depth, not reach, and 20,000 views can be the target.
- **3% vs 10x:** he advocates tiny 3% twists to make proven concepts novel, while the closing takeaway is to make overall aesthetic dramatically (10x) better rather than a little better. These apply to different things (iterating a proven concept vs raising a brand's baseline).
- **Brands vs creators:** much of the hook advice is framed for brands with constraints (no talking, no title text), where the visual hook must carry the weight; a personal brand has more hook layers available.
- **Benchmark caveats:** the watch-time numbers apply to 45-60 second videos, which he says is where most brand videos sit.
- **Budget framing:** the tiers assume monthly content budgets of $5k to $100k-plus; the principles (testing, analytics, borrowing formats, repeatable concepts, repurposing winners) are presented as the same thesis at every level.
