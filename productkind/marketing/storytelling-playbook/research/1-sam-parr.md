# Become a Dangerously Good Writer in 55 Minutes: Sam Parr with Alex Garcia and Brian Blum

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uf4fR3qcDkU

Note on attribution: the transcript does not label speakers. "Sam" is used where the speaker is clearly Sam Parr (his businesses, his stories); "the hosts" is used for points made by the interviewers (who run short-form content and UGC ad work). Two sponsor reads (Motion, Rich Panel) are excluded.

## Core thesis (2-4 sentences)

Copywriting is understanding what people want and choosing the words that get them to do what you want. The headline or first line carries most of the value, and it works by opening a curiosity gap the reader needs to close; every sentence after that exists to get the next one read (the "slippery slope"). Length is never the problem, boredom is: stories with tension, simple short sentences with rhythm, and a customer-as-hero structure keep the right person reading to the end. The fastest way to get good is copy work: copying proven classics word for word until you absorb their rules.

## Principles and techniques

### Copywriting is about behaviour, not words

- **What they say:** Copywriting isn't just writing words; it's understanding what people want and how to get them to do what you want, then finding the words needed for that outcome. Good copy gets what's in my head into yours and influences your behaviour.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Words are the last step; the desired outcome and the reader's wants come first.
- **How to apply:** Start from the action you want and what the reader wants, then pick words.
- **Examples they give:** Sam's two tweets (2020 and 2022) quoted at the start.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:00:32]

### AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action

- **What they say:** One of the basic principles of copywriting is AIDA. Sam used it even to open a conversation with a stranger (first grab attention). Later he builds a full ad live using AIDA.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Each stage moves the reader one step closer to acting; attention must come first.
- **How to apply:** Attention: a surprising specific claim. Interest: describe the feeling they know. Desire: reveal the real cause and that it's fixable. Action: the product plus proof. (See Frameworks.)
- **Examples they give:** Opening line to a stranger: a silly chickpea/lentil joke. Sleep ad: "a scientific reason why after 35, 75% of men wake up" at 2 to 3am (facts admitted as invented).
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:01:02], [0:34:57]

### You can say anything if you make them laugh; talk like you already know them

- **What they say:** For sales and flirting alike, you can say virtually anything as long as you make them laugh and it's clearly not harmful. The key is to talk to people like you already know them ("restaurant owner energy"). Don't be stiff.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Stiffness makes people wonder what's wrong with you; familiar warmth disarms. Jokes are high risk, high reward: you test the water to see what lands.
- **How to apply:** Open with warmth and familiarity, as if continuing a conversation; use humour to buy permission.
- **Examples they give:** A restaurant owner greeting strangers with "nice to see you again, how's the family?" and offering a toddler ice cream.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:02:05]

### The headline (hook) is most of the ad

- **What they say:** Ogilvy: on average five times as many people read the headline as the body; once you've written the headline you've spent 80 cents of your dollar. Ogilvy spent weeks on the single Rolls-Royce headline. Most people write the article and then the headline at the last minute; young YouTube creators now know the hook and packaging matter more than the video itself.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Most people only ever see the headline, so it decides whether anything else is read.
- **How to apply:** Spend disproportionate time on the headline/hook; don't leave it until last.
- **Examples they give:** "At 60 mph the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:03:07], [0:04:08]

### Mind the gap (curiosity gap / open loops)

- **What they say:** The Rolls-Royce headline works because of the curiosity gap: "you always have to mind the gap." Something grabs attention and you need to read the rest to release the tension. Like a melody that must be resolved, humans aren't good at leaving a cliffhanger. The hosts call this "open loops" and use them in short-form scripts; they say you now have to "stack open loop on open loop" through the script. One host lengthened videos from about 45 seconds to 1:15 to 1:20 and they performed better, adding curiosity gaps "almost every 8 seconds".
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Unresolved tension is uncomfortable; people keep reading or watching to resolve it.
- **How to apply:** Headline/hook states something incomplete or contradictory; the body resolves it. In video, re-open a new loop regularly (roughly every 8 seconds, per one host) rather than one at the start only.
- **Examples they give:** "They laughed when I sat down at the piano, but then I started to play." Upworthy-style "You're not going to believe number seven" (called clickbait and junk, but it drove a competitor site to 200m monthly visitors in about six months).
- **Applies to:** all (stacking loops: short video especially)
- **Timestamp:** [0:04:38]-[0:07:11], [0:09:44]

### Long isn't bad, boring is

- **What they say:** Sam disagrees that attention spans have changed ("zero data", his opinion): human attention has always been the same. Longer things almost always convert better; ugly almost always converts better; it just has to be interesting. "No one will read this, it's too long" really means "not interesting enough". "There's no such thing as too long, just too boring."
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Interest, not length, determines drop-off; longer copy gives more room to persuade the right reader.
- **How to apply:** Don't cut stories or detail for length alone; cut what's boring. Plain design is fine.
- **Examples they give:** Grey-area telehealth affiliates running 27-minute video sales letters as pre-roll ads. Sam's copythat.com: about 2,000 words on a white background, converting at around 5%.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:07:41]-[0:09:14], [0:24:07]

### Break the fourth wall in the opening line

- **What they say:** Sam's favourite hook tells the reader they're reading because he's making them. He calls it getting you down the slippery slope; the hosts call it breaking the fourth wall.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It demonstrates the power of copy while pulling the reader into the second sentence.
- **How to apply:** Address the reader's act of reading/watching directly in line one.
- **Examples they give:** "You're reading this not because you want to, but because I want you to." Followed by "Now, you're reading this second sentence because..."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:09:14], [0:00:00]

### Tease what's coming next

- **What they say:** Tony Robbins' 800-page books constantly tease: in this chapter I'll tell you this, but wait until the next chapter. The hosts note it sounds like a YouTube intro. "There's always teasing."
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A promised payoff later keeps people through the current section.
- **How to apply:** Signal an upcoming reveal before delivering the current point.
- **Examples they give:** "Wait until you get the next chapter..."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:10:15]

### Add a time constraint ("really quick")

- **What they say:** Borrowed from pickup-artist books: when people know you'll leave soon they relax and let you finish. In copy, "what I'm about to tell you next will blow your mind, but really quick let me tell you this" is a strong tactic. A host adds it's a good way to insert a detail.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A stated time limit lowers resistance and snaps attention in.
- **How to apply:** Frame an aside as brief, while teasing the bigger point that follows.
- **Examples they give:** "I only have one minute cuz I'm running late, but really quick."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:10:45]

### Target a specific person with the headline ("niches make riches")

- **What they say:** A headline that only appeals to one group gets very high engagement from that group. "Niches make riches." Aspirational targeting also works: few people are sailboat racers but many want to be. Niche products can expand to people buying for someone in the niche. Sam's Hampton ads call out a specific group then speak "very intimately" to how they feel.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It's the easiest way to a high engagement rate; the targeted person feels spoken to.
- **How to apply:** Name the group in the hook (by place, status, identity, aspiration), then describe their feeling precisely.
- **Examples they give:** BuzzFeed "50 reasons you know you're from Denver" (about 30% of Denver people click). Homesick candles (a scent per US state). "If you make over $3 million a year, I know exactly how you feel." Rolex "The men who shape destinies wear Rolex."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:11:47]-[0:13:19]

### Turn a winning piece into a series

- **What they say:** Sam's "levels of wealth" piece ($1m vs $10m vs $100m) performed well and got press coverage; the hosts say it should be a series and a YouTube video. Sam agrees.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A proven format can be repeated.
- **How to apply:** When a format works, repeat it across multiple posts/formats.
- **Examples they give:** Levels of wealth comparison.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:13:19]-[0:13:49]

### Twist a well-known phrase

- **What they say:** Take a well-known phrase or idea and spin it so it becomes unique to your brand.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Familiarity gets recognition; the twist makes it yours.
- **How to apply:** Pick a common phrase related to your benefit and change one element.
- **Examples they give:** "You may get lost, but not in the crowd." Old Volkswagen and van ads riffing on Lamborghini.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:14:19]

### Use memorable rhetorical phrase structures

- **What they say:** A book recommended by HubSpot's founder lists types of phrase you can steal. Churchill's "we shall fight on the beaches" repetition changed the course of the war by making language memorable. A host adds that syntax and alliteration matter. "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself" is specific: curiosity gap then payoff at the end.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Structured phrasing makes lines memorable and gives a built-in setup and payoff.
- **How to apply:** Borrow proven phrase shapes (repetition, antimetabole, alliteration); put the payoff at the end of the line.
- **Examples they give:** Churchill's Dunkirk speech; "fear itself".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:15:20]-[0:15:51]

### The first word or sentence has to punch

- **What they say:** "The first word needs to punch or the first sentence needs to punch you." Punch somebody in the face with the first sentence.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Attention is won or lost immediately.
- **How to apply:** Make the opening line bold, surprising, self-deprecating or shocking.
- **Examples they give:** The Hustle ad "My friends think I'm smart. I'm not." (It's because I read The Hustle.) About $10m spent on it; Sam says it was singularly responsible for the exit. The shocking "toilet bowl" opener (below).
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:16:21]-[0:16:51], [0:39:01]

### The slippery slope (every sentence sells the next)

- **What they say:** From Joe Sugarman: the purpose of the first sentence is to get you to read the second, the second to get you to read the third, and so on. Based on the rule of consistency: once someone does a small thing, they're likely to keep going (the small window sticker then big yard sign study, about 40% higher).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Consistency: once someone starts reading, they keep reading.
- **How to apply:** Judge each sentence only by whether it pulls the reader into the next one; start with a tiny, easy commitment.
- **Examples they give:** "Can you hold this for me?" before the real favour; politician's sticker then sign.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:17:22]-[0:18:56]

### Aim for the right reader, not everyone

- **What they say:** The goal isn't to get 100% of people to buy. It's to get the right person to read all of the ad; a large share of them will eventually buy.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Full read-through by the right person converts.
- **How to apply:** Write for full attention from your target, accepting others drop off.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond the principle.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:18:56], [0:00:00]

### Rhythm: vary sentence length

- **What they say:** Good writing is like music: it starts, it stops. Some sentences short, some a bit longer, some keep going. "Short, medium, long, short." Sam: rhythm is his favourite thing; many people ramble in the same sentence length over and over.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Rhythm is a powerful way to keep attention; monotony loses it.
- **How to apply:** Deliberately mix sentence lengths in a short/medium/long/short pattern.
- **Examples they give:** Gary Provost-style graphic Sam has posted.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:19:59]-[0:20:29]

### Write at a seventh-grade reading level

- **What they say:** If you're new, write at a seventh-grade level; check with hemingwayapp.com. USA Today is fourth grade, the New York Times seventh.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Even "smart" publications write simply.
- **How to apply:** Paste copy into Hemingway App and simplify until grade 7 or lower.
- **Examples they give:** USA Today, NYT grade levels.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:20:29]

### One point per sentence; swap commas for full stops

- **What they say:** Each sentence should make one point. When you need a comma, often use a full stop instead. Short sentences are best.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Clarity and rhythm.
- **How to apply:** Find commas and split sentences at them.
- **Examples they give:** None specific.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:20:29]-[0:21:01]

### Start sentences with "And" or "But"

- **What they say:** For rhythm, start the second or third sentence with "and" or "but". Transition words.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Creates rhythm and pulls into the next sentence.
- **How to apply:** Open follow-on sentences with conjunctions.
- **Examples they give:** None specific.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:21:01]

### Explain complex things simply (constraint forces clarity)

- **What they say:** Warren Buffett explains insurance in very simple sentences; Sam analysed his letters: average sentence about 17 words (down from 25 when younger). A host: the secret of copywriting is constraint forcing clarity; Sam: "100%". Hemingway expresses profound emotion said simply.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Anyone can understand complicated topics if sentences are short and plain.
- **How to apply:** Keep average sentence length around 17 words or fewer, even for complex subjects.
- **Examples they give:** Buffett on GEICO; Hemingway's "The Old Man and the Sea".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:21:01]-[0:22:02]

### Cut a third, three times (write with your eraser)

- **What they say:** From Shaan (Puri): write your first draft, cut a third, cut a third, cut a third again. "Killing your darlings." "Write with your eraser." "Write drunk and edit sober." Ogilvy: not a great writer, a great editor. You have to write the crap first.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Forces you to say the word you actually mean rather than clauses and roundabout sentences.
- **How to apply:** Draft freely, then do three successive cuts of about a third each.
- **Examples they give:** None beyond the sayings.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:22:02]-[0:23:04]

### Story, story, story (with tension)

- **What they say:** Many people avoid stories when selling because they think it'll be too long. Everything has to be a story; the best way to make something interesting is to make it a story. A story has a beginning, middle and end "with a little bit of tension in there". The hosts say stories are more important than ever because of AI and because Meta ad libraries now lack them.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Stories make things interesting; interest beats length.
- **How to apply:** Frame the pitch as a narrative with a clear beginning, middle, end and tension.
- **Examples they give:** WSJ "tale of two young men" ad (ran 28 years, over $2bn revenue); Sam copied it for The Hustle's $300/yr Trends and it "crushed".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:23:04]-[0:24:38], [0:25:40]

### Customer is the hero, brand is the guide

- **What they say:** Use the hero's journey: the hero is the customer, the brand is the guide who saves them. The hero was lost, something happened, he overcame. A host adds the return-to-origin stage (the reunion).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** It positions the product as the thing that made the reader's success possible.
- **How to apply:** Structure: lost/struggling hero, guide (brand) appears, overcoming, return and contrast.
- **Examples they give:** WSJ ad: the successful graduate read the Journal.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:24:38]-[0:25:09]

### Founder story plus hero's journey

- **What they say:** Sam's best Hampton ad was a story: at his company sale he sat facing 14 lawyers and accountants, thinking "what am I doing here", overwhelmed when he should feel successful, wishing for peers. That's why he started Hampton. The hosts frame it as founder story plus hero's journey (if Sam had had Hampton, he'd have been in a different position).
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** A real, specific moment of vulnerability sets up the product as the missing guide.
- **How to apply:** Open on a specific moment of struggle, then show the product as what you wished you'd had.
- **Examples they give:** The 14-person meeting at the HubSpot sale.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:25:40]-[0:26:40]

### "But" and "therefore" (not "and then")

- **What they say:** The hosts cite Trey Parker/South Park's "but... therefore" as a good storytelling mechanism. In copy work on viral scripts, a host noticed transition words: "but then this happened, and then therefore this happened", then tension.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Causal and contrasting links create tension and momentum.
- **How to apply:** Link beats with "but" and "therefore".
- **Examples they give:** None beyond the mechanism.
- **Applies to:** all (explicitly used in short-form scripts)
- **Timestamp:** [0:26:40], [0:28:43]

### Failed solution: absolve the viewer of guilt

- **What they say:** The hosts use "the failed solution" a lot: you tried to solve this and it didn't work; that's not your fault; you just didn't have the right ingredient or mechanism. Sam uses "It's not your fault" in his sleep ad too.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Absolves guilt and makes room for a new mechanism.
- **How to apply:** Name what they've tried, say it's not their fault, name the missing piece.
- **Examples they give:** "Maybe you didn't feel confident in that meeting. It's not your fault." "It's not your fault... you just don't have enough vitamin D."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:27:11], [0:35:57]

### Use the retention graph to place new curiosity gaps

- **What they say:** A host studied retention graphs; if everyone dropped off at 12 seconds, he'd introduce new attention or a curiosity gap there. That plus copy work took him from 100 to 200k views to a first 2m-view video.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Drop-off points show where tension has run out.
- **How to apply:** Check where viewers leave; put a new open loop just before that point.
- **Examples they give:** The 12-second drop-off.
- **Applies to:** short video
- **Timestamp:** [0:28:43]

### Copy work

- **What they say:** "Copy work is game changing." Find writing that is widely regarded as good and copy it word for word, for about an hour at a time, for months. Like learning an instrument: copy Jingle Bells, then harder songs, until you see the rules and texture and combine what you like. For anything you want taste in, blindly copy until you find the rules of the language. Write it by hand: you feel the texture and remember more physically than typing.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** You can't write a hit song from nothing; copying reveals patterns you then combine.
- **How to apply:** See Exercises.
- **Examples they give:** Sam copied the Boron Letters, The Great Gatsby, SNL scripts. A host transcribed five multi-million-view scripts over two days and got his first 2m-view video.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:27:41]-[0:32:20]

### Lead with proof or transformation

- **What they say:** For a supplement, the best ad is proof: a customer quote or a before/after (e.g. a sleep tracker score). Do a before and after and don't even show the product.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** For supplements, proof is what persuades.
- **How to apply:** Show the measurable change rather than the product.
- **Examples they give:** Whoop score before/after taking it.
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:34:26]

### Story headline that speaks to who the person is

- **What they say:** Alternatively, a story line that speaks to the specific person; it depends who they're targeting.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Identity plus stakes grabs the right person.
- **How to apply:** Write a first-person line naming a personal stake.
- **Examples they give:** "I almost didn't wake up for my little one."

### Trojan horse: bury the product, build desire first

- **What they say:** The hosts call Sam's sleep ad a Trojan horse: bury the product deep in the story and build desire for a long time. Weak ads throw the product at you immediately with no reason to believe.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Without built desire there's no reason to believe.
- **How to apply:** Hold the product back until problem, cause and desire are established.
- **Examples they give:** AG1's "the nightly drink for restorative sleep" criticised as "so basic".
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:37:28]

### Get early yeses (head nods)

- **What they say:** A copywriting rule: get people to say yes very early, with lines they'll nod at. Put all that as early as possible.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Early agreement builds momentum (linked to consistency).
- **How to apply:** Ask a rhetorical question describing their experience, e.g. "don't you always notice...?"
- **Examples they give:** "Why suddenly at 35 you can't sleep more than 3 hours straight."
- **Applies to:** all
- **Timestamp:** [0:37:28]-[0:37:59]

### Shocking, visual comparison

- **What they say:** Make the abstract visceral. A clear, obvious "no" question, then "but that's exactly what you're doing". Numbers mean nothing alone; show the physical quantity.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Visual framing lands where a statistic doesn't; punches in the first sentence.
- **How to apply:** Compare the product's problem to something disgusting or absurd; or turn a number into a physical object.
- **Examples they give:** "Imagine storing your food in the toilet bowl." Microwaving a child's milk in a melting plastic cup vs polyester pajamas in the dryer. 150g of fat shown as a brick next to popcorn. Deconstructed Coke bottle showing the sugar share.
- **Applies to:** all (visual demos especially short video)
- **Timestamp:** [0:37:59]-[0:40:34]

### Handle objections inside the copy

- **What they say:** Like Eminem in 8 Mile's final battle: name every insult against yourself first so the opponent has nothing left. Sam asks the reader's objections in the writing: "I know what you're thinking..." Can also go in FAQs. A host adds personal anecdotes handle objections indirectly.
- **Why it works (their reasoning):** Pre-empting objections disarms them.
- **How to apply:** List the reader's doubts; address them explicitly or with a first-hand story.
- **Examples they give:** "I know what you're thinking. Who's this person?" Lenovo headphones TikTok: "I accidentally wore these in the shower" instead of "they're waterproof"; battery lasting 3 weeks answers "why only $40?"
- **Applies to:** all

### Raw, frame-breaking reality can beat scripted content

- **What they say:** Sam's biggest reel (about 4m views) was unscripted: "there was no writing". ## Frameworks and templates

### AIDA educational story ad (Sam's live sleep-supplement build)

1. **Attention:** A surprising, specific, scientific-sounding fact about the target. ("There's a scientific reason why after 35, 75% of men wake up between 2 and 3am.")
2. **Interest:** Describe the exact feeling they know, and what they've told themselves ("you just thought this is age... normal").
3. **Desire:** "The truth is..." reveal the real cause; tie to an early-yes question ("Haven't you noticed it's worse in winter?"); "It's not your fault."
4. **Good news:** "The good news is this is easy to fix."
5. **Differentiate:** Most alternatives fail for a reason; "Not ours" plus credibility.
6. **Social proof:** "Trusted by 100,000 dads just like you."
7. **Action.**

### Shock comparison template

1. "Imagine [doing something obviously disgusting/absurd]." or "Would you [obviously bad thing]? Of course not."
2. "But that's exactly what you're doing when you [everyday behaviour]."
3. Explain the mechanism briefly.
4. "That's why [product attribute] is better."

### Hero's journey (customer as hero)

1. Hero (customer) is lost or struggling.
2. Something happens: the guide (brand) appears.
3. Hero overcomes.
4. Return to origin/contrast (e.g. the reunion where the two men differ).

### Founder story ad

1. Specific moment: where you were, who was in the room.
2. The feeling that contradicted expectations ("I should feel successful... but overwhelmed").
3. What you wished you'd had.
4. "That's why we started [product]."

### Failed solution

1. "You tried to solve [problem] with [X], but it didn't work."
2. "That's not your fault."
3. "You just didn't have [the right ingredient/mechanism]."

### Slippery slope sentence chain (Sugarman)

1. Sentence 1's only job: get sentence 2 read.
2. Sentence 2's only job: get sentence 3 read, and so on to the end.

### Editing pass

1. Write the rough draft ("write drunk").
2. Cut a third. Cut a third. Cut a third again.
3. Split comma sentences into full stops; one point per sentence.
4. Vary length (short, medium, long, short); start some sentences with And/But.
5. Check reading level in Hemingway App (grade 7 or below).

### Viral video to ad

1. Location and subject ("We're at my father's business").
2. Big specific claim ("over $10 million of onions a year").
3. Tease: "You have to see how he does it."
4. Proof: "This video got 3 million views."
5. Audience call-out: "If you're a business owner, you'll understand why."
6. The insight (frame-breaking) linked to the product; quick CTA.

## Exercises / practice habits they recommend

- **Copy work for six months ("copy hour"):** Google best/most effective/top-selling ads, top American short stories, best stand-up jokes, best SNL bits or famous TV scenes, whatever is critically acclaimed or popular in your genre. Copy the handful of classics word for word, by hand, for about an hour a day, for three to six months. [0:45:14]-[0:46:17]
- **Pick the skill you're studying:** For comedians, study timing/delivery. [0:46:17]
- **Transfer to other crafts:** Recreate a shot to learn editing, recreate a song, rebuild websites you like in Canva for design. [0:46:17]
- **Short-form copy work:** Transcribe five videos with millions of views, look for transition words and tension points, and study retention graphs for drop-offs. [0:28:12]-[0:28:43]
- **Write by hand:** Physical writing aids memory. Sam keeps a five-year journal (one entry a day, compare to prior years). [0:32:20]-[0:32:51]
- **Read magazines and fiction outside your field** and steal lines. [0:47:19]-[0:48:51]
- **Run copy through Hemingway App.** [0:20:29]

## Things they say NOT to do

- Don't write the headline last, as an afterthought. [0:04:08]
- Don't assume long is the problem; don't cut stories because you fear length. [0:07:41], [0:24:07]
- Don't ramble in the same sentence length over and over. [0:20:29]
- Don't pack multiple points into one sentence with commas. [0:20:29]
- Don't be stiff; talk like you know them. [0:02:05]
- Don't throw the product at people immediately with no built desire (AG1 "nightly drink for restorative sleep", Caraway "store more and stress less" called terrible/basic). [0:34:26], [0:37:28], [0:37:59]
- Don't state a bare number (150g of fat "means nothing"); make it visual. [0:40:03]
- Don't just shove benefits in people's faces (the hosts' criticism of current Meta ads that are all two short sentences). [0:26:40]-[0:27:11]
- Upworthy-style clickbait worked for traffic but Sam calls it "junk". [0:05:40]
- Humour doesn't work for every brand. [0:43:41]

## Where this source disagrees with common advice or is nuanced

- **Attention spans:** The hosts say attention spans are "cooked" and you must stack open loops; Sam disagrees, saying attention has always been the same (admitting he has "zero data"). Both agree the content must be interesting throughout.
- **Length:** Against the "keep it short" norm, Sam says longer almost always converts better and one host found 1:15 to 1:20 videos beat 45-second ones when loaded with curiosity gaps every ~8 seconds. Yet he also pushes short sentences and cutting a third three times: short sentences, not short pieces.
- **Ugly converts:** Plain design (2,000 words on white) converts about 5%; polish isn't needed.
- **Clickbait:** Curiosity gaps are central, but Sam labels pure Upworthy clickbait as junk; the gap must be resolved by real content.
- **Scripting:** Despite a talk on writing, Sam's biggest video had "no writing"; raw, frame-breaking footage won. The writing came in when repurposing it into an ad.
- **Price-led ads:** Not automatically bad; fine for bottom-of-funnel, high-intent audiences.
- **Stories vs platform norms:** The hosts note Meta ads trend to two-sentence benefit statements, yet their best performers were stories.
- **Invented facts:** Sam repeatedly says his live examples' facts were made up for illustration and that he would check the truth; the technique is the lesson, not the claims.
- **Humility:** Sam says he's "not that good", most people are just bad at copy.
- **Retention benchmarks:** Hosts treat above 50% retention at the 3-second mark (or 50% at 5 seconds) as the signal of a hit.
