# Storytelling and copywriting playbook

A reusable playbook for writing our short videos (TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts), LinkedIn posts and captions so people stop, stay and act.

It's built from seven expert sources recommended by Nicholas Mlakar (full transcripts, read 2026-09-24) and checked against our own performance data. It records **what the experts recommend**, not our house voice. Adapting it to our values and language rules is a separate, later step. Where a technique doesn't sit right, change it deliberately rather than quietly dropping it.

**Evidence behind every rule:**

- [research/00-synthesis.md](research/00-synthesis.md): all principles across sources, with who says what.
- [research/01-diagnosis.md](research/01-diagnosis.md): our September videos and 20 LinkedIn posts checked against those principles.
- `research/1-...md` to `research/7-...md`: per-source notes with timestamps.
- [storytelling-formats.md](storytelling-formats.md): 15 story shapes (Kallaway's ten storytelling formats plus five teaching formats), each with a beat table mapped to Kallaway's loop.

**Where it's applied:** the `video-script`, `linkedin-post` and `captions` skills and their critics (`video-script-critic`, `linkedin-critic`, `caption-critic`) in `.claude/`.

## 1. The one-page version

Every piece of content is a chain. Each link's only job is to earn the next one (Parr's "slippery slope").

```
IDEA          ->  HOOK            ->  CONFIRM          ->  LOOP              ->  PAYOFF
can it be         stop the scroll     prove the hook       stakes, question,     resolve the loop,
said in one       in 1 to 3 secs      wasn't bait,         twist, re-hook,       then (and only
second?           or one line         within 5 to 10 s     repeat                then) the ask
```

**Ten rules that carry most of the weight:**

1. **The idea sets the ceiling.** If you can't say it in one line that a stranger gets in one second, fix the idea before the words (Galloway).
2. **Spend disproportionate time on the hook.** Five times more people read the headline than the body (Parr, quoting Ogilvy). Write at least 10 hooks per piece and pick one (Galloway).
3. **Open a specific gap.** Say something incomplete, contradictory or surprising that only the rest of the piece resolves. "Something crazy happened" isn't a gap; the viewer needs enough detail to form a guess (Kallaway, Mino).
4. **Lead with the most interesting line you have.** It's usually buried in paragraph two or at second 15. Move it to the top (Galloway: least context first).
5. **Confirm the hook in the next sentence.** Within 5 to 10 seconds, make it obvious the piece is about what the hook promised. Otherwise it's clickbait and costs trust next time too (Mino, Galloway).
6. **Stakes before story.** Who is this happening to, what's at risk, why now (Kallaway). For teaching content, the stake is the cost of not knowing (Galloway).
7. **Never let a loop close without opening the next one.** "Which would have been great except...", "But here's the problem..." (Kallaway). Section ends are where people leave.
8. **Deliver the value inside the piece.** Don't hold back the takeaway to sell ("value-edging", Ralston). The ask comes after the payoff, not instead of it.
9. **Specific beats universal.** Numbers, names, real moments, uncomfortable details (Mino, Howard, Parr).
10. **Measure against your own median and change one thing at a time** (Ralston, Garcia, Galloway).

## 2. Step 1: the idea

The experts agree that no hook or edit saves a weak idea (Galloway, Garcia). Run each idea through this before writing.

### Idea checklist

- [ ] **Glance test:** can it be said as a one-line log line that a stranger gets in one second? (Galloway)
- [ ] **Question:** does it leave a question the viewer has to watch or read to answer? (Galloway)
- [ ] **Painful problem:** is it about a costly problem our ideal viewer has (time, money, reputation, confidence)? (Ralston)
- [ ] **Our angle:** what's our specific solution or lived experience here? A student sharing what they're testing counts; posturing as an expert doesn't. (Ralston)
- [ ] **Proven structure:** is there a format that's already working, in our niche, an adjacent niche or another category, that this fits? (Mino, Galloway, Garcia)
- [ ] **Twist:** what's our 3% twist on it, or what does everyone else say about this that we disagree with? (Garcia, Mino, Ralston)
- [ ] **Specific:** does it contain a real moment, number, name or detail only we'd include? (Howard, Mino)
- [ ] **Core, casual and new:** would a die-hard follower and a complete stranger both get something from it? (Galloway)

### Where ideas come from

| Source | How | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer problems | List 3 to 10 painful problems. Each has many angles, and each angle is a piece. 5 problems gives 25+ pieces. | Ralston |
| Our past winners | Anything well above our median. Repeat it, twist it, turn it into a series. Audiences forget within seconds. | Galloway, Ralston, Parr |
| Other people's outliers | Posts or videos at 3x+ their creator's average, in our niche and adjacent ones. Ask: what's our spin on that? | Galloway, Mino |
| Other categories | Borrow formats from fashion, cooking, finance. "$100 accountant vs $1,000 accountant" came from cooking's "$1 vs $10". | Garcia |
| Contrarian takes | Two columns: what our niche says, and what we believe instead. Track which takes get DMs and follow-up questions. | Ralston |
| The story in the day | A day of meetings becomes a story with a goal, a day count and a stake. | Garcia |
| Curiosity | "See something, save something." Chase references you don't understand. Act on your own "I want to learn about X". | Howard |

### Generate many, keep one

Galloway's funnel: 100 ideas, filtered to about 10 by elimination criteria, then pick the one you'd put your career on. "Can we package it?" (can it get a strong hook and first frame?) removes about two thirds on its own. If 30 of 100 survive, the criteria are too loose. If one or two do, they're too strict.

## 3. Step 2: the hook

### What a hook has to do

In the first 1 to 3 seconds of a video, or the first line of a post, the hook has to:

1. **Stop the scroll** with something the feed doesn't usually show (Garcia).
2. **Be understood at a glance:** fewest, plainest words (Galloway).
3. **Open a specific gap** the viewer wants closed (Parr, Kallaway, Galloway).
4. **Signal who it's for** and why it's worth their time (Parr, Mino, Ralston).

### Hook ingredients

Combine two or three. The strongest hooks in the sources stack a gap with specificity and proof.

| Ingredient | What it looks like | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Contradiction** | Two true things that shouldn't both be true. "I found the cheapest 911 GT3... but nobody will buy it." | Galloway |
| **Specific number or name** | Exact figures, places, tools, times. Can double or triple views. | Mino, Parr |
| **Proof or result first** | What happened, shown rather than listed. Results beat names. | Mino, Ralston |
| **The viewer's pain read back** | Their exact situation or worst fear, in their words, so they feel heard. | Mino, Parr |
| **Audience call-out** | Name the specific group. "Niches make riches." | Parr, Ralston |
| **Stakes and deadline** | Something at risk, on a clock. "The call was in 2 hours" beats "later that day". | Kallaway, Galloway |
| **Extreme or emphasised word** | Language that provokes shock, curiosity, fear or anger. Painful emotions are strongest. | Mino |
| **Contrarian take** | What everyone says, and why it's wrong. | Ralston, Mino |
| **Short stated time frame** | "Six objections in 60 seconds." High value per second. | Mino |
| **A real moment, mid-action** | Open inside the scene, not before it. | Kallaway, Galloway, Mino |
| **Visual comparison** | "Imagine storing your food in the toilet bowl." Makes the abstract visceral. | Parr |
| **Self-deprecation or breaking the fourth wall** | "My friends think I'm smart. I'm not." | Parr |

### Hook formulas

Fill in the blanks. The examples use our own topics to show the shape; check any claim before using it.

| # | Formula | Our-topic example | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **[Thing A is true]. [Thing B that contradicts A is also true].** | "Engineering says it's done. Your users still don't have it." | Galloway (contradiction) |
| 2 | **[Plain statement] -> add "I found" -> add a superlative -> add "but [contradiction]".** | "Out of our nine courses, the shortest one took us the longest to build." | Galloway (good, better, best) |
| 3 | **[Specific result]. [The surprising detail].** | "If you google 'little parrot', our site is the first result. Above the actual parrots." | Mino, Ralston (proof first) |
| 4 | **We were about to [action] when we realised [problem].** | "We were about to add prices to the landing pages when we realised we'd be testing two things at once." | Kallaway (stakes + head fake) |
| 5 | **[Question the reader answers about their own life]?** | "When was the last time you had a great day at work?" | Mino, Galloway |
| 6 | **[The number that's embarrassing]. [What we did anyway].** | "We posted 76 times on Instagram. We got 33 followers." | Mino (uncomfortable details) |
| 7 | **I can't [X], so today I'm [surprising Y].** | "I can't afford [an SEO agency], so today I'm [asking my AI agent to audit our site]." | Mino |
| 8 | **Is it possible to [outcome] in [short time or constraint]?** Needs a question the viewer has really asked, real stakes and a real chance of failure. Don't reveal the answer early. | "Is it possible to fix a broken sign-up in an AI-built app without writing code?" | Mino (challenge) |
| 9 | **Here's how to [specific, differentiated outcome].** + read the pain back + "The solution that's always worked for me is..." + "Now, I know you're probably thinking..." | "Here's how to write a bug report an engineer can act on without asking you a single question." | Mino (educational) |
| 10 | **POV: [relatable real moment]**, real footage first, then narration. | "POV: your stakeholder just asked when your prototype launches." | Mino (POV) |
| 11 | **[$X version] vs [$10X version] of [thing].** | "A 5-minute bug report vs a 5-second one." | Garcia (borrowed contrast) |
| 12 | **Day [N] of trying to [big goal], but today [obstacle], because [constraint], and [personal stake].** | "Day [3] of validating our learning path, and [today's real obstacle]." | Garcia (story in the day) |
| 13 | **Imagine [something obviously absurd]. That's exactly what you do when [everyday behaviour].** | "Imagine posting a letter with no address. That's what 'I found a bug' is to an engineer." | Parr (visual comparison) |
| 14 | **[Group], stop [common behaviour] if you want [outcome].** | "Product managers, stop sending engineers 'I found a bug'." | Parr (call-out), Mino (extreme word) |
| 15 | **Everyone says [common advice]. [What happened when we did it].** | "Everyone says enable paid subscriptions on day one. 44% of my new subscribers came from somewhere else." | Ralston (contrarian), Kallaway |

### Hook don'ts

- **Empty teasers:** "Something crazy happened", "You won't believe this", "Things were about to change" (Kallaway).
- **Generic phrasing** that sounds like something said "two scrolls ago": "3 lessons that changed my life", "Here's how to level up" (Mino).
- **Statement intros:** "In this video we'll cover...", "As a [role], you may have heard..." (Galloway).
- **Leading with the lesson** in a story. It closes the loop before it opens (Mino).
- **Revealing the payoff in the first 3 seconds** of a challenge or story (Mino).
- **Unanswerable or yes/no questions** with a guessable answer. The viewer must want to find out (Kallaway, Galloway).
- **Asking for something before giving anything:** "What do you think?" as line one (our data, see the diagnosis).
- **A hook the piece doesn't deliver on** (Mino, Galloway, Parr).

## 4. Step 3: confirm the promise

Mino says this is the step "most people get wrong".

- **Sentence two confirms sentence one.** It restates or directly extends the hook's topic, so the viewer knows within 5 seconds that they're in the right place (Mino).
- **Deliver the promise within 10 seconds.** Galloway has seen 60 to 70% of viewers leave in the first 10 seconds, nearly always because they didn't get what the packaging promised. Show it if you can; visual confirmation beats verbal.
- **On-screen text and spoken words must match** (Mino).
- **Match the hook type to the real content.** A teaching piece gets an educational hook; a story gets a story or challenge hook (Mino).
- **Make the start feel like the piece has already begun.** "The best intro doesn't feel like an intro." No "anyway, let's get into it" (Galloway).

**Test:** read only your first two sentences. Does sentence two prove that sentence one wasn't bait?

## 5. Step 4: the body, keep the loop running

### Kallaway's four-step loop

Use this as the default structure for any story, including client stories, build-in-public posts and "here's what happened" videos.

| Step | What it does | How to write it | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Stakes** | Switches on the viewer's prediction machine | A **character** (you, a learner, "a PM I talked to last week"), **something at risk**, and **urgency** ("within a week", "48 hours before the budget ran out"). Stakes need personal relevance, not drama. | Skipping it. Before the story starts, the viewer must know who, what's at risk and why now. |
| **2. Big question** | A steady drip of anticipation | A specific question the viewer can start guessing about. Load the biggest one as early as possible. Before any important reveal: stop, back up, give just enough context for them to guess first. | Being vague ("the call went well"), or flooding the setup with detail. |
| **3. Head fake** | The biggest spike of attention | A reveal that breaks their prediction: surprising but immediately logical. Plant clues beforehand, the way a magician shows the empty hat. | A random twist with no setup, which confuses rather than rewards. |
| **4. Re-hook** | Keeps them from leaving | As the head fake lands, open the next question in the same breath. Then repeat from step 2. | Dead air: "Anyway, now I'm on to..." |

**Re-hook phrase bank** (Kallaway, Parr, Mino):

- "...which would have been great, except..."
- "But here's the problem with that."
- "And that's when I realised..."
- "...which is exactly why..."
- "But then this happened, so..." (Parr's but/therefore rule, not "and then")
- "Now, I know you're probably thinking..." (Mino)
- "But wait until you see..." (Parr: tease what's next)

### Stakes menu

When a piece feels flat, it usually lacks stakes (Galloway, Kallaway).

| Stake | Example shape |
| --- | --- |
| **Doubter or enemy** | "My stakeholder thought it was ready to ship." |
| **Time limit** | "The demo was on Thursday." |
| **Reward** | "If this worked, we'd know whether anyone wanted the path." |
| **Punishment or loss** | "Three weeks later, the roadmap moved." |
| **Missing out** | "Every week without this, you're re-explaining your app from scratch." |
| **Cost of not knowing** (for teaching content) | "The best-written page gets zero visitors if Google hasn't indexed it." |

### Keeping attention through the middle

- **Danger zones:** every section end is where attention drops. Put a re-hook there, never a sales line (Kallaway). On video, check the retention graph for the second where people leave and add a new gap just before it (Parr).
- **Stack open loops.** One of Parr's hosts adds a new curiosity gap "almost every 8 seconds" (Parr).
- **Drag out the key moment.** Take the moment you could sum up in one sentence and spread it over several beats: what you heard, what you saw, how your stomach dropped (Mino).
- **Beats, not lists.** A list of abstractions ("whose data it holds, who's allowed in...") is a summary. Turn one item into a moment instead (Mino, Parr).
- **Include the uncomfortable detail.** If you don't feel slightly exposed, you're probably leaving out what people would care about. Share insecurities; don't overshare (Mino, Ralston).
- **Handle objections before they scroll:** "Now, I know you're probably thinking..." (Mino, Parr).
- **Failed solution:** "You tried X. It didn't work. That's not your fault; you didn't have Y." (Parr)
- **Early yeses:** lines the viewer nods at, as early as possible (Parr).
- **Make numbers visual:** a bare number "means nothing" (Parr).
- **Emotional why over logistics:** nobody cares about the plan, only why it matters (Mino).
- **No conclusion language mid-piece:** "at the end of the day" causes drop-off (Galloway).
- **Never make a newcomer feel they missed something:** no "as I said last week". Sow context inside natural lines (Galloway).
- **Long isn't the problem, boring is.** "There's no such thing as too long, just too boring" (Parr). Cut what's boring, not what's long.

### Other structures the sources give

- **Customer as hero** (Parr): the customer is lost or struggling, the guide (us) appears, they overcome it, then a contrast with where they started.
- **Founder story** (Parr): a specific moment (where, who was in the room), the feeling that contradicted expectations, what you wished you'd had, "that's why we built...".
- **AIDA teaching ad** (Parr): a surprising specific fact, then the feeling they know, then "the truth is..." (the real cause) with "it's not your fault", then "the good news is it's fixable", then why alternatives fail, then social proof, then the action.
- **Educational script** (Mino): the specific outcome, pain read back, "the solution that's always worked for me", the objection handled, the steps.

## 6. Step 5: the payoff and the ask

- **Resolve the loop you opened.** A curiosity gap that isn't paid off with real content is clickbait (Parr, Mino, Kallaway).
- **Give the full takeaway.** Don't value-edge. Ralston made the paid thing better and gave it away free, and it changed his business. Capture the relationship with the ask instead (email, waitlist, follow).
- **Hold the product back until desire is built** (Parr's "Trojan horse"). Weak ads throw the product in immediately.
- **Put the product inside the story** rather than interrupting it (Galloway: Red Bull never stops to say "buy Red Bull").
- **Social proof near the close** where it's real (Parr).
- **Decide the piece's job before writing the ask** (Ralston): exposure (a follow-ask, or none), community (a question about the reader's own experience), or conversion (one clear link). One job per piece.

## 7. Sentence craft

Mostly Parr, and it applies to everything written: posts, captions, scripts.

- **Each sentence only has to earn the next one** (Joe Sugarman, via Parr).
- **Rhythm:** vary length. Short. Medium. Then a longer one that keeps going a little. Short again.
- **One point per sentence.** Where you'd use a comma, try a full stop.
- **Start some sentences with And or But.**
- **Simple words, at about a seventh-grade reading level.** Check it in Hemingway App. Buffett averages about 17 words a sentence.
- **Cut a third, three times.** Write the rough draft freely first.
- **Talk like you already know them.** Don't be stiff.
- **Humour if it fits** the brand (Parr).
- **Twist a well-known phrase** so it's yours (Parr).
- **Universal language:** cut jargon the viewer won't know, unless you're deliberately filtering for insiders (Galloway, Ralston).

## 8. Format guide: short video

**Our current problem** ([diagnosis](research/01-diagnosis.md)): average watch time is 3.4 to 4.0 seconds, with the drop at 0:01 to 0:02. Swapping between GIF, B-roll and stock made no difference, so the lever is frame one, the first line and the script structure.

### The three hook layers (Garcia)

Plan all three for every video. Each is a separate chance to stop the scroll.

| Layer | What it is | Rules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Text hook** | On-screen title in frame one | Readable in under a second, few words, no all caps (Galloway). Complements what's said rather than repeating it. Works without sound. |
| **Visual hook** | What the first frame shows | Pattern-disrupt: an unusual **angle**, an **action**, a distinctive **aesthetic** (Garcia). A "click to unpause" moment: frozen just before the payoff, mid-action or mid-sentence, never someone smiling at the camera (Galloway). A real face or a real screen, mid-action (our own TikTok review). |
| **Spoken hook** | The first words | The formulas in [section 3](#3-step-2-the-hook). The first four or five words carry it, because that's all a viewer hears by second 2. |

### Second-by-second plan (Garcia)

Fill this in before writing the rest of the script.

| Time | What we show | What text says | What we say | What gap is open |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 to 0:01 |  |  |  |  |
| 0:01 to 0:02 |  |  |  |  |
| 0:02 to 0:03 |  |  |  |  |
| 0:03 to 0:06 |  |  | Confirm the hook |  |
| 0:06 to 0:09 |  |  |  | New gap or stakes |
| 0:09 to 0:12 |  |  |  | Re-hook before 12 s |
| 0:12 to end |  |  | Loop: question, head fake, re-hook |  |
| last beat |  |  | Payoff, then the ask |  |

### Short video rules

- **Past 12 seconds is the first milestone.** Garcia: 12 seconds of average watch time is "where good videos die". Plan the first 12 in 3-second blocks.
- **Length follows interest.** Parr: one host's videos did better at 1:15 to 1:20 than at 45 seconds once loaded with a gap every 8 seconds. Garcia: 7 to 10 second one-shot and B-roll-plus-text reels are valid for testing topics. Until our watch time rises, shorter scripts (12 to 18 seconds) are the cheaper way to test hooks (our TikTok review).
- **Three hook variants per script.** Use Instagram trial reels, or alternate across days, to test hooks and on-screen text before scaling the winner (Garcia).
- **Cheap topic test first.** B-roll plus one line of text. If it takes off, the topic is proven; scale it into a talking video, series or carousel (Garcia).
- **Repeatable formats.** One set-up, many stories (Garcia: 25 videos in 3 hours). Series beat one-offs (Garcia, Parr).
- **Talking to camera** (Mino): picture one friend who needs this, emphasise a word in every sentence, use your hands, no monotone, good front light.
- **Deliver the value in the video.** A problem followed by "we're building a course" is value-edging (Ralston). Give the answer, then the ask.

### Short video pre-publish checklist

- [ ] With the sound off, frame one alone makes someone want to see what happens next.
- [ ] The text hook is readable in under a second.
- [ ] The first five spoken words contain the gap, a number, a contradiction or a stake.
- [ ] Seconds 3 to 6 confirm the hook.
- [ ] There's a new gap or re-hook before 12 seconds.
- [ ] Every section end has a re-hook, not a sales line.
- [ ] The video delivers the promised value before any ask.
- [ ] We wrote at least 10 hooks and are testing at least 2.
- [ ] We wrote down a prediction (views, average watch time) before posting.

## 9. Format guide: LinkedIn posts

None of the seven sources covers LinkedIn specifically. The storytelling and copywriting principles apply directly (Kallaway and Mino say the loop works for "emails, tweets, posts"), and our own data backs them up.

**What our data says** ([diagnosis](research/01-diagnosis.md), 20 posts, median 457 impressions):

| Top posts (1.3x to 3.6x the median) opened with... | Bottom posts (0.4x to 0.6x) opened with... |
| --- | --- |
| A question the reader answers about their own life | A topic introduction ("As a product manager, you may have heard...") |
| A real moment with a turn ("We were about to... when we realised") | An audience call-out and a tip promise ("this is the first SEO task I'd do") |
| A belief the post later proves wrong | A general opinion about other people |
| A contradiction ("the shortest one took the longest") | A request ("What do you think?") |
| Specific, uncomfortable numbers ("76 posts, 33 followers") | Someone else's news or quote |
| A result ("our site is the first result. Above the actual parrots.") |  |

A good body didn't rescue a weak first line.

### LinkedIn post structure

1. **Line 1, the hook:** one of the formulas in section 3, usually 1 to 6 or 15. It's the line the feed shows before "...see more", so it has to stand alone.
2. **Line 2, confirm and deepen:** prove line 1 wasn't bait. Add the stakes: who, what's at risk, why now.
3. **The moment:** open the scene. Drag out the key beat with specific, textured detail (Mino, Howard).
4. **The big question:** make the reader guess (Kallaway).
5. **The head fake:** the turn, the surprise, "But there's a fallacy here." (Kallaway)
6. **Re-hook and repeat** if the post is longer. Every paragraph break is a danger zone.
7. **The payoff:** the lesson, method or takeaway, given in full.
8. **The ask:** one job only. For community, a question about the reader's own experience; our most-commented posts end this way. For conversion, one link.

**Teaching posts** keep the same shape but swap the story for the cost of not knowing. Open on the stake or a result, not on the topic: "The best-written page in your niche can get zero visitors from Google" beats "This is the first SEO task I'd do". Then deliver the steps.

### LinkedIn pre-publish checklist

- [ ] Line 1 works on its own, above "see more".
- [ ] Line 1 is a moment, a result, a contradiction, a specific number or a question about the reader's life. It's not a topic intro, a general claim or a request.
- [ ] The most interesting line of the post is at the top, not in paragraph 2 or 5.
- [ ] Line 2 confirms line 1.
- [ ] There are stakes: who, what's at risk, why now.
- [ ] There's at least one turn or head fake.
- [ ] The post delivers its takeaway in full.
- [ ] It passes a sentence-craft pass: cut a third, one point per sentence, varied rhythm.
- [ ] One ask, matched to the post's job.
- [ ] We noted the post's job and expected impressions against our 457 median.

## 10. Format guide: captions

The sources treat captions as an extra hook layer and a way to carry what the video can't (Garcia: "leveraging captions even as hooks"; his B-roll-plus-text reels put "the rest in the caption").

- **The first line is a hook**, not a label. "How do you run an AI-built app yourself?" is a topic label; Mino would call it generic phrasing heard "two scrolls ago". Use the same ingredients as section 3: a specific stake, a number, a contradiction.
- **Confirm, don't repeat.** The caption should add to the video's hook rather than restate its first line word for word (Galloway: complement, don't repeat).
- **Carry the value the video pointed at:** steps, the prompt, the checklist. That gives people a reason to save and share (Garcia: shares are the strongest signal).
- **Call out the audience** so the right viewer knows it's for them (Parr, Ralston).
- **Ralston's search point:** most of his traffic now comes from search, and plain, descriptive packaging wins there over time. On platforms where people search (YouTube, and increasingly TikTok and Instagram), say plainly what the video is about somewhere in the caption.
- **One ask**, matched to the piece's job.

## 11. Before and after: our own content rewritten

These show how to apply the playbook. They follow the experts' techniques, not yet our voice rules, and every claim comes from the original piece.

### Video: pm-08 "When can we launch it?"

**Before** (average watch 2.4 to 4.6 s):

> Frame one: stock clip, subtitle begins. "You built something with AI to test an idea. Your stakeholder saw it and asked when we can launch it. [...] If you want to answer that stakeholder with real reasons, we're building a learning path for it."

**After:**

> **Frame one (text):** "It works on my laptop. Why can't it launch?" **Frame one (visual):** a real screen with the prototype running, and a Slack message arriving from a stakeholder: "Looks great, when can we launch?" **0:00 (spoken):** "Your stakeholder just saw your AI prototype and asked when it launches." **0:03 (confirm):** "Here are the five things it skipped, so you can answer them." **Body:** each of the five (whose data it holds, who's allowed in, what happens under real load, who gets woken at 2am, who maintains it) as one short beat with a concrete example, and a re-hook between them ("And that's the easy one."). **Payoff, then ask:** "Save this for the next time someone asks. We're building a learning path that goes through each one. Comment on this video and we'll send you the waitlist link."

What changed: the contradiction is now in frame one, the stakes arrive in the first sentence, and the video delivers the five answers instead of promising a course that will.

### Video: vc-08 "Can you run your own app?"

**Before:** "Can you run your own app? You built it with AI."

**After:** "A customer can't sign in to the app you built with AI. The AI gives you a fix. Do you publish it, restore yesterday's version, or collect more evidence first?"

What changed: a yes/no question became stakes plus a specific big question the viewer can guess at (Kallaway), using the three options already in the script.

### LinkedIn: webhook vs API (238 impressions, 0.5x)

**Before:** "As a product manager, you may have heard the terms 'API requests' and 'webhooks'. If you have ever been too afraid to ask what the difference is..."

**After:** "With an API request, you ask another service for information. With a webhook, you give it your address so it can tell you when something happens. That's the whole difference, and here's why it matters the next time your team says 'we'll just add a webhook'."

What changed: the payoff that sat 350 words down (the mental model) is now the hook, and a stake is added (the meeting where it comes up). The body keeps the #cutecats example as the story.

### LinkedIn: SEO indexing (243 impressions, 0.5x)

**Before:** "If you built your website or app with AI, this is the first SEO task I'd do: check whether Google has actually indexed your important pages."

**After:** "You can have the best-written page in your niche and get zero visitors from Google, because Google never added it to its index. If you built your site with AI, here's the free check I'd do first."

What changed: the stake from line 3 (the cost of not knowing) is now line 1. The audience call-out and promise move to line 2, which confirms it.

### LinkedIn: built with code (179 impressions, 0.4x)

**Before:** "We built this video with AI. What do you think?"

**After:** "We made this video with AI, and no video model touched it. Every card, pill and icon is React code that Claude Code wrote."

What changed: the head fake from paragraph two (it's code, not a generated video) is now the hook. The question for the reader moves to the end.

## 12. Measure and iterate

### What to measure

| Platform | Metric | Benchmark or rule | Who |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short video | Viewers still watching at 3 s (Instagram also shows this as skip rate) | Keep at least half. A hit holds 50% or more at 3 s; Parr's 4m-view video held about 80%. | Garcia, Parr |
| Short video | Average watch time | 12 s is where good videos die (for 45 to 60 s videos). Watch the trend in the first hours: rising can take off, flat is fine, falling is done. | Garcia |
| Short video | Retention graph | Find the second where people leave, across several videos, and fix what happens there. | Garcia, Parr, Galloway |
| Short video | Shares to likes | About 1 share per 2 likes is strong. | Garcia |
| All | Views or impressions vs our median | Judge against our own median, not other people's numbers. | Ralston, Galloway |
| LinkedIn | Impressions vs median (currently 457), out-of-network %, comments | Our best post reached 61% out of network. Track which first-line type each post used. | Our data |
| Conversion posts | Sign-ups by UTM `utm_content` | A post whose job is conversion is judged on sign-ups, not impressions. | Ralston |

**At our current scale,** Ralston's point applies: "If you do 30 videos, one of them is going to get 415." Relative outliers are the signal; double down on them.

### Routines

**Before each post (Galloway):** write down the piece's job, the hook type used, and a prediction (impressions or views, average watch time). Judge results against the prediction.

**Weekly (Garcia):** spend one planning session mapping the week's posts as tests. Each test changes **one** variable (idea, hook, format or packaging) so we know what caused the result.

**Monthly (Ralston's top 10% review):**

1. Take the last 90 days per platform and work out the median of the metric that matters.
2. Give each piece its multiplier against the median.
3. Set aside the one freak outlier.
4. Study the top 10% together. What do they share: topic, format, hook type, first frame, first line?
5. Force those elements into the next batch and see if they hold.
6. Add 1 to 3 validated items to a checklist every future piece must meet.

**Log:** keep one line per post in the platform's analytics file (for LinkedIn, `../channels/linkedin/`): date, piece, hook type, job, prediction, result, multiplier.

### Scale what works

- **Winners become series** (Parr, Garcia, Galloway).
- **80/20** (Garcia, Galloway): 80% of output on proven formats and topics, 20% on experiments.
- **Accordion** (Ralston): post more to learn fast, then fewer and better pieces once you know what works, then repeat.
- **Repurpose winners** (Garcia): a winning first line becomes an email subject, ad headline or landing page headline.
- **Repeat yourself** (Ralston): audiences forget within seconds.

## 13. Practice habits

| Habit | How | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Copy work** | Copy great writing word for word by hand, about an hour a day, for 3 to 6 months: classic ads, top posts, great scripts. For short video, transcribe five multi-million-view scripts and mark where the tension and transitions are. | Parr |
| **Hook swipe file** | Save great hooks, first frames, titles and outliers as you scroll, in themed collections. Review them weekly. | Galloway |
| **10 hooks per piece** | Write at least 10, pick one, test two. | Galloway |
| **Ideal viewer document** | One person, in granular detail: their exact pains, fears and words. Pull hooks from it. | Mino |
| **Two-column contrarian list** | What our niche says vs what we believe. Refresh it monthly. | Ralston |
| **Curiosity inputs** | Save anything that sparks, chase references you don't understand, read outside tech (fashion, fiction, magazines). | Howard, Parr, Garcia |
| **Story in the day** | Each day, note one moment with an obstacle and a stake. | Garcia |
| **AI for research, humans for the final word** | AI gets writing "80% of the way there" (Galloway). Use it for ideation, outliers and fact-finding; write and edit the final yourself. | Galloway, Parr |
| **Ship on schedule** | Research is enjoyable; turning it into a finished piece is stressful, and that's normal. Make a commitment to publish so research turns into output. | Howard |

## 14. Where the experts disagree

Decide deliberately rather than by default.

| Question | One side | Other side | How to use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity gaps or plain packaging? | Parr, Kallaway, Mino, Galloway: the gap is everything. | Ralston: in search, plain evergreen packaging beats curiosity titles over time. | Gaps for feeds, where people are served content. Plain, descriptive language wherever people search (captions, YouTube titles). |
| Virality or trust? | Galloway: views drive most outcomes. | Ralston: optimise for trust; even limit reach to filter for the right people. | Decide each piece's job first (section 6). |
| Hooks first, or the posting habit first? | Most sources: the hook is the priority. | Ralston: beginners should build consistency first and optimise from data later. | Both: post consistently and test one hook variable at a time. |
| Story or education? | Kallaway, Parr: make everything a story. | Mino: storytelling is overhyped for business; educational content builds trust and sells. | Match the hook to the real content (section 4). Teaching posts still need stakes. |
| Which emotion? | Mino: painful emotions (fear, anger) are strongest. | Parr: humour is one of the easiest ways to sell, if it fits the brand. | A brand choice, to make when adapting this playbook. |
| How long? | Parr: longer converts better if it's interesting. | Garcia: 7 to 10 second reels are valid tests. | Length follows how long you can keep the loop running. |
| Niche or broad? | Ralston, Galloway: start narrow and expand. | Howard: niching flattens content; odd combinations of interests are magnetic. | Narrow topic, personal texture. |
| Remix or originate? | Mino, Galloway, Garcia, Parr: remix proven formats with a twist. | Ralston: start from customer problems and your own experience, not competitors' outliers. | Borrow the structure, bring the substance. |
| Polish? | Garcia: aesthetic and composition create demand. | Galloway: make it watchable, fix ideas and packaging, polish later. | Watchable first; the first-frame composition is worth the effort even now. |
