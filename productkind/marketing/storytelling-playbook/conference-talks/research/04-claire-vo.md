**The talk in one sentence:** AI has made execution so cheap that a feature-and-date roadmap now drives teams into backlog, parity and churn traps at machine speed, so product leaders should replace it with durable convictions tested against real evidence, disposable features, honest commitment levels and a few very ambitious bets.

## 1. Opening (first 60 to 90 seconds)

- First sentence, verbatim: "Good morning, everyone. How's it going? Everybody excited to be here?"
- Opening type: crowd warm-up, then an in-joke and self-deprecation (the party someone called "the How I AI sauna", her 8.15 bedtime, a baby; 0:12 to 0:32), then a callback to her own past claim ("last year I came here and I said product management is dead", 0:32), then a provocation ("This year I'm coming for the roadmap. Yeah, you're welcome.", 1:08 to 1:18).
- When the speaker introduces themselves: never. "I have been in product for over two decades" (1:35) is the only credential, slipped in mid-claim.
- When the stakes and the talk's promise become clear: the promise at 1:40 ("a lot of us in this room are about to write our last roadmap"); the real stakes at 5:48 to 6:02 when she asks who is shipping more than ever and then "Whose revenue is going up proportionate with the amount of PRs?"
- Observation: room noise rises right after the first question (about 0:06 to 0:11 in the audio), consistent with the crowd answering. "Who's with me... product management is completely different?" (0:54) asks for hands, and she reports "Okay, got some hands." She admits last year's claim was wrong ("be proved completely wrong in the market. It just keeps me really honest", 0:38 to 0:49) before making a new one of the same shape.
- Interpretation: the opening works because it is a callback series the room is in on: last year's bold claim, last night's party, and a new bold claim delivered as a running bit ("OKRs next and then my job is over"). Admitting last year's claim was wrong buys permission for this year's. It relies heavily on existing relationship with the audience; a speaker without that history could not open on an in-joke about a party. The 20 seconds of warm-up chat would be dead air for a cold audience.

## 2. Throughline and structure

- Structure type: confession plus "what we got wrong" argument: provocation, personal confession, how it used to work, a personal story, named traps, a new concept, the replacement framework, and an ask. A strong single throughline (the roadmap) with a nested story (the product graph) that opens at 6:07 and closes at 19:26.
- Main sections with timestamps:
  - 0:00 to 1:50: warm-up, last year's claim, this year's provocation ("coming for the roadmap")
  - 1:50 to 3:09: "I have a confession": shipping more than ever, 40 bots, out of good ideas
  - 3:09 to 6:02: how it used to work (engineering scarcity, everyone saying "no"); bottleneck moved from building to believing; the revenue question
  - 6:02 to 8:47: product graph story; the AI factory running itself; "I'm not sure any of it mattered"
  - 8:47 to 10:05: roadmaps made sense under scarcity; "now we can ship all our bad ideas"
  - 10:05 to 12:10: three traps (backlog, parity, churn), "path to mid"
  - 12:10 to 15:18: "roadmap zero"; prioritisation stops being strategy; the remaining constraint is truth
  - 15:18 to 17:08: the replacement cycle (conviction, evidence, factory, allocation); durable convictions, disposable features
  - 17:08 to 19:49: good stubborn vs bad stubborn; not every ship is a promise (probe, experiment, promise); product graph resolution
  - 19:49 to 22:12: a roadmap of ambition; "the ambition game"; the OKR anecdote
  - 22:12 to 23:50: the ask ("Build your last roadmap") and the "Good." triplet
- Number of main points: one argument with three traps, a four-part cycle and a three-level commitment scale. About five ideas a listener could repeat.
- Signposting and transitions: lighter than a framework talk. Clear markers at "Before I go into why I think that, I want to tell you all I have a confession" (1:50), "three traps that I want to warn you about" with first, second, final (10:05 to 11:35), "here's how I think about what we do instead of a roadmap. First..." (15:15), "So here is my ask slash gift to you all" (22:12). In between, transitions are spoken thinking ("And that really got me thinking", 8:40), and the middle (12:10 to 15:18) has no clear signpost.

## 5. Evidence and credibility

- Data, demos, case studies, screenshots, named customers: almost no external data. Evidence is her own practice: 40 bots, PRs "up 3X", 70 re-architectures, the product graph, a feature flag. The revenue-versus-PRs claim is posed as a question to the room, not backed by numbers. No demo. A generic Q1 to Q3 roadmap grid on a slide (9:00) illustrates the old artefact.
- How authority is earned: by showing her own work and her own doubts, and by insider status (party host, last year's speaker, "two decades"). Being the heaviest AI user in the room ("the biggest token maxer on the planet", 5:00) is what makes her scepticism credible: she is not an AI critic, she is an AI maximalist with doubts.
- Self-promotion: light and inverted. ChatPRD appears as the setting of a cautionary tale; How I AI appears via the party. "I will see you later this afternoon" (23:45) hints at a later appearance. No pitch.

- "You" vs "I"/"we" balance: you/your about 134, I/me/my about 221, we/us/our about 115. Heavily first-person, which suits a confession talk; "you" rises sharply in the last five minutes when she turns to the ask.
- Participation: four direct prompts. "Everybody excited to be here?" (0:00), "who's with me... completely different?" with hands (0:54), "who's shipping more than ever... Whose revenue is going up proportionate?" (5:48), "has anybody shipped some..." with hands, acknowledged as "ah, thank you, right in the middle" (18:00). She also hands the room a self-check: "Am I being good stubborn? Am I being bad stubborn?" (17:36).
- How it handles an expert, already-saturated audience: well. At an AI-saturated summit she argues against the day's dominant mood (ship faster) from inside it, and asks the one metric question (revenue vs PRs) that velocity talks avoid. She names what the experts already feel ("from the inside feel very productive"). The weakest part for experts is 3:09 to 4:36 and 8:47 to 10:00, which retell the scarcity history everyone in the room lived.

- Pace: about 4,150 words in about 23.7 minutes, roughly 175 words per minute. Fast and continuous: silence detection finds only four pauses longer than 1.2 seconds in the whole talk (1:03, 13:53, 15:35, 16:05), compared with 32 in Geoff Charles's shorter talk.
- Pauses, vocal variety, filler words: very conversational. "Like" appears 114 times (about five per minute), many as filler; "really" 24, "I think" 33. High vocal variety: role-played voices for engineering and design (4:05 to 4:24), mock enthusiasm ("ship, ship, ship"), a stage whisper feel on "And here's the secret" (8:30). The near absence of pauses means big lines ("I am out of good ideas", "durable convictions, but disposable features") get no silence after them to land.

- Recovery from mistakes or tech issues: none visible. Self-corrections are folded in as asides ("It was like almost a one shot, not quite", 6:43; "OKRs, unfortunately not dead yet", 21:37).

## 9. Ending and Q&A

- Last line, verbatim: "I will see you later this afternoon and please come say hi." The last line of content: "Write your last roadmap. I promise you is extremely fun on this other side." (23:40 to 23:46)
- Callback to the opening? Yes: the ask "Build your last roadmap" (22:20) returns to the 1:40 promise, and OKRs (opened 1:18) were closed a minute earlier.
- Call to action: "Build your last roadmap", with a clarification that she means no more feature lists with guessed impact and dates, not no planning (22:22 to 22:43). Then: raise your conviction and ambition, define success a year or two out. Earlier, a concrete metric: count "how many huge experiments are you running a month" (21:49). That metric is the most doable on Monday; the main ask is a mindset more than an action.
- Lands or fades? The content lands: the "Good." triplet (23:14 to 23:40) is the best rhetorical moment of the talk: you will write code and trash it, "Good"; your AI will have better ideas than your team, "Good"; you can't promise next year, "Good". It flips each fear into permission. She then steps on it with logistics ("Enjoy the rest of your summit..."), so the final seconds fade. Sustained room noise after the last words suggests applause (inferred); the edit cuts at 23:57. No Q&A.

## Strengths to steal (3 to 5)

- Confess from inside the trend: she is the heaviest AI user in the room admitting it hasn't produced good ideas. Scepticism from an insider is credible; scepticism from an outsider is dismissed.
- Open a personal story early, leave it unresolved, and pay it off near the end (product graph, 6:07 to 19:26). It gives the abstract middle a reason to keep listening.
- Coin compact, contrastive phrases and put them on a near-empty slide at the moment you say them ("Durable convictions / Disposable features").
- Ask the room the one uncomfortable metric question the conference avoids (revenue vs PRs) instead of asserting it.
- End by turning each fear into permission with a repeated one-word beat ("Good."), a structure anyone can reuse.

## Weaknesses to avoid (2 to 4)

- The middle (12:10 to 15:18) is a run of "I think" claims with no story, no slide and no change of mode; the idea of "roadmap zero" is explained three times instead of shown once.
- Fast, pause-free delivery with heavy "like" use: her biggest lines get no silence to land, and the filler dilutes authority on a recording.
- Retelling history the audience lived (the scarcity era, twice) spends about three minutes on what experts already know.
- Stepping on the close: the "Good." triplet and "Write your last roadmap" should be the final words, not followed by "Enjoy the rest of your summit".
