- Opening framing: strong. A teased cold open and a specific product decision instead of bios, and a question that puts a guest slightly on the spot. The "two faces" frame is not explained or reused, though.

## What fireside formats teach a solo speaker

1. Open on a specific, slightly contested artefact, not a bio. "The toggle" pulls the room into a real decision in two words. A solo speaker can do the same with a product decision, a screenshot or a line from a customer.
2. Questions are signposts. Each new question tells the audience where they are. A solo speaker can ask the audience's question aloud before answering it ("So how far ahead should you plan?") to get the same clarity.
3. A second voice is a free pattern interrupt every minute. Alone, you have to manufacture the same variety: a story, then a slide, then a question to the room, then a joke.
4. Concrete seeds produce concrete answers. The best exchange came from a specific anecdote (the model with "an attitude"), not from an abstract question. Plan your examples before your claims.
5. Turn a claim into tension with the room. The 2027 planning poll took a guest's line and set it against what the audience was doing that week. One well-placed show of hands does more than five rhetorical questions.
6. A list of good topics is not a talk. Without a stated throughline the conversation is pleasant and forgettable, and the title ended up retrofitted to one segment. A solo talk can't rely on chemistry, so it needs the argument this session lacked.
7. A round-up ending (predictions, "which of us were right") gives a tidy close and a forward hook; a solo speaker can borrow it as "here's my bet for next year, check back".

## Strengths to steal (3 to 5)

1. Cold open on a specific product decision, with a teased build-up ("I warned them backstage what we were going to start with").
2. Personal identity stories that explain a mindset change in under a minute (perfectionist upbringing plus polished Stripe, now forced to ship imperfect things).
3. A tangible artefact of humility: the personal document of wrong predictions. It is specific, admits fallibility and makes the "plan two to three months out" claim believable.
4. Audience poll that sets a speaker's claim against the room's reality (2027 annual planning), followed by a joke that releases the tension.
5. Short, concrete working advice from someone who admits being new to it (bring specific use cases, sample readings and evals to research).

## Weaknesses to avoid (2 to 4)

1. No throughline: eleven topics, and the framing ("two faces", the toggle) is dropped after three minutes. The title had to be pulled from one segment after the fact.
2. Abstract stretches with no example (platform layers and hooks, 13:26 to 15:01), unchecked by the moderator.
3. Long, opinionated, double-barrelled questions (moderator takes 31% of words) and an uneven split between guests (Nan 21%).
4. Answers that admit they are not new ("old principles in disguise", "a really boring answer") without then adding the specific twist that would make them worth the stage.
