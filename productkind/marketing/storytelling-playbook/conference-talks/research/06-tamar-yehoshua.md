**The talk in one sentence:** Atlassian's CPO argues that the PM's job hasn't changed but what a PM can do has expanded, and uses three internal projects to show that whether a PM should "row" (write code) or "steer" depends on the product and its phase.

## 1. Opening (first 60 to 90 seconds)

- First sentence, verbatim: "Wow, it's so amazing to be in a room with so many awesome product leaders."
- Opening type: thanks and warm-up, then a shared memory. At 00:30 she asks the room to recall "a year or two ago", when "everyone was pointing at everyone else's craft and saying, your job is going to go away", while a Spider-Man pointing meme labelled Designer, Engineers and PM is on screen.
- When the speaker introduces themselves: never by name or title. She positions herself through the company at 02:07 ("I'm going to talk to you about what it looks like at Atlassian").
- When the stakes and the talk's promise become clear: the topic is named at 00:23 ("what is the role of a PM"). The actual promise comes at 01:45 to 02:07: "But what does this look like in a company with over 10,000 people?", aimed at people who hear startup podcasts and think "I don't have that reality". The thesis follows at 02:23.
- Observation vs interpretation:
  - Observation: the first 90 seconds recap a debate everyone in the room already knows (jobs going away, then the "AI builder"), citing Lenny's podcast and X.
  - Interpretation: the meme is a good visual shorthand and the shared memory gets nods, but the first 100 seconds tell this audience nothing new. The 01:45 pivot ("you listen to these podcasts and you kind of scratch your head") is the real hook: it names the big-company reader's frustration precisely. It should have been the opening line.

## 2. Throughline and structure

- Structure type: framework walk-through with case studies. A metaphor (rowing vs steering a ship) plus "it depends", then three examples, a synthesis, the enablement playbook and an honest gap.
- Main sections with timestamps:
  - 00:00 to 02:30: warm-up, jobs-going-away recap, AI builder, big-company promise, thesis
  - 02:30 to 04:40: the job is unchanged, how it is done has changed (tools, context, the "teamwork graph"), CEO anecdote
  - 04:40 to 06:00: ship metaphor ("are you rowing or are you steering?"), "it depends", three examples promised
  - 06:00 to 10:10: Case 1, Confluence Remix and Slides (feature in an existing codebase): PM rows
  - 10:12 to 12:25: Case 2, RovoClaw (zero to one): PM rows, then steers
  - 12:25 to 16:14: Case 3, Jira (large legacy codebase): PM steers, no production code
  - 16:14 to 17:00: synthesis: what PMs now do and no longer do
  - 17:00 to 19:52: the playbook: AI fluency index, AI Builders Week
  - 19:52 to 20:51: measuring outcomes: "We haven't figured it out"
  - 20:51 to 21:25: close
- Number of main points: one thesis, one framework (row vs steer), three cases, one playbook.
- Signposting and transitions: very clear. She previews the three cases and their types at 05:44, opens each with a title slide and product logo, and closes each with a "what was the role of the PM?" summary slide showing a ship on a wavy line tagged Row or Steer (09:30, 12:00). Interpretation: the most navigable structure of the two talks in this batch; the audience always knows where they are.

## 5. Evidence and credibility

- Data, demos, case studies, screenshots, named customers: rich on internal numbers: 26 PRs in a month, 2x eval throughput, 14 bugs in an hour, test creation from half a day to 10 minutes, 6 and 8 weeks vs about 6 months, 3x throughput, 22 features in 10 weeks, 900 pieces of feedback, 90% triage accuracy (on the slide only, not spoken), 1,000+ people, 120 workflows. Named employees (Aya, Josh, Kevin, the CEO). No external customers; all numbers are self-reported and unaudited, and "measured outputs" is mentioned without method.
- How authority is earned: by showing work from inside a 10,000-person company, which is exactly what most of the room lacks. Credentials are never listed. The honest "we haven't figured it out" adds credibility to the numbers that came before.
- Self-promotion: heavy. Rovo, Confluence, Jira, Loom, Jira Service Management, the teamwork graph and a downloadable "AI Builders Week in a box" (19:37) are all Atlassian products or assets. She pre-empts it at 05:56 ("obviously using Atlassian products because that's what we use internally"), which helps, but the Jira case reads as a feature list.

## 6. Audience relationship

- "You" vs "I"/"we" balance: about 121 "you/your", 57 "I/me/my", 82 "we/our/us" in 3,857 words. More audience-facing than most keynotes, though many "you"s are instructions ("please go and build an agent").
- Participation: none real. Two rhetorical hands-up moments that are never taken ("I'm sure if I asked you to raise your hand", 06:52; "How many times have you seen the code and it didn't match the design?", 08:28). One invitation to contact her about measurement (20:41).
- How it handles an expert, saturated audience: mixed. The big-company angle and the row vs steer framework are genuinely useful to senior leaders. But some lines assume or prescribe in a way that can grate with experts: "I hope most of you understand that evals is a significant part of your job now" (07:41), "everybody here is prototyping, that I'm 100% sure" (13:47). The title's contrarian claim (not converging) is never argued against anyone; she agrees the roles overlap.

## Strengths to steal (3 to 5)

1. **Name the audience's frustration with a concrete gap.** "You listen to these podcasts... I don't have that reality" (01:58) tells big-company PMs this talk is for them. A sharp, specific promise.
2. **One metaphor, returned to at every section end.** Row vs steer, with a ship on every summary slide, turns three cases into one argument and lets the audience predict and check.
3. **Named people with a choice to make.** Aya (never used a terminal, 26 PRs) and Josh (stops coding when he sees the team drift) are the two moments that hold attention; both have a number or a decision.
4. **Admit the gap you haven't solved.** "I'll be honest. We haven't figured it out" (19:58) is the most credible line in the talk and invites the room in.
5. **A slide system with structure built in.** Case title, carousel cards, summary grid, strikethrough list: the deck shows where you are without a single bullet-point slide.

## Weaknesses to avoid (2 to 4)

1. **The product-tour middle.** The Jira case (12:25 to 16:14) strings tools together with no person or conflict. If a case can't have a protagonist, cut it to one minute.
2. **No room to land.** At 180 wpm with four pauses over one second, big numbers and jokes get no beat. Leave a pause after every number you want quoted.
3. **Telling experts what they should already know.** "I hope most of you understand that evals is a significant part of your job" lectures the senior people the talk is for.
4. **A generic close.** "Best time in the world to be a PM" ends on a line nobody will quote. End on the ship question or the honest gap instead.
