# Conference talk playbook

A playbook for building a 15 to 30 minute conference talk that a senior audience remembers. It covers storytelling, engagement, content, slides, delivery and the ending.

It's built from a close analysis of 11 sessions at Lenny & Friends Summit 2026, where the audience was senior PMs, product leaders and founders:

- Katie Dill (Stripe)
- Dan Shipper (Every)
- Geoff Charles (Ramp)
- Claire Vo
- Marty Cagan
- Tamar Yehoshua (Atlassian)
- Elena Verna (Lovable)
- Robby Stein (Google)
- Karri Saarinen (Linear)
- an Anthropic panel
- an OpenAI conversation

Every rule below comes from what those speakers did and what it did to attention. It is observation, not theory.

**Evidence:**

- [research/00-synthesis.md](research/00-synthesis.md): the cross-talk patterns, the comparison table and the limits of the data.
- `research/01-...md` to `research/11-...md`: one note per talk, with timestamps, story tables, energy maps and scorecards.
- [research/talk-analysis-template.md](research/talk-analysis-template.md): the template used for every talk. Use it to score your own rehearsal recording too.

**How to use it:** work through Part 1 to build the talk, Part 2 when you write the slides, and Part 3 when you rehearse. Then run the checklist in Part 4 before you go on stage.

---

## The 12 rules

The rules are in order of impact, judged by how often each one separated the strong talks from the weak ones.

1. **Your first sentence is content.** No thanks, no "I'm going to talk about". Start in a scene, an admission or a contrast. The host has already introduced you. _(10 of 11 talks opened with a greeting, thanks or an announcement. Only Dill got to content within 10 seconds.)_
2. **Lower your own status early.** Admit a failure, a regret, a wrong prediction or something you haven't figured out, within the first two minutes. _(This was the strongest hook in the set: Verna, Cagan, Vo, Stein, Tara Sesha.)_
3. **Give the audience one sentence they can hold, and say it out loud by minute 2.** The title's idea should be spoken early and then delivered. _(Cagan and Yehoshua never spoke their title phrase. Saarinen explained his in the last 90 seconds.)_
4. **Three points, each with a story.** If a point has no scene, person, number or visual, cut it or give it one. _(Every attention dip in the set sits on a point with no story.)_
5. **One spine metaphor, returned to at the end.** Choose an image from outside the industry, give it a label, carry the label into the audience's world, and bookend with it. _(Dill: "zombie buildings" became "zombie UI". Charles: racing.)_
6. **Coin two or three names with a behaviour attached.** People repeat names, not arguments. _(Examples: capability blind, Quality Wednesday, labs team of one, durable convictions.)_
7. **Tell the expert room something it can't hear elsewhere.** Use insider failures, real numbers, a disagreement with the day's consensus, or proof the room witnessed. Never lecture them on what they already believe.
8. **Stage your central story, don't summarise it.** Give it a day, a place, the words someone said, a turn and a number. _(Verna's central story, being "fired" from management, was reported in one sentence and was the biggest missed opportunity in the set.)_
9. **Plan a mode change every 2 to 3 minutes, and a big one at minutes 8 to 12.** Use a story, a question to the room, a slide reveal, a video or a show of hands. _(Every talk dipped in its middle third.)_
10. **Speak at about 150 words per minute, and pause after every number and every line you want quoted.** Cut "I think" and filler "like". _(The two slowest speakers had the two highest like-to-view ratios. The most filler-heavy sessions were at the bottom.)_
11. **Make the slides argue.** Use one line or one image per slide, let a slide deliver a punchline, and never show text you're reading out.
12. **Your last words are your best line.** Thank people before the close, never after it. Nothing comes after the final line: no plug, no logistics, no hedging. _(7 of 11 talks faded after their strongest line.)_

## Part 1: Build the talk

### Step 1. Write the talk canvas before any slides

Fill this in on one page. If a field won't fill, the talk isn't ready for slides yet.

| Field | What to write | Example from the set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The one sentence** | The whole talk in one sentence a listener could repeat to a colleague. | Verna: "When the cost of building falls below the cost of coordinating, the org chart should change." |
| **The audience's current belief** | What the room believes or fears walking in, in their words. | Yehoshua: "You listen to these podcasts... I don't have that reality." |
| **The turn** | What you want them to believe walking out, and why it's a change. | Vo: "This year I'm coming for the roadmap." |
| **Your admission** | The failure, regret or open question you'll share. It has to be real, and about you. | Cagan's regrets. Tara Sesha's document of wrong predictions. |
| **The spine** | One image or metaphor from outside the industry, and its label. | Dill: the post-war building boom and "zombie buildings". |
| **Two or three coined names** | Short names for your ideas, each with a behaviour. | "Capability blind": park the idea and re-test it with every new model. |
| **Three points** | Each point has a story, an example or a number assigned to it. | Verna: why I did it, what needs to be true, is it right for you. |
| **The Monday action per point** | Something a listener can do next week. | Saarinen: Quality Wednesday, finding and fixing one defect a week. |
| **What only you can tell them** | Insider data, a failure, a number, a screenshot, a disagreement. | Stein: Reels as a "Huge failure" in Brazil. |
| **The last line** | Written word for word now, short, ideally a contrast. | Verna: "Management needs to be a career path, not a promotion." |

### Step 2. Check the content against the expert room

Go through your points and mark each one:

- **Known:** they already believe it. Cut it, or keep it only as the setup for a twist.
- **Fresh angle:** a known idea with a twist they haven't framed this way. Dill reframed "What's the quality standard for something made with AI?" by striking out "for something made with AI". Keep these.
- **Only I can say this:** your data, your failure, your screenshot, your disagreement. Build the talk around these.

If more than one point is **Known**, the talk will sag. Also ask:

- **The day's consensus:** what will the other speakers say? Position against it out loud if you disagree. Saarinen did: "several talks about software factories... don't become one."
- **The question nobody asks:** what uncomfortable question can you put to the room? Vo asked: "Whose revenue is going up proportionate with the amount of PRs?"

### Step 3. Build the stories

Every point needs at least one staged story. A staged story has five beats:

1. **Scene:** a day, a place, a person by name. ("In a design critique last spring...")
2. **Expectation:** what you or they thought would happen.
3. **Turn:** what actually happened, ideally with the exact words said. Use dialogue. Stein's "do a barrel roll" and Dill's partner asking "what's the quality standard for something made with AI?" both did this.
4. **Number or artefact:** a figure, a screenshot or a before and after. Examples: 56 iterations, 26 PRs in a month, 12% less editing work.
5. **Point:** one sentence. Say it, or let a slide say it.

Length: 30 to 90 seconds for supporting stories. The central story can run 2 to 3 minutes if every beat is there.

**Patterns that worked in the set:**

- **Wrong first, then learn.** You held the wrong view, then something showed you otherwise. Examples: the Anthropic computer-use project (20 minutes for a Photoshop task, parked, then a leap with a newer model) and Cagan asking "how could I have missed this?"
- **The audience as the subject first.** Before your story, ask the room to think of their own version. Stein: "What was the last major purchase that you made? Quickly think about it." Then tell yours.
- **Proof the room lived through.** Use something they saw at the event that day. Dill showed version 1 against version 56 of the opening animation everyone had just watched.
- **Named person with a choice.** Aya "had never even used a terminal before" and ended up checking in 26 PRs in a month (Yehoshua). A person deciding something holds attention better than a process.
- **One long story along your framework.** Shipper's Kate Bench story moved through his own pipeline diagram stage by stage and ended on a number.

**Avoid:**

- reporting the emotional peak in one sentence;
- stories with no person, like a tool chain tour;
- second-hand claims without a source ("800 million users");
- turning a good story into a list straight after telling it.

### Step 4. Structure the talk on a timeline

Template for a **20-minute** talk. For 30 minutes, add a fourth story-led point or let the central story run longer. Don't add more points.

| Time | Beat | What happens | Done well by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 to 0:45 | **Cold open** | A scene, admission or contrast. No thanks, no agenda. | Dill (building boom), Charles (the crash) |
| 0:45 to 2:00 | **Stakes and promise** | Name the room's belief or fear. Say the one sentence. Promise three things, in order. | Verna's three-part promise at 0:58 |
| 2:00 to 3:00 | **The spine** | Introduce the metaphor and its label, and carry it into their world. | Dill: "zombie buildings" to "zombie UI" |
| 3:00 to 8:00 | **Point 1** | Staged story, the point, a coined name, the Monday action. | Shipper's "labs team of one" |
| 8:00 to 9:00 | **Big mode change** | A show of hands, a video, a reveal slide, or a question to the room. This is where talks start to sag. | OpenAI's 2027 planning poll, Dill's animation |
| 9:00 to 14:00 | **Point 2** | The central, longest story. Your admission lives here if it didn't open the talk. | Shipper's Kate Bench, the Anthropic computer-use story |
| 14:00 to 17:30 | **Point 3** | The most counterintuitive point, or the objection you answer. "You're probably asking..." | Charles's three questions section |
| 17:30 to 19:00 | **Recap** | Three lines on one slide, each tied to its coined name. | Dill's recap slide |
| 19:00 to 20:00 | **Close** | Thanks, if you want them. Then the bookend back to the spine. Then the last line, a pause, and walk off. | Dill's Gothic bookend, Verna's closing contrast |

**Signposting:**

- Number the points out loud and on a chapter slide, and keep the numbering straight. Cagan's and Verna's spoken numbering both slipped.
- Use the audience's question as a section header ("So how far ahead should you plan?"). In the panels, the questions did this job.
- Open one loop early that you close late. Vo opened her product-graph story at 6:07 and paid it off at 19:26.
- Don't answer your central question in the first five minutes. The Anthropic panel answered its headline question by 4:31 and lost its pull.

### Step 5. Write the opening and the close word for word

These are the only two parts to memorise exactly.

**Opening patterns that worked:**

- **Outside-world scene:** "Right after World War II, there was a massive building boom, the largest in history." (Dill)
- **Status drop:** "I got fired out of my job. I didn't get fired out of the company." (Verna)
- **Contrarian personal moment against the day's mood:** "I took a break this summer from paying attention to AI", then the reveal that nothing much had changed. (Saarinen)
- **Callback to your own past claim:** "Last year I came here and I said product management is dead." (Vo)
- **Joke misdirect into a story:** "[I] once signed up to race cars at Le Mans. I'm just kidding. Lemons." (Charles)
- **Cold open on one specific decision:** "The toggle." (Claire Vo moderating OpenAI)

**Closing patterns that worked:**

- **Bookend:** return to the opening image, now meaning something new (Dill: modernism to Gothic, "zombie" one last time).
- **Contrast line, then pause:** "Management needs to be a career path, not a promotion." (Verna)
- **Fear into permission, repeated:** "...and then you're going to trash it. Good." "Your AI is going to have better ideas than your team. Good." Three times. (Vo)
- **A forward hook:** a quick round of predictions, with a promise to see who was right (the OpenAI conversation). Solo version: "Here's my bet for next year."

**Closing anti-patterns:**

- **Hedging:** "It's just one way", "you probably heard 10 other ones today".
- **"So that's what I mean":** the title explained too late.
- **A new topic in the last minute.**
- **Plugs, self-introductions or logistics after the last line.**
- **Generic optimism:** "best time to be a PM".
- **"I hope this was useful":** said once or twice.

## Part 2: Slides

**Format:**

- **One idea per slide.** Either one line of large type (6 to 12 words) or one full-bleed image with a small caption. The best decks in the set ran at about 2 slides a minute with almost no words.
- **Headlines as claims, not topics.** A line like Saarinen's "customers are not buying the lines of code" beats a topic label like "Product quality". Saarinen's full-sentence headlines argued more clearly than his speech.
- **A consistent system:** chapter cards with the number and keyword, story slides, a recap slide. Yehoshua's case title, card and summary-grid system showed where you were without a single bullet point.
- **One keyword per point** if you are the content, not the slides. Cagan used one word per regret ("humility", "thinking") to keep the room's count when his spoken numbering slipped.

**Making slides do work:**

- **A slide that delivers the punchline,** at least once. A strikethrough works well: Dill struck out "for something made with AI", and Yehoshua struck out the things PMs stop doing, leaving "Fewer synchronous meetings".
- **A contrast you can see.** Verna showed nine grey steps (idea, meeting, PM, designer, meeting, engineer, review, approval, ship) above three bright ones (idea, build, learn). The length difference is the argument.
- **Pre-announce the provocative slide:** "Some people don't like the next slide." It turns a click into a reveal.
- **A recurring diagram with a moving marker** works as a map for a long story (Shipper's pipeline).
- **A dark holding slide** sends the room's eyes back to you for a story (Dill's purple parallelogram).
- **Show the real thing:** screenshots of your actual tool, the real v1, a short video. Charles paired every claim with a number and a screenshot. Cut code and dashboards that can't be read from the back row.

**Avoid:**

- text you read out;
- unreadable small type (Yehoshua's monospace cards);
- no visuals at all (the panel scored 1 on slides);
- unsourced numbers on screen.

## Part 3: Delivery and rehearsal

### Targets to measure in rehearsal

Record every full run-through and transcribe it with the same whisper setup used for this research. Then check these numbers:

| Measure | Target | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pace | 140 to 160 words per minute | Dill and Cagan, at about 155 wpm, had the highest like-to-view ratios. Talks at 200+ wpm blurred. |
| Pauses over 1.2 seconds | 25 or more in 20 minutes | Charles had 32 and Cagan 23. Yehoshua had 4, and her numbers and jokes never landed. |
| "I think" | Close to zero | Saarinen's 43 flattened his strongest claims. Dill used it once and Cagan never did. |
| Filler "like" | Under 5 per 1,000 words | The three most filler-heavy sessions are near the bottom of the ratings. |
| Time to first content | Under 10 seconds | Dill got to content in 7 seconds. Most of the others took 20 to 60. |
| Time to your one sentence | Under 2 minutes | Stein took 3:17 and Cagan's list started at 5:37. |

### How to rehearse

1. **Rehearse the opening and close until they're automatic.** Do this first.
2. **Mark the pauses in the script:** after every number, every coined name, every punchline and before the last line.
3. **Run the full talk 5 or more times** and score at least one recording with the [analysis template](research/talk-analysis-template.md). Draw your own energy map. Wherever you'd rate a stretch 2 out of 5, add a story, a visual or a question.
4. **Rehearse in front of one person from the target audience** and ask them the next day what they remember. If they can't repeat the one sentence and at least one coined name, fix the talk, not the delivery.
5. **Stand still for stories,** stay in one spot with open hands, and move only between sections. Dill's calm, mostly still presence read as confidence.
6. **Rehearse a tech failure.** Know what you'll say if the video doesn't play.

### Engaging the room

Use 2 or 3 moments, not more, and always use the answer:

- **"Think of your own..."** before your story (Stein).
- **"Can you guess...?"** with a reveal (Dill's "Can you guess what this brand sells?", answered by "Korean barbecue").
- **A show of hands that tests a claim against the room** (OpenAI's "Who is doing 2027 planning?"). React to the result live and turn it into evidence for your next point. Follow it with a joke if the result makes people uncomfortable.
- **Voice the sceptic yourself:** "You might be thinking: why not just let the model do it?" In the panel, the moderator's sceptical follow-up produced the sharpest line of the session. Alone, you have to raise the objection yourself.

## Part 4: Pre-flight checklist

**Content**

- [ ] The one sentence is spoken out loud before minute 2, and the title's phrase appears in the talk.
- [ ] Three points, each with a staged story, a number or artefact, a coined name and a Monday action.
- [ ] At least one honest admission about yourself in the first two minutes.
- [ ] At least one thing only you can tell this room: data, a failure, a screenshot, a disagreement.
- [ ] Nothing the room already believes, unless it sets up a twist.
- [ ] Every number has a source you can name if asked.
- [ ] One loop opened early and closed late. The central question isn't answered before the last third.

**Structure and attention**

- [ ] The first sentence is content. Thanks and introductions are handled by the host or moved later.
- [ ] A planned mode change every 2 to 3 minutes, and a big one around minutes 8 to 12.
- [ ] Spoken numbering matches the slides throughout.
- [ ] 2 or 3 audience moments, each with a planned way to use the answer.

**Slides**

- [ ] One idea per slide. No slide repeats what you say word for word.
- [ ] At least one slide delivers a punchline, and at least one visual contrast carries an argument.
- [ ] Everything is readable from the back row.
- [ ] A recap slide that uses your coined names.

**Ending**

- [ ] The bookend returns to the opening image.
- [ ] Thanks come before the final line.
- [ ] The final line is written, short and followed by a pause and silence. No plug, logistics or hedging after it.

**Delivery**

- [ ] The rehearsal recording measures 140 to 160 wpm, 25 or more long pauses, "I think" close to zero.
- [ ] Someone from the audience can repeat the one sentence and a coined name the next day.

## If you're on a panel or doing a fireside chat

The two conversation formats had the lowest ratings in the set, so they need extra help.

- **Agree a throughline with the moderator beforehand,** and the one sentence each of you wants the room to leave with. The OpenAI session had 11 topics and no throughline, so its YouTube title came from one 2.5-minute segment.
- **Bring concrete seeds:** a story, a number, a screenshot. The best exchange in the OpenAI session came from a specific anecdote, a model that "had an attitude", not from an abstract question.
- **Disagree on stage when you really do.** The Anthropic panel had a real disagreement and never brought it out: "five overlapping products" against "half the job is saying no".
- **Ask for one visual** even in a no-slides format.
- **Own the last answer.** Make it your one sentence, not a general hope.
