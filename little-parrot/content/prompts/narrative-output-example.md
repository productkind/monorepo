# Build a Valuable Product: Break Down Your Idea for AI "Vibe Coding"

Welcome to this micro-course! In the next 8 challenges, you'll learn how to take a product idea and break it into manageable pieces – from defining the problem all the way to writing an AI prompt to build an MVP (Minimum Viable Product). By the end, you’ll have a clear plan (and a prompt template) to start building your product with AI tools like Lovable.

## Challenge 1: Define the Problem You’re Solving

**Why start with a problem statement?** Before jumping into solutions, it’s crucial to understand the **user’s problem**. A clear problem statement serves as a guiding light for your project. It helps you and your team stay focused on what users really need, rather than getting lost in features or technology. In fact, a well-written problem statement can rally everyone around a common mission and build empathy for the end user <sup>1</sup> . It also gives you a way to measure success – you succeed when the problem is solved, not just when a product is launched <sup>2</sup> . By articulating the problem (including its root cause and emotional impact on the user), you open the door to more creative and effective solutions <sup>3</sup>.

**How to write a problem statement**: We’ll use a simple formula from design thinking <sup>4</sup>. Fill in the blanks in this sentence to frame the problem from the user’s perspective:

_I am a [user persona] trying to [achieve a desired outcome], but [describe the barrier or problem] because [explain the root cause of that barrier], which makes me feel [emotion]._<sup>5 6</sup>

This format forces you to clarify **who** has the problem, **what** they’re trying to do, **what’s blocking them, why** that blockage exists, and **how it makes them feel**. Including the emotional aspect builds empathy – it reminds us there’s a real person experiencing frustration, confusion, or other feelings due to this problem.

**Example – Problem Statement:**

_I am a book club organizer trying to keep our monthly book club running smoothly and everyone engaged, but our coordination is a complete mess – messages and book suggestions are scattered across WhatsApp and email, because there’s no central place to organize everything (and group chats bury the important stuff in off-topic messages), which makes me feel frustrated and exhausted, like I spend more time organizing than enjoying the books._

In the example above, notice how **specific** it is. We know exactly who the user is (a book club organizer), what they want (an easy way to keep the club running and engaging), what the obstacles are (chaotic, scattered communication), why those obstacles exist (no single tool or hub for coordination), and how it makes the user feel (burned out and not having fun). A specific problem statement like this will guide us to a more targeted solution. (If we simply said “organizing book clubs is hard,” we might build something too generic or miss the real pain points.)

Now it’s your turn. Take a moment to craft a problem statement for **your** product idea using the template. Don’t worry about getting it perfect on the first try – you can refine it as you learn more.

**INPUT:** Write your problem statement in the format above (persona, goal, barrier, cause, emotion). Persona Example: `I am a busy parent...` (etc.)

## Challenge 2: Identify Your Target User (Persona)

Who are you building this product for? To design a great solution, you need a clear picture of the **target user**. In product design, we often create a **persona** – a fictional yet realistic character representing our typical user <sup>7</sup>. This persona is an **archetype** based on real user research or assumptions; it captures the user's key characteristics, needs, frustrations, and goals.

A persona helps you **keep the user in mind** throughout development. Whenever you’re making a decision, you can ask: _“Would this help [Persona’s Name] achieve their goals? What would [Persona] think of this feature?”_ It’s a way to build empathy and ensure you’re solving the right problems for the right people <sup>7</sup>.

**How to create a simple persona**: You don’t need a 10-page biography. Focus on a few main points: - **About**: Basic demographics or background relevant to your product. What is their situation or context? - **Frustrations**: What pain points or problems do they encounter (especially related to the problem you identified)? - **Goals**: What do they ultimately want to achieve? What does success or happiness look like for them?

Fill in each of those for your persona. Give them a name and a descriptive title (e.g. “Sarah, the overwhelmed book club organizer”), so they feel like a real person.

**Example – Persona:**

- **Persona: Sarah**, the overwhelmed book club organizer
- **About:** Sarah started a book club with her friends. She loves reading but has very limited free time. She’s not super tech-savvy; however, she’s comfortable with basic apps and willing to try simple digital tools to help manage the club.
- **Frustrations:** Sarah says things like _“I can't find the book suggestions in the chat history.”_ The club’s coordination is chaos: _“We spend 30 minutes each meeting just figuring out what to read next.”_ Often, _“half the group doesn’t vote because they miss the message,”_ and she feels _“like a nag, constantly reminding people about deadlines.”_ These quotes show her pain and annoyance with the current process.
- Goals: Sarah’s goals are clear. She wants to _spend no more than 10 minutes per month on book club admin tasks_ (not hours digging through messages). She hopes to _get everyone to actually participate in choosing books_ so it’s a group effort. She **never** wants to lose a good book suggestion again – every recommendation should be captured. Most of all, she wants to **keep the club fun** and not let organizing it turn into a chore.

This persona gives us a vivid understanding of our user. We can almost imagine Sarah using our product – which features would make her life easier, and what would annoy her.

Now, define the persona for your product idea:

**INPUT:** Create your persona.<br> Persona: _(e.g. “Sarah, the overwhelmed book club organizer”)_

**INPUT:** About: _(Who are they? Brief background and relevant details. e.g. “Started a book club with friends; loves reading but has limited free time; not very technical.”)_

**INPUT:** Frustrations: _(What problems or annoyances do they face? e.g. “Can't find book suggestions in scattered chats”; “spends 30 minutes every meeting choosing a book”; etc.)_

**INPUT:** Goals: _(What do they want to achieve? e.g. “Keep admin work under 10 minutes a month”; “get everyone to participate”; “never lose a suggestion”; “make book club fun again.”)_

Take your time to fill these out. Your persona will guide a lot of decisions moving forward. Remember to keep it **user-centered** – focus on their needs and feelings, not what technology they use or what solution you have in mind (those come later!).

## Challenge 3: Storyboard – From Struggle to Solution

Now that you know **who** you’re designing for and **what** problem you need to solve, let’s visualize how your solution will help your user. A great way to do this is by creating a **storyboard**. This is a series of panels or sketches that illustrate the user’s journey: from experiencing the problem, to using your solution, to reaching a happy outcome.

Think of it like a comic strip of your user’s story. Why storyboard? Because “seeing” the user’s experience step by step helps you identify _what exactly your solution needs to do._ It highlights critical moments that your product must address. Plus, it’s a fun, creative exercise to ensure you truly empathize with your user’s experience.

**How to storyboard:** Follow these steps【Draft】: 1. **Start with the struggle.** Draw or describe a scene showing your user **frustrated** with the current situation (before your solution exists). This should depict the problem you wrote earlier.<br> 2. **Show the ending, solved.** Next, imagine the future **with** your solution: how is your user feeling and what are they doing once the problem is resolved? Draw the **happy ending** – the user succeeding, thanks to your product.<br> 3. **Map the journey.** Fill in 2-4 panels between the start and end, showing **how** the user gets from the struggle to the solved state using your solution. Each panel is a key moment or interaction with your product that helps overcome a part of the problem.

Even if you’re not an artist, simple sketches or stick figures are fine – the point is to capture the idea. If drawing isn’t possible, you can also just write out what happens in each “panel” in words.

**Example – Storyboard for Sarah’s Book Club Solution:**

- **Panel 1 – The Struggle (😫):** _Scene:_ Sarah is at her kitchen table late at night, looking **stressed**. She has her phone in hand with multiple chat apps open (WhatsApp, email), papers or sticky notes with book titles scattered around. There’s a coffee cup and maybe an aspirin bottle next to her – hinting at a headache. **Thought bubble:** “Did Jake suggest that mystery novel or was it Jamie? And which chat was it in?” She’s overwhelmed trying to track suggestions. _Caption:_ “Another month, another chaotic book selection process.” (This panel illustrates the **pain**: disorganized communication and a frustrated Sarah.)
- **Panel 2 – The Happy Ending (😊):** _Scene:_ Now Sarah is relaxed on her couch with a book in hand and a smile on her face. Her laptop is open on the coffee table showing a **clean list** of book suggestions on a simple app interface (our solution). Her phone is put aside (no frantic messaging needed). _Thought bubble:_ “Everyone voted, we picked a great book, and it only took 2 minutes!” She’s clearly happy and stress-free. _Caption:_ “Book club is fun again – it practically runs itself!” (This panel shows the outcome when the problem is solved: Sarah enjoys reading, coordination is easy.)
- **Panels 3-6 – The Journey (🚀 How We Get There):** These panels show _how Sarah uses the product_ to go from 😫 to 😊:
- **Panel 3 – Discovery & Setup (💡):** Sarah finds a new app and creates **“Sarah’s Book Club”** with one click. The screen says “Name your club” and she types it in. She looks cautiously optimistic. Thought: “Could it really be this simple?” (Key moment: easy setup, hope begins.)
- **Panel 4 – The First Magic (✨):** Sarah enters a few book suggestions into the app (it takes seconds). She shares a link with her friends. Her friend Emma immediately adds a suggestion too, which instantly appears. No logins or complicated steps – just a shared link. Sarah is pleasantly surprised. Sarah thinks: “Everyone can see everything in one place!” (Key moment: centralizing suggestions effortlessly.)
- **Panel 5 – Group Participation (👥):** Sarah checks the app later and sees 5 friends have joined and added 12 suggestions total! Each suggestion shows who added it. Sarah is amazed: _“They're actually participating! More suggestions in one day than in the last three months combined.”_ (Key moment: the product successfully engages the whole group, solving the “people not participating” problem.)
- **Panel 6 – Decision & Peace (✅):** It’s Monday morning; the app shows one book marked as “Currently Reading” (clearly the chosen book). There’s a simple indicator highlighting the top- voted book. Sarah clicks “Finalize Selection.” She closes her laptop with satisfaction. She gets a text: “Love the pick! So easy!” from a club member. Sarah feels at peace. _Thought:_ “From chaos to decision in 48 hours, not 3 weeks.” (Key moment: the solution dramatically speeds up and smooths out the decision process.)

Notice how each panel in the storyboard corresponds to a **feature or benefit** of the product: easy setup, adding suggestions in one place, everyone contributing, quick voting/decision. By visualizing the story, we’ve implicitly listed what our solution needs to do to make the happy ending happen.

**Reflect on your storyboard:** What are the key moments for your user? Even if you can’t draw right now, imagine the **“before”** and **“after”** scenes for your user, and 2-4 steps in between where your product helps them. This narrative will be the backbone of your solution.

_(If you have pen and paper, consider sketching your own 4-6 panel storyboard now! Stick figures and simple labels are okay. If not, simply write down the sequence of moments from problem to solution, like we did for Sarah.)_

**Explain:** A storyboard ensures everyone can **see the user’s journey**. It’s a powerful tool to communicate your vision. You can share it with teammates or stakeholders to get them bought into the problem and the solution approach. More importantly, it helps you identify what features or interactions are truly needed. If a step is in your storyboard (e.g., “friends add suggestions easily”), then your product must have that capability. In the next steps, we’ll take these moments and turn them into a more formal plan for development.

### Challenge 4: Introducing Story Mapping

With a clear story in mind, it’s time to break down your solution into pieces that you can build. We’ll do this with story mapping (also known as user-story mapping). Story mapping is a technique to outline the user’s journey through your product in a structured way 8 . Think of it as taking the storyboard moments and arranging them as a to-do list for your app. In story mapping, you list all the actions a user needs to take to go from start to finish in your product. These actions are often organized into levels: - Activities or Stages: The high-level phases of use (e.g. “Set up account”, “Manage book suggestions”, “Choose a book” in Sarah’s case). - Steps or Tasks: Under each activity, the specific things the user does (e.g. “Invite members” or “Add a book suggestion” or “Vote on a suggestion”). - (Sometimes) Details/Sub-tasks: Smaller interactions needed for each step (e.g. “Enter book title into form”, “Click vote button”). You don’t need to worry about formal hierarchy too much for a simple project. The goal is to get all the user actions out of your head and onto paper (or a screen). This will ensure you don’t forget 9 anything important the app must do, and it lets you see the big picture of your product. Why do this? Story mapping helps with prioritization and planning. It visually lays out how the user will navigate your app to achieve their goal 10 . It also makes it easier to spot which features are core and which are nice-to-have (for later). Jeff Patton, who popularized story mapping, advocated it as a replacement for long, dull requirement documents – instead of reading a spec, your team can literally look at a map of the user’s interactions 9 . It sparks conversations and ensures everyone understands how the pieces fit together. Example – Story Map (excerpt for Sarah’s app): Imagine a simple map for the book club app. It might start like this: - Activity 1: Set Up Club

- Step 1: Create a new club (enter name, get share link)
- Step 2: Invite friends (send them the link)
- Activity 2: Collect Suggestions
- Step 1: Add book suggestion (by organizer or members)
- Step 2: View all suggestions in list
- Activity 3: Decide on Book
- Step 1: Vote on suggestions (each member votes)
- Step 2: Display the top-voted book
- Step 3: Mark the chosen book as “current” (This is a simplified example. Your real story map might have more steps or different structure based on your product idea.) The story map covers the whole end-to-end experience. By laying it out, we can clearly see what we need to build. In the next challenge, you will create a story map for your own product idea. 5 Challenge 5: Map Out Your Solution (User Story Map Exercise) Now it’s your turn to outline what the user does in your app step by step. Use the understanding from your storyboard and persona to enumerate the key actions. Don’t worry about how the app will implement them (that comes later); focus on what the user needs to do and in what order. Tips for story mapping your idea: - Start at the very beginning: How does a user first find or start using your product? Do they sign up or just open an app? This is the first activity. - Then move through the user’s journey: What’s the next major thing they do? And after that? Continue until the user’s main goal (identified in your problem statement) is achieved. - Write each action as something the user does (e.g. “user submits a book suggestion” or “user sets a reminder”). Avoid phrasing them as internal system tasks. - Keep it high-level enough that you have maybe 5-15 steps in total. (If you have a lot more, see if some can be grouped into a higher-level activity.) It often helps to list steps in a numbered sequence (which implies the order of use). You can refine grouping into activities later if needed. INPUT: Outline the key steps a user will take to use your product and solve their problem. List them in the order they would happen. You can number them or use bullets. (For example: “1. User creates a new account... 2. User enters information about X... 3. User shares link with friend...” and so on.) (Feel free to refer back to your storyboard or persona if you need to recall what needs to happen.) After writing your steps, double-check: Does this cover the entire journey from the user’s perspective? From the moment they encounter the problem to the point where it’s solved, every action on your map should move the user forward. If something is unrelated to solving the main problem, it might not need to be in this flow (at least not for the first version of your product). Great! You now have a user story map – essentially a rough blueprint of features and interactions for your app. The next step is deciding what to build first. Challenge 6: Carving Out an MVP Time to prioritize. You likely have more steps in your story map than you can (or should) build immediately. Successful products often start small, with just the core functionality that delivers value – this is your MVP (Minimum Viable Product). What’s an MVP? It stands for Minimum Viable Product, meaning the simplest version of the product that is usable and valuable to users 5 . It’s not a crappy, half-baked product or just a prototype you slap together – it should be a complete loop that solves the primary problem, but with no extra bells and whistles. Think of it as the “smallest possible piece” of your idea that still delivers the main value 11 . By keeping it minimal, you can build and launch faster, then learn from real users and iterate. As one expert put it, “Think big, but act small: Build the smallest possible piece of functionality and ship it. Don’t think MVP means poor quality – the ‘minimal’ means minimal features, not minimal effort or quality.” 11 Why build only an MVP first? There are many benefits: - Speed and focus: With fewer features, you can develop and launch quickly. This means you get your solution into users’ hands sooner. - User feedback: An MVP allows you to test your assumptions. You can find out early if you’re solving the right 6 problem. If not, you can pivot or adjust with less wasted work. - Resource savings: You avoid spending time and money on features that users don’t actually need or want. Build the core that matters most, and save the “nice-to-haves” for later. - Clarity: For you and your team, an MVP forces clarity on what’s truly essential. It’s easy to get caught up thinking everything is important. But when you constrain scope, you make tough choices about what will have the biggest impact on the user’s problem. Quiz: Which statement about an MVP is TRUE? A. An MVP is a prototype with every feature, just not polished. B. An MVP is the smallest version of the product that still delivers core value to the user. C. An MVP should be of low quality since it’s only a test version. D. An MVP is the same as the final product’s first full release (just version 1.0). (Think about it... The correct answer is the one that aligns with solving the user’s main problem with minimal features, not compromising on solving the problem or on quality.) Now, look at your story map from Challenge 5. Which steps or features are absolutely necessary to solve the user’s primary problem? Those are your MVP. The rest can likely wait for later versions. Often, the MVP is a subset of your story map – maybe the first one or two activities, or even just a single core workflow – while other nice features are left out initially. Example – MVP for Sarah’s App: From the story map we listed, what’s the minimal product that still makes book club organizing easier? Probably: - The ability to add and view book suggestions in one central list. - A way for members to vote or indicate preference on suggestions. - Showing which book is selected (maybe automatically the top vote-getter, or manually marked by organizer). These three things alone would directly address Sarah’s biggest pain: scattered suggestions and chaotic voting. We can exclude features like user profiles, notifications, discussion forums, etc., in the first version. Those aren’t strictly needed to solve the immediate problem (though they might be nice later). The MVP is laser-focused on suggestions + voting + result. Your turn – define your MVP: INPUT: From your story map, list the features or steps that will be in your MVP. In other words, what are the must-haves to solve the core problem? (You can list them as bullet points or a short paragraph. e.g. “MVP will include: a central suggestion board, a voting mechanism, and a result display. Other features like comments or reminders will be added later.”) By articulating your MVP, you’ve made a critical decision: you know what to build first. This will keep you focused when you start actually building with AI. It’s tempting to add more, but remember the principle: maximize value by building the smallest possible piece that works. You can always expand later after testing your MVP. Challenge 7: Write Clear Prompts to Build with AI (Vibe Coding) You have a plan – problem, persona, story, map, MVP – now it’s time to turn this plan into reality. Uniquely, we’re going to use an AI development tool (Lovable) to build our app by writing prompts instead of manual coding. This approach is sometimes nicknamed “vibe coding” – you describe the vibe or behavior of the app you want, and the AI assembles the code for you. 7 But to get the best results, you must communicate clearly with the AI. Think of it as giving instructions to a really smart (but literal) junior developer. If your instructions (prompts) are vague or too broad, you might get something unexpected. We’ll cover how to structure your prompts and a strategy to build iteratively. How to structure prompts for an AI builder: 1. Start with a high-level overview: Your first prompt should paint the big picture of your app. Include the type of app, the main user or persona, and the primary goal or use case. Essentially, summarize your problem statement in instructive form. (E.g. “Build a simple web app for a book club organizer to collect book suggestions and votes from members.”) 2. List core features (MVP): Still in the initial prompt, mention the key features you outlined as MVP. Be specific but concise. (E.g. “It should have a page where members can add new book suggestions, a voting feature for those suggestions, and a way to display the top-voted book of the month.”) 3. Provide any essential details or constraints: If you have preferences about the design or tech, you can add (though if not, the AI will choose defaults). For example, “Make the interface very simple and user-friendly, with clear buttons for adding suggestions and voting.” Or “Use a friendly tone, maybe a book theme in the UI.” You could also specify platforms (“as a web app viewable on mobile and desktop”). 4. Use clear, structured language: Write prompts in numbered steps or bullet points if needed, to break down tasks. The AI often handles step-by-step instructions well. Avoid ambiguity – e.g., instead of “make it like Facebook but for books,” say “create a homepage listing current book, with a form to submit new books.” 5. Iterate incrementally: Don’t expect to get everything perfect in one giant prompt. It’s usually better to build in pieces. For example, you might first prompt for the basic UI and functionality. Then once the base app is generated, you might give a second prompt like “Add a login page for members” or “Connect to a database to save suggestions” or “Improve the styling with a cozy book-club theme.” Each prompt refines or extends the app. This step-by-step approach (adding one feature at a time) aligns with how AI “vibe coding” works best 12 – it allows you to check results and adjust as you go. 6. Be prepared to refine: If the AI output isn’t exactly what you want, you can prompt again with adjustments. E.g. “The voting feature is confusing; please change it so each user can only vote once and show a vote count.” The key is to be specific about what to change. Best Practices: According to experts, successful AI builders often use clear and structured prompts and start with small incremental tasks 12 . So don’t feel you have to describe your entire app in one paragraph. You can break it down. Prompt Template: Here’s a template you can use as a starting point for your first prompt to Lovable (or a similar AI dev tool): “Build a [platform/type of application] for [user/persona]. The app should [overall purpose or goal]. It needs to include the following features: [feature 1], [feature 2], [feature 3] (…etc, list your MVP features). Make the design [any style preferences: simple, modern, themed…] and ensure it’s easy for the user to [key action].” For example, using Sarah’s case, a prompt might be: “Build a web app for a book club organizer to streamline book selection. It should allow multiple users (club members) to submit book suggestions, display all suggestions in a list, and let users vote on their favorite suggestion. After voting, the app should show which book is selected as the book of the month. Make the interface clean and user-friendly, with a book club theme (maybe a small book icon and warm colors). Ensure it’s easy to use for non-technical people.” This prompt gives context (who it’s for and why) and specifics (what it should do). From here, the AI would generate an initial version of the app. Then we could refine with additional prompts (e.g., “Add an 8 email invite feature” or “Make the voting results only visible to the organizer” or whatever we need next, based on testing). This iterative cycle is how you co-create with AI effectively. Challenge 8: Test with Users Right Away You’ve defined the problem, designed a solution, and even built a first version (in theory). What’s next? Testing with real users! The whole point of an MVP is to get it in front of people and learn. This final step is about validating your idea and gathering feedback as early as possible. Why test now? Because no matter how well you’ve thought through the problem, real users will surprise you. They might use the product in unexpected ways, or they might still have unmet needs. Early user testing helps you catch these things before you invest too much in building more. As the Lean Startup mantra goes: you want to “build – measure – learn” in quick cycles. In practice, “testing” could be as simple as showing your prototype/MVP to a few target users (maybe colleagues, friends, or friendly potential customers who fit your persona) and asking them to use it. Observe where they get confused or frustrated, and ask for their impressions: Did it solve your problem? What did you like or not like? Sometimes even one customer using it will give you a wealth of insight – “we can do all the research and prototyping in the world, but nothing beats that first customer 13 trying the product” . For Sarah’s book club app, testing might involve asking a couple of book club organizers to try coordinating their next pick with the app. Their feedback (e.g., “It was great except I wish I could notify people in-app rather than separately”) would directly inform what to build next (maybe notifications become a priority). Plan your user test: Think about who could give you honest feedback on your product idea. Ideally, someone who matches your persona or at least has the problem you’re solving. It could be a friend, a co-worker, or a small group of potential users. You don’t need a fully polished product to start testing – even a clickable mockup or the early version from Lovable is fine. And remember, testing isn’t a one-time thing. It’s a mindset of continually learning and improving. After your first test, you might go back to your prompt, refine the app, then test again with more users or more features. 9 INPUT: Who will you test your MVP with, and how? Write down a quick plan (even if it’s just “I’ll share the app link with two colleagues who also run book clubs and ask for their feedback on ease of use and if it solves their coordination issues”). (Example: “I will invite my friend (who is my target user persona) to use the app for her next book club meeting. I’ll watch how she adds suggestions and votes, and interview her afterwards about what was easy or hard. If something frustrates her or if she still falls back to WhatsApp, that’s a sign I need to improve that part.”) By planning this out, you commit to actually getting real-world input. This is where your product becomes truly valuable – when it’s in the hands of users and making their lives better. Testing early and often is the key to building something people love (and it saves you from building the wrong thing). Conclusion & Next Steps Congratulations on working through the process! By breaking the process into these steps, you’ve made a daunting task (building a product) much more manageable. This is the same approach experienced product designers and founders use – the difference is you have the power of AI to accelerate the building part.
